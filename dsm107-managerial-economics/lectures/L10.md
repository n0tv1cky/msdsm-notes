# DSM-107 Managerial Economics — Session L10 Study Notes

## 1. Overview

This session continues the firm's **profit-maximization problem as a constrained optimization**, where the **demand curve is the constraint**. It opens by revisiting the demand curve (downward sloping; law of demand; real-world Cheerios estimate), then explains **why profitable firms tend to be large**: economies of scale / increasing returns to scale (from specialization and engineering reasons), spreading of fixed costs (R&D, advertising), bargaining power over input prices, and demand-side **network effects**. It then covers the counterweight — **diseconomies of scale** from bureaucracy (worked out with a 10 → 100 → 1,000 employee supervision example). The session formalises the three technology classes (IRS, CRS, DRS) using the λ (lambda) scaling definition, then moves to **cost functions**: total cost curve (positive intercept = fixed cost), **average cost** (slope of ray from origin), **marginal cost** (slope of the total cost curve), and the MC–AC relationship (MC < AC ⇒ AC falls; MC > AC ⇒ AC rises; they intersect at minimum AC = **minimum efficient scale**), illustrated with a class-height analogy. The final part derives, with simple calculus, **marginal revenue**, the first-order condition **MR = MC**, the **slope of the isoprofit curve = −(P − MC)/Q**, the **price elasticity of demand** ε and its classification, and the key tangency result **(P − MC)/P = −1/ε** — i.e. the profit margin is inversely related to consumers' price sensitivity. Ends with exam/quiz logistics.

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order Taught

### 2.1 Recap: Profit maximization as constrained optimization
- The firm **cannot sell however much it wants**; the **demand curve is the constraint**.
- Given that constraint, the firm chooses **price and quantity**.
- Demand curve, in plain terms = **the amount of money customers are willing to pay for the product**.
- **Law of demand**: downward-sloping curve — *the lower the price, the more customers wish to buy*.
- Example used: a **real-world estimated demand curve for Cheerios (breakfast cereal)**.
- The demand curve "represents the trade-off between price and quantity that firms are constrained to meet."

### 2.2 Why profitable firms are often larger
Reasons grouped as follows:

**(a) Lower cost per unit of output** (cost side).

**(b) Technological advantages of large-scale production**
- *Capital* in economics = **machines**. Machines may be **tailored to large-scale production**.
- **Worked illustration (cereal machine):** if the basic cereal-packing machine can produce **5 lakh (500,000) packets per month**, the firm benefits only if it makes and sells **at least 5 lakh** units; otherwise the machine is being used **below optimal**.
- Term for this: **economies of scale** = **increasing returns to scale**.
- Definition given: *"doubling every input, if it triples the firm's output"* ⇒ increasing returns to scale.

**(c) The three scale classes (introduced here, formalised later)**
- **Increasing returns to scale (IRS)** — double inputs ⇒ output **more than** doubles (e.g. triples). An x% rise in every input ⇒ **more than x%** rise in output.
- **Decreasing returns to scale (DRS)** — x% rise in inputs ⇒ **less than x%** rise in output.
- **Constant returns to scale (CRS)** — x% rise in inputs ⇒ **exactly x%** rise in output.

### 2.3 Sources of increasing returns to scale

**Source 1 — Specialization within the firm (a "managerial" reason)**
- As the firm gets larger, teams become **more and more specialized**, raising organizational productivity.
- **Minimizes training time**, because employees focus on specific tasks and **learn by doing**.
- **Startup contrast:** in a startup the *same person* does multiple tasks (e.g. **advertising AND logistics planning**) ⇒ lower productivity in both, plus time lost getting on board with each new task.
- **Large-firm contrast:** distinct **marketing team, logistics team, production team, quality-control team** — no multitasking, each specialized.

**Source 2 — Engineering reasons**
- **Pipe example** (brewery liquid supply, petrol, gas through pipes): if you **double the diameter of a pipe**, the **volume carried more than doubles**.
- The **circumference (material used) roughly doubles**, but the **volume more than doubles** ⇒ more output flows per unit of material input.

### 2.4 Other advantages of large firms (cost side)

**Fixed vs. variable cost**
- **Fixed cost** = a cost that **does not change with the amount of output produced**.
- **Variable cost** = varies with output (named, discussed later via cost curves).

**Spreading fixed costs**
- **R&D example:** money spent on R&D (paying scientists) to obtain an **intellectual property right** is *not linked to production* — production happens after the invention. The more units produced using that innovation, the **lower the unit cost of R&D**.
- **Advertising example:** ad cost depends on the **popularity of the slot** on the marketing platform (e.g. television), **not** on output size. Hence: **larger production ⇒ lower advertising cost per unit**.
  - Implication: **smaller firms can't afford ads on big marketing platforms** because the fixed advertising cost cannot be spread over many units.

**Bargaining power**
- Firms don't just sell output, they also **buy inputs** (raw materials, intermediate products) that are not made in-house.
- Large firms have **greater bargaining power** ⇒ negotiate **better input prices** ⇒ lower costs.

*(Both of the above are **supply-side** advantages.)*

### 2.5 Demand-side advantages: network effects / network economies of scale
- Idea: **more people buying your product makes the product itself more attractive** — "more begets more."
- **E-commerce platform example:** you go to the platform popular for books if buying books; popular for gadgets if buying gadgets. Many **sellers** come because many **buyers** come, and vice versa — **self-perpetuating**; higher traffic invites even more traffic.
- **Social networks (Facebook):** appeal comes from friends/family/people we follow already being there.
- **Software (Windows):** more popular partly *because* more people already use it ⇒ **interoperability/compatibility** makes new buyers stick with the same operating system.

### 2.6 Diseconomies of scale (decreasing returns to scale)
- Large firms can be **very bureaucratic** — many layers, too many employees ⇒ productivity disadvantages.

**Worked example — the supervision/bureaucracy pyramid**
Assumption: **1 supervisor is needed for every 10 employees** (because you cannot verify effort; employees may need guidance/discipline).

| Production-floor employees | Supervisors | Managers | Senior manager | **Total headcount** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | — (1 person is both supervisor & manager/CEO) | 1 | — | **11** |
| 100 | 10 | 1 | — | **111** |
| 1,000 | 100 | 10 | 1 | **1,111** |

- Going from the 10-employee firm to the 1,000-employee firm: **production-floor employees ×100** (so production ≈ ×100), but **total headcount goes from 11 to 1,111 — more than ×100**.
- ⇒ **Wage cost rises more than proportionally to output** ⇒ diseconomies of scale.
- Additional qualitative cost: **more layers to cross** to reach the CEO ⇒ employees are **disincentivized from sharing ideas**; **some ideas get lost**.
- Summary statement from slide: *"Increase in production workers may require more than proportional increase in supervision; management and organizational cost will grow as a proportion of the firm's overall cost."*
- **Real-world example:** **Apple** manufactured its own products until roughly the **mid-1990s / early 2000s**, then decided in-house production was too costly and **outsourced production**, retaining only **high-value activities** in-house.
- Consequence: **many firms put a limit on how large they want to be.**

### 2.7 Formal definition of returns to scale (board work)

Let **X** and **Y** be inputs producing output **Q**, and let **λ > 0** be a scaling factor. Scale both inputs by λ:

- If **Q(λX, λY) = λ · Q(X, Y)** → **Constant Returns to Scale (CRS)**
- If **Q(λX, λY) > λ · Q(X, Y)** → **Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS)** / economies of scale
- If **Q(λX, λY) < λ · Q(X, Y)** → **Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS)** / diseconomies of scale

### 2.8 Cost functions

- **Cost function** tells how the **cost of production changes as output changes**: **C = C(Q)**.
- Important because the **isoprofit curve depends on cost**: **π = (P − unit cost) × Q**.
  - In the *previous* session unit cost was assumed **constant**; in general **unit cost varies with output**.

**Total cost curve (graph described)**
- **x-axis: quantity of cars (Q)**; **y-axis: total cost of production (C)**.
- Observations elicited from the class:
  1. **Total cost increases with quantity.**
  2. **Total cost is positive even when quantity = 0** ⇒ the **intercept is positive**.
     - This intercept is **fixed cost** — buildings, maintenance of machinery, etc., incurred even with zero production.
- The curve is drawn **convex** (increasingly steep) ⇒ rising marginal cost.

### 2.9 Average cost (AC)

- **Definition:** AC = **cost per unit produced** = **C(Q)/Q**.
- **Worked number (cars example):** at **point A**, total cost = **$80,000** for **20 cars** ⇒ **AC = 80,000 / 20 = $4,000 per car**. This is the value plotted in the lower panel.
- **Graph (lower panel):** x-axis = quantity of cars; y-axis = average cost. Shape: **U-shaped** — AC **falls initially**, reaches a minimum, then **rises**.
- **Graphical rule:** *Average cost at any point = **slope of the ray from the origin** to that point on the total cost curve.*
  - At point **B** the ray makes a **smaller angle** than at **A** ⇒ **AC(B) < AC(A)**.

**Why AC falls (economies of scale):**
1. **Mechanical/fixed-cost spreading** — electricity, buildings, machine maintenance, advertising, IPR are fixed costs spread over more cars. *Nothing technological happening.*
2. **Technological — gains from specialization** — as the firm matures from startup to mid-sized firm, teams specialize, productivity rises, cost per unit falls.

**Why AC eventually rises:**
- These graphs are usually drawn for a **plant**, not the whole **firm**. A **plant has limited capacity**; a **firm can open new plants**.
- The upturn = **capacity constraints**: workers doing **overtime** (paid extra), machines run on overtime ⇒ **breakdowns**, higher operational/maintenance cost ⇒ higher AC.
- In the **long run**, or at **firm level** (new plants can be opened, no plant needs overtime), this upturn **need not happen** — economies of scale persist.

### 2.10 Marginal cost (MC)

- **Definition:** the change in **total cost** from producing **one additional unit** of output.
  - "By what amount will my total cost change if I increase output by one unit?"
- **Graphical rule:** MC = **slope of the total cost curve at that point** (NOT the slope of the ray from the origin — that's AC).
- **Worked numbers:** at **point A** (moving from 20 to 21 cars), the slope ⇒ **MC ≈ 2,200**. At **point D** (further right), MC is **higher still**.
- Because the **total cost curve is convex**, **MC increases with output**.

### 2.11 Relationship between MC and AC

Graph described: both curves on (quantity, cost) axes — **MC in pink**, **AC in blue**; in this illustration MC rises at a **constant rate** (it could also rise at an increasing rate, or fall).

**Rules:**
- **If MC < AC (pink below blue) ⇒ AC is falling.**
- **If MC > AC (pink above blue) ⇒ AC is rising.**
- ⇒ **MC and AC intersect exactly at the minimum point of AC.**
- That minimum point is called the **minimum efficient scale**. Beyond it, economies of scale are **exhausted** and AC keeps rising.

**Class-height analogy (used to teach this):**
- 29 people in the Zoom room, **average height ≈ 5′8″**. A 30th person joins with height **5′9″**. Since the **marginal** person's height **exceeds** the average, the **average increases**. Same logic: marginal unit's cost above average cost ⇒ average cost rises; below ⇒ average cost falls.

**Q&A clarification (important conceptual point):**
- Economies of scale is a statement about **average cost only**. It says **nothing** about whether MC is rising or falling.
- MC **need not be falling** for AC to fall — all that matters is **MC < AC**.
- If MC is falling, AC will also keep falling. Once MC rises above AC (right of the intersection point B), economies of scale have been **exhausted**.

### 2.12 Estimating the demand curve (back to the demand side)

To make pricing and production decisions, managers need to know demand. Two approaches:

**(a) Existing products — estimate from data**
- Firm has data from its stores on **quantity sold at different price points** (as in the Cheerios case).
- Fit a **regression** — a relationship between **sales and price**.
- **Caution (econometric identification):** you must check whether the observed price–quantity relationship arises from **shifts in consumers' demand** or from **shifts in the supply of the product**. Requires **advanced econometric techniques**.

**(b) New products (no data) — surveys of willingness to pay**
- Example: **space travel** — no existing sales data, so ask people their **willingness to pay**.
- **Survey design problem:** respondents may **understate** their true valuation, fearing the seller will charge exactly their valuation and leave them no benefit ("I can only pay this much, not more").
- ⇒ The trick is to design the survey to **elicit the true valuation** of consumers.

**Linear demand curve for cars (graph described)**
- Axes: **quantity on x-axis, price on y-axis**; straight, **downward-sloping** line.
- Numbers used: **if the car is priced above $8,000, nobody buys**; as price falls, more and more people buy.
- **Interpretation:** the demand curve shows **willingness to pay in decreasing order** — the consumer at the top/left has the **highest willingness to pay**; as price falls you bring in consumers with **lower and lower willingness to pay**.

**Two strategic archetypes (from previous session, restated)**
- **Mass-market / "Tesco" strategy** — price very low, attract many low-willingness-to-pay customers, **bet on volumes** for profit.
- **Premium / "Apple" strategy** — charge a high premium, requires **dedicated clientele / brand loyalists** who stay despite the high price; profit comes from the **margin per unit**.
- Which works depends on **how steep or flat the demand curve is**:
  - **Flat** demand curve ⇒ people **don't have much willingness to pay** anyway; you **lose customers very quickly** as price rises.
  - **Steep** demand curve ⇒ a **large number of people with high willingness to pay**.
- This steepness is the notion of **price sensitivity / elasticity**.

### 2.13 Digression: Price discrimination (student question about "buy one get one" offers)

Base case in Chapter 7: **uniform pricing** — everyone pays the same price per unit.

- **First-degree price discrimination** — a **different price for every individual** for the same product.
  - Example: a customer arrives in a **Mercedes-Benz** ⇒ assumed high willingness to pay ⇒ charged a high price; a modest-looking customer ⇒ charged a low price.
  - Online: **internet cookies / browsing history / location (posh neighbourhood)** used to show higher-priced items; the widely-discussed claim that **Uber prices shown on iPhone vs Android** differed.
  - **US university tuition**: schools ask for **parents' tax records**; richer parents ⇒ higher tuition, poorer ⇒ lower. You can't bluff because income is verified.
  - Principle: **the more information a seller has about you, the more they can squeeze your willingness to pay.**
- **Second-degree price discrimination** — **discount conditional on buying more**: "buy two get one free", "buy one get one free". You get the lower per-unit price **only if you buy enough**; you can't buy one at half price.
  - Distinction drawn with FMCG volume pricing: selling **10 detergents at the same unit price** as 1 is *volume-based profit under uniform pricing*, **not** second-degree. Second degree = **higher price to someone buying one, lower per-unit price to someone buying two**.
  - "Volume-based profit need not always involve second degree, but second degree is essentially increasing profits through volumes." (Converse need not be true.)
- **Third-degree price discrimination** — pricing by **observable group attributes** (e.g. student status, weekday free time).
  - **Restaurant happy hours example:** happy hours/low prices **mid-week (e.g. Wednesday)**. Reasoning: people free on a weekday are likely **less busy and less rich**; busy people (who can pay more) come on **weekends**, when a **high price** is charged. Uniform high pricing every day would leave tables **empty** mid-week.
- **Purpose of all these strategies:** to **increase producer surplus at the cost of consumer surplus** (to be shown graphically later).

### 2.14 Marginal revenue and the profit-maximization condition (calculus)

- **Economic profit** = **Revenue − Cost**.
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** = **change in revenue from selling one additional unit of output**.

**Derivation of MR:**

Revenue: **R = P × Q**, and **P is itself a function of Q** (because P comes from the demand curve).

$$MR = \frac{d(PQ)}{dQ} = P + Q\frac{dP}{dQ}$$

(uses the product/"UV" rule, since both P and Q are functions of Q).

- **Economic meaning:** to increase quantity sold, you must also **lower the price** — that price reduction on all units must be accounted for. Since dP/dQ < 0, **MR < P**.

**Profit maximization (first-order condition):**

Let **π = P·Q − C(Q)**.

$$\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial Q} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \underbrace{P + Q\frac{dP}{dQ}}_{MR} - \underbrace{\frac{dC}{dQ}}_{MC} = 0$$

$$\boxed{MR = MC \text{ at the profit-maximizing quantity}}$$

*(Note: the lecturer wrote the cost term as (∂C/∂Q)·dQ and then noted that in the standard MC definition dQ = 1, so MC = ∂C/∂Q.)*

### 2.15 Slope of the isoprofit curve (derivation)

- **Isoprofit curve** lives in **(quantity, price) space** and is **downward sloping**; along it **profit is constant**.
- Why derive its slope: to link **consumers' price sensitivity** to the **profit margin**.

Start from **π = P·Q − C(Q)**. Take the total differential; along an isoprofit curve **dπ = 0**:

$$Q\,dP + P\,dQ = \frac{\partial C}{\partial Q}\,dQ = MC\cdot dQ$$

(Here MC·dQ is written because dQ need not equal 1 in this general case.)

Rearranging:

$$\boxed{\frac{dP}{dQ}\Big|_{\text{isoprofit}} = -\frac{P - MC}{Q}}$$

- **Sign check:** since **P > MC** (profit positive on the marginal unit), **(P − MC) > 0**, so the slope is **negative** ⇒ the isoprofit curve is **downward sloping**.

### 2.16 Price elasticity of demand (ε)

- **Definition:** the **percentage change in quantity demanded per given percentage change in price** — i.e. consumers' **price sensitivity**.

$$\boxed{\varepsilon = \frac{dQ/Q}{dP/P}}$$

- **Sign:** always **negative**, because dP/dQ (the demand curve slope) is negative.
- **Elasticity varies along a linear demand curve:** at the top (price-intercept end) ε = **−∞**; moving down the curve it rises toward **0**.

**Classification:**

| Value of ε | Name |
|---|---|
| ε = −∞ | **Perfectly elastic** |
| −∞ < ε < −1 | **Elastic** |
| ε = −1 | **Unitary elastic** |
| −1 < ε < 0 | **Inelastic** |
| ε = 0 | **Perfectly inelastic** |

- **Perfectly inelastic interpretation:** for **any** change in price, quantity demanded does not change — e.g. an **essential life-saving medicine**, or **drinking water**; you cannot survive without it.
- **Perfectly elastic interpretation:** any increase in price and you **abandon the product for another seller** — e.g. the **roadside vegetable seller**: if one seller charges more than the next, why buy from him?

### 2.17 The key result: elasticity and the profit margin (the Lerner-type condition)

**Setup (graph):** the **demand curve** (downward sloping) and the **isoprofit curve** are **tangent** at the optimum — call the tangency **point Z**. At Z, the **slopes are equal**.

From the elasticity definition:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{P}{Q}\cdot\frac{dQ}{dP} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{dP}{dQ} = \frac{P}{\varepsilon\, Q}$$

Equate with the isoprofit slope at the tangency point Z:

$$\frac{P}{\varepsilon Q} = -\frac{P-MC}{Q}$$

$$\boxed{\frac{P - MC}{P} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}$$

- Since **ε is negative**, the two negatives cancel and the **right-hand side is positive**.
- **Interpretation:** **(P − MC)/P is the profit margin as a percentage of price.** It is **inversely related to the elasticity of demand**:
  - **Higher elasticity (more price-sensitive consumers) ⇒ lower profit margin.**
  - **Lower elasticity (less price-sensitive consumers) ⇒ higher profit margin.**
- Core takeaway: *"You can make more profit if your consumers are less price sensitive; less profit if your consumers are more price sensitive."*

| Profit max FOC | MR = MC |
| Isoprofit slope | dP/dQ = −(P − MC)/Q |

| Tangency result | (P − MC)/P = −1/ε |

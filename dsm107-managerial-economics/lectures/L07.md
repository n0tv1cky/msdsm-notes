## 1. Overview

This session has two halves. The first half completes the **income effect / substitution effect** decomposition of a wage rise in the labour–leisure (consumption vs. free time) model, then critically evaluates the model itself — answering the standard objections (people don't do maths, other factors matter, groups differ, culture and politics matter) with the ideas of *heuristics*, *ceteris paribus*, and the *representative agent*. The second half opens a new module: **modelling social interactions with game theory**. It closes with Q&A on pure vs. mixed strategies, multiple equilibria, model selection, and why game theory gives way to market demand/supply models when the number of agents becomes very large.

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order Taught

### A. Recap — Income and Substitution Effects of a Wage Rise

**Setup (from previous session):**
- Axes: **free time (leisure) on the x-axis**, **consumption (goods/income) on the y-axis**.
- Budget constraint / feasible frontier; slope = wage = opportunity cost of free time.
- Indifference curves represent preferences; optimum is at tangency of indifference curve with the budget line.

**The A → C → D decomposition:**
- **Point A** = original optimum on the original budget line.
- **Point D** = new (final) optimum on the *new*, steeper budget line after the wage rise.
- **Point C** = hypothetical intermediate point. Constructed by drawing a **dashed budget line parallel to the ORIGINAL budget line** (i.e. same relative prices, same slope as before) shifted upward until it is **tangent to the NEW (final) indifference curve through D**.
  - This dashed line **does not exist in the real world** — it is an analytical device to break the observed A → D movement into two conceptual steps.
  - Interpretation: "Holding the person as satisfied as she ends up being at D, what would she have chosen if only her affordability had risen but relative prices had not changed?" → answer is C.

- **Income effect = A → C.** Assesses the increased feasible options from a rise in wage *without* considering the change in relative prices. Purely an affordability/purchasing-power change → **parallel upward shift** of the feasible set.
- **Substitution effect = C → D.** Movement **along the indifference curve** until tangency with the new budget line. Captures the change in *relative prices*: the wage rise has made free time **more expensive** (higher opportunity cost of leisure), so leisure is consumed less.

**Result in the worked case:** overall movement A → D gives **less free time and more consumption**, because the **substitution effect dominates the income effect**.

**Signs of the two effects (exam-relevant rule):**
- **Income effect:** can be **positive or zero** (no change). Goods with a negative income effect (inferior goods) exist but are **excluded from this discussion**.
- **Substitution effect:** **always negative** — i.e. **always in the opposite direction to the price change**. If something becomes costlier you consume less of it; if cheaper, more of it. Here the price of free time (the wage) rose → free time consumed falls.

**Summary statement given:** *A wage rise has two effects on the choice of free time:*
1. **Income effect** — the extra purchasing power. Do you keep leisure constant and consume more, or keep consumption constant and buy more leisure?
2. **Substitution effect** — the effect of the **change in opportunity cost** of free time.

**Role of flexibility/preferences:** A student on summer break has high flexibility and can substitute freely between consumption and free time. Someone with domestic responsibilities (note: "free time" here includes household chores, not just idleness) faces tighter constraints and may not be able to work longer even if wages rise. **Different constraints and tastes ⇒ differently shaped indifference curves ⇒ different responses to the same wage rise.**

### B. Critiques of the Model (and the Responses)

This section was given substantial time — likely conceptual exam material.

**Critique 1: "People don't actually draw graphs or compute tangency points."**
- True. Real people use **heuristics, habit, and trial and error** — they try working more or less over several iterations and settle on what suits them.
- The model is used *by analysts* to **predict** the outcome of that decision process, not to describe the conscious mental arithmetic.
- **Analogy given:** A cricketer or tennis player doesn't compute trajectories — hand–eye coordination is natural. But a programmer building an Xbox cricket/tennis game *must* write mathematical equations so the on-screen player behaves as a real player would. Economists are like the programmer: modelling naturally-occurring behaviour mathematically.
- **"A model is a caricature — that is the feature, not the bug."** If you imitate the person entirely, you can no longer understand the thought process clearly. Models exist to make us think more clearly.

**Critique 2: "What about all the other factors?"** (e.g. childcare availability, domestic duties)
- Answer: **ceteris paribus** (Latin: "all else held constant"). Every variable not modelled is treated as an **exogenous factor held fixed**. If those factors change (employer adds or withdraws childcare), the decision changes.

**Critique 3: "What about large groups of workers with different preferences?"**
- Answer: this is a **representative agent model** — it describes an *average* person. It need not describe every individual; it is adequate if it proxies what most people do.

**Critique 4: "What about culture and politics?"**
- **Culture:** Cross-country evidence from the start of the module — **South Koreans work far longer hours than the French despite similar per-capita income.** Culture is not modelled explicitly but is **embedded in the shape of the indifference curves**:
  - Society values leisure more → indifference curves **steeper**;
  - Society values work/consumption more → indifference curves **flatter**;
  - (with **free time on the x-axis, consumption on the y-axis**).
- **Politics:** Objection — workers don't choose their own hours; employers do (unless self-employed/freelance). Response:
  - Societies **vote for parties on the basis of working-hours policy** (European examples of voting to reduce, sometimes increase, statutory hours). So hours are chosen **indirectly through politics**.
  - Also through **job selection**: if an employer is known for long hours and you dislike that, you may not apply. People **sort into jobs** matching their preferences — provided they have the luxury of options.

### C. New Module: Modelling Social Interactions

**Objective stated:** *Use the tools of game theory to model social interactions and explain social dilemmas.*

**What's new versus earlier modules:**
- Module 1: a single producer choosing a technology (interaction with the customer only via price).
- Module 2: a single employee choosing work vs. free time (interaction with the employer only via the wage).
- **This module: two or more individuals interacting directly**, where interactions may be **strategic**.
- Tool required: **game theory** — a branch of mathematics for modelling social interactions.

**Definition — Social dilemma:** *A situation in which actions taken by each person independently, thinking about their own self-interest, result in a **socially suboptimal outcome**.*
- Key point: "suboptimal" is **relative to some other outcome that is also FEASIBLE** — an outcome that *could* have been achieved if people acted together and accounted for society-wide costs and benefits.
- General cause: **people do not fully account for the effects of their actions on others.**

**Examples of social dilemmas:**
1. **Climate change** — individual consumption, transport, energy, plastic use choices generate emissions/damage whose costs fall on everyone; each individual ignores the damage imposed on others.
2. **Traffic jams** (the "relatable" everyday example) — you take your personal vehicle for comfort/safety/less exposure to pollution; you count the fuel cost from your own pocket and your own benefit, but **not the congestion cost you impose on others**. Paradox: nobody *wants* to sit in a jam, yet jams occur day after day. This is the signature of a social dilemma — undesirable outcomes arising from each person's self-interested action.
3. **Tragedy of the commons.** "Commons" = **common property resources** — resources not owned by anyone in particular.
   - Examples: a pond free for anyone to fish; **free public Wi-Fi** (bandwidth is always too low to even make a call, precisely *because* it is free and everyone over-exploits it).
   - Fishing: if everyone fishes freely, the catch exceeds the **replenishment rate** → stock exhaustion → nobody, including you, can fish in future.
   - Canal irrigation: drawing too much water starves other farmers. Across India, especially **peninsular India**, villages have **informal rules** governing how scarce irrigation water is shared.
4. **Free riding.** A shared good (group project, a **clean kitchen** in a shared flat) benefits everyone once provided. Cleaning is costly effort, so others free ride. Anticipating that they will be free-ridden on, **nobody puts in effort → permanently dirty kitchen.**

**Social dilemmas can also be about foregone BENEFITS, not just excess costs:**
- **Vaccination.** Taking a flu/epidemic vaccine protects you *and* protects others (they can't catch it from you). Individuals **underestimate the benefit they confer on others**, so private demand is too low. Hence governments **subsidise vaccines** — e.g. during COVID, some brands were nominally priced or free.

**Not all social interaction is a dilemma — the counterexample:**
- **The invisible hand** (Adam Smith idea): capitalism/market economy rests on individuals pursuing self-interest yet benefiting society. The producer makes the best possible phone to get the highest price; the consumer gets the best possible phone and pays a price compensating the producer. **No charity or altruism is involved between buyer and seller**, yet enormously sophisticated production and consumption happen via the market.
- Conclusion: social interactions can be **beneficial**; they are not always suboptimal — but the suboptimal ones (e.g. climate change) can threaten our existence.

**Solving social dilemmas:**
- **Moral appeals / altruism** — can be ingrained as culture (e.g. anti-littering norms in developed countries achieved with little government policy).
- **Government policy** — often necessary at large scale (traffic, climate). Game theory helps in **designing** such policy.
- Method: **first** characterise the situation using game theory (identify whether it's a tragedy of the commons, free riding, etc.); **then** work out how to solve it.

**Q&A: "Can government policy completely eliminate free riding?"**
- Answer: **No — realistically not.** Example: **tax evasion** is free riding (everyone uses roads and public facilities; some who can afford to pay taxes don't). Government **capacity is limited**, especially in developing countries, so some free riding always persists.
- Practical approach is a **mix of policies**, including **moral persuasion**: e.g. a thank-you email for paying tax ("thank you for contributing to the nation") producing a **warm-glow effect**; the **"Give It Up" LPG subsidy campaign**.
- Goal is to **reduce** free riding; **elimination is desirable but not practically achievable**.

### D. Game Theory Vocabulary (Definitions — likely exam material)

- **Social interaction:** a situation involving **more than one person or party**, where one's action affects **both their own outcomes/wellbeing and other people's outcomes**.
- **Strategic interaction:** a **subset of social interaction** in which people are **aware** that their actions have consequences for others.
- **Strategy:** the actions that people can take when engaging in a social interaction.
- **Game:** a **model of a strategic interaction**.
- **Game theory:** a **set of models of strategic interactions**.

**A game is defined by four elements:**
1. **Players** — at least two.
2. **Feasible strategies** — the actions available to each player.
3. **Information** — what each player knows when choosing an action: do they know the other's action? do they know the other's available strategies and payoffs?
4. **Payoffs** — the outcomes for **every possible combination of actions**.

**Timing structures:**
- **Sequential game:** one player moves first and the second observes the move before responding — e.g. **chess**.
- **Simultaneous game:** both choose without observing the other; the outcome is revealed only afterwards — e.g. **a batsman deciding to pull/hook while the bowler decides on a yorker**; each can only guess, and both actions are revealed only in the split second the ball is delivered.

**Repetition:**
- **One-shot game:** a single interaction.
- **Repeated game:** the same interaction occurs repeatedly (e.g. a planting decision every year). Behaviour differs: with repetition, people **remember past actions** and are far more conscious of how they act. Analogy: you behave more carefully with **neighbours** (repeated) than with a stranger sitting next to you on a **flight or bus** (one-shot).

**Scope of this course:** only **simultaneous, full-information (complete-information) games**.
- **Full information** = both players know their own and the other's feasible strategies, **and** the entire payoff matrix.

### E. Worked Example — The Anil & Bala Rice/Cassava Game

**Story and assumptions:**
- Two farmers: **Anil** and **Bala** — assumed to be the only two farmers in the society.
- Two crops: **Rice** and **Cassava**.
- **Anil's land produces both rice and cassava equally well** (absolute advantage in both / equally good at both).
- **Bala's land is particularly suited to RICE** (comparative advantage in rice); it is **ill-suited to cassava**.
- **One-shot, simultaneous, full-information game.** Neither farmer knows what the other has chosen; they discover the outcome only after sowing and producing.
- **Payoffs depend on market prices × quantity produced.** Excess supply of a crop relative to demand → **price crash** (glut). Farmers dislike price crashes — "we routinely see agricultural prices crash and farmers dump output on roads in resentment."
- Note on coordination: Anil and Bala *could* talk and coordinate, but **commitments can be broken** — there is always an **incentive to cheat** if deviating yields a higher payoff. (Traffic analogy: if everyone agrees to take public transport, each individual reasons "the road will be empty, so let me drive" — and everyone ends up in a jam.)

**Qualitative description of the four cells:**

| | Bala: Rice | Bala: Cassava |
|---|---|---|
| **Anil: Rice** | Glut of rice (price crash), shortage of cassava | No glut; good prices for both crops — BUT Bala is growing the crop his land is unsuited for |
| **Anil: Cassava** | No glut; good prices for both — AND each is matched to his land's productive capacity (best case) | Glut of cassava (cassava price crash), shortage of rice; Bala again mismatched to his land |

**The payoff matrix (row player = Anil; column player = Bala; first number = row player's payoff, second = column player's payoff):**

|  | **Bala: Rice (R)** | **Bala: Cassava (C)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Anil: Rice (R)** | **4, 4** | **6, 3** |
| **Anil: Cassava (C)** | **6, 6** ✓✓ | **5, 2** |

**Reading convention (stated explicitly):** the **first element in each cell is the ROW player's (Anil's) payoff**; the **second element is the COLUMN player's (Bala's) payoff**.

**Justification of each number (discussed in class):**
- **(R, R) = (4, 4):** both grow rice → rice glut → price crash → both get a modest 4.
- **(R, C) = (6, 3):** Anil is the *only* rice grower → no glut → **higher rice price → Anil gets 6** (up from 4). Bala is the only cassava grower so the cassava *price* is high, **but** his land is unsuited to cassava so **quantity produced is very small**; since payoff = price × quantity, **Bala gets only 3** (down from 4).
- **(C, R) = (6, 6):** Best joint outcome. Different crops → **no glut, prices high for both**. Bala grows rice — the crop his land is **best suited** for — so his quantity is large → **Bala gets 6**. Anil is equally good at both, so his payoff is unchanged at **6**.
- **(C, C) = (5, 2):** Both grow cassava → cassava glut → price crash; and no one grows rice → rice shortage. Because Bala's land is bad for cassava, **the glut is not as severe** as the rice glut in (R,R), so Anil's payoff (**5**) is a bit better than his 4 in (R,R). Bala is worst off at **2** — bad crop-land match *and* a glut.

**Definition — Best response:** *A strategy that yields the **highest payoff given the other player's strategy**.*
- Each player must compute a best response **to each** of the other player's strategies. These may be the same or different across the other's strategies.

**Solving the game (the exact procedure demonstrated):**

*Anil's best responses (Anil is the row player → compare payoffs DOWN a column, holding Bala's choice fixed):*
- If **Bala plays Rice** (first column): Anil gets **4** from Rice, **6** from Cassava → **Anil's best response = Cassava**. ✓ on (C, R).
- If **Bala plays Cassava** (second column): Anil gets **6** from Rice, **5** from Cassava → **Anil's best response = Rice**. ✓ on (R, C).

*Bala's best responses (Bala is the column player → compare payoffs ACROSS a row, holding Anil's choice fixed):*
- If **Anil plays Rice** (first row): Bala gets **4** from Rice, **3** from Cassava → **Bala's best response = Rice**. ✓ on (R, R).
- If **Anil plays Cassava** (second row): Bala gets **6** from Rice, **2** from Cassava → **Bala's best response = Rice**. ✓ on (C, R).
- (Note: Rice is Bala's best response to *both* of Anil's choices.)

*Result:* the cell **(C, R)** — Anil = Cassava, Bala = Rice — is the **only cell carrying two tick marks**.

**Definition — Equilibrium (of a game):** *An outcome in which **each player's strategy is a best response to the other player's strategy**.*

- Here the equilibrium is written **(C, R)** — again, **first element = row player's (Anil's) strategy, second element = column player's (Bala's) strategy**.
- Logic: whenever Anil chooses C, Bala's best move is R; whenever Bala chooses R, Anil's best move is C. Given what the other is doing, **neither has any reason to change**.

**Named result — NASH EQUILIBRIUM:**
- Named after **John Nash**, mathematician and **Nobel laureate in Economics**.
- In his **PhD thesis** he proved: **for any game with a FINITE number of players and a FINITE number of strategies, an equilibrium always exists.**
  - (Here: 2 players, 2 strategies each — but the theorem holds for any finite numbers.)
- His proof **included mixed-strategy equilibria**, not just pure strategies.
- Cultural reference given: the book/film **"A Beautiful Mind"** (Russell Crowe plays Nash).

**Concluding point:** Just as we (the analysts) can solve the matrix, **Anil and Bala can solve it too** — so they will realise it is in their interest to play (Cassava, Rice), and that is the outcome that actually gets realised.

### F. Q&A — Extensions and Conceptual Points

**(i) Very large numbers of players (e.g. all farmers in India choosing rice vs. wheat):**
- The number is still **finite** (finite ≠ infinite; "infinite is uncountable", this is countable), so a Nash equilibrium exists — but it would be **hopelessly complicated to write on paper**.
- A simple **2×2 matrix is not the right model** there. Options: group farmers into a small number of *groups* and analyse those strategically, or switch to a different class of model.
- **Key principle:** *Strategic interaction requires the number of players to be sufficiently small that one person's action materially affects others' payoffs.* Among crores of people, a single (ordinary) person's action does not materially change others' outcomes — the **strategic element becomes less salient as numbers grow** (though collectively we still affect each other, as with climate change and traffic).
- Hence for mass phenomena we use **market demand and market supply models** (announced as the **next segment — Chapter 7**), which use mathematical expressions to summarise the behaviour of huge numbers of people (all farmers' wheat supply; all consumers' wheat demand).
- Game theory remains the right tool where strategic interaction is strong: **organisations, neighbourhoods**, small-group settings.

**(ii) Can we introduce probabilities? — Pure vs. Mixed strategies:**
- What was solved above is a **pure strategy equilibrium**: probability **100%** is placed on a single strategy (Anil sows cassava with certainty; Bala sows rice with certainty).
- A **mixed strategy equilibrium** assigns probabilities less than 100% across strategies — e.g. (illustratively, not the equilibrium of this game) Anil sows cassava with **30%** probability and rice with **70%**.
- Cricket illustration: as a batsman you assign, say, **30%** probability to a short ball and **70%** to a yorker.
- Nash's proof covers mixed-strategy equilibria.

**(iii) Where do you stop when building a model?**
- It's partly an **art**, but there is a rule of thumb: **include a variable if omitting it would change the model's prediction; exclude it if including it would not materially change the prediction but would needlessly complicate the model.**
- Which variables are relevant is determined by **theory and empirical evidence** — this judgement is the real essence of model selection.

**(iv) Can a game have more than one Nash equilibrium?**
- **Yes — multiple equilibria are possible.** On the matrix, **more than one cell will carry two ticks.** They can all be identified, but **you cannot be sure which one will actually arise.**
- Sometimes one equilibrium is **better** than the other; sometimes it's an arbitrary coin toss.
- **Example of arbitrary multiple equilibria:** some countries **drive on the left** (India), others **drive on the right** — neither is better or worse; each is simply an equilibrium.
- **Example where equilibria differ materially:** **underdevelopment**. A development economist views underdevelopment as an economy **stuck in the worse of two equilibria**; without a **policy nudge** it stays there. Policies such as **education policy and industrial policy** can move a country from the inferior to the superior equilibrium.
- **Clarification (important conceptual point):** multiple equilibria are **not a defect of the model** or a sign of "too few parameters." Adding parameters is *not* the fix. The **society itself genuinely has multiple possible outcomes**; the model's job is to **explain society as it is**, not to force a unique answer. (As policy makers, once you determine one equilibrium is materially better, the task is to engineer the move.)
- A game with multiple equilibria will be covered in a later session.

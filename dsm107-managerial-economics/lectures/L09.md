## 1. Overview

This session closes out the game-theory/social-interactions module and opens the new module on **"The Firm and its Customers"** (Chapter 7 of the reading). The first half finishes the discussion of **social (non-self-regarding) preferences**: how altruism reshapes indifference curves drawn over *own payoff vs. the other player's payoff*, how altruism can change the equilibrium of the pest-control Prisoners' Dilemma from (Terminator, Terminator) to (IPC, IPC), how **repeated interaction** can sustain cooperation even among purely selfish players, and other social preferences (inequality aversion, reciprocity, social norms). The second half shifts from individuals and pairwise social interactions to the **interaction between profit-maximising firms and utility-maximising customers**: why firms are large, why different firms pursue radically different pricing strategies (Tesco's low-margin/high-volume vs. Apple's high-margin/premium), the role of **differentiated products**, **market power**, and **price elasticity of demand**, and finally the two central analytical tools — the **isoprofit curve** (what the firm *is willing* to trade off) and the **demand curve** (what the firm *is constrained* to trade off), both drawn with price on the y-axis and quantity on the x-axis.

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order Taught

### A. Recap: Self-interest and social optimum
- Self-regarding (purely self-interested) preferences are **not** the only kind of preferences.
- Sometimes self-interest *does* deliver the social optimum → the **Invisible Hand game**.
- Sometimes self-interest **destroys** cooperation and the socially beneficial outcome → the **Prisoners' Dilemma**.

### B. Experimental evidence for altruism
- In a **one-shot Prisoners' Dilemma**, roughly **1/5 (20%) or more** of players choose to **cooperate** — i.e. they play the *dominated* strategy. Interpreted as evidence of **altruism**.
- When the game is **repeated**, the likelihood of cooperation rises to about **50%**.

### C. The pest-control game (payoff structure used throughout)
Two farmers, **Anil** and **Bala**. Strategies: **T = Terminator (toxic pesticide)** and **I = IPC (Integrated Pest Control)**.

| | Bala: I | Bala: T |
|---|---|---|
| **Anil: I** | (3, 3) | (1, 4) |
| **Anil: T** | (4, 1) | (2, 2) |

(Anil's payoff first.) Dominant-strategy equilibrium with purely selfish players = **(T, T) = (2, 2)**, which is worse for both than (I, I) = (3, 3).

### D. Indifference curves over payoffs (graphical treatment of altruism)
**Axes:** Anil's payoff on the **x-axis**, Bala's payoff on the **y-axis**.

- **Purely self-interested Anil:** indifference curves are **vertical lines**. He cares only about his own payoff; Bala's payoff is irrelevant. Moving to a vertical line further **right** = higher utility; lines further left = lower utility. E.g. his utility rises when his payoff moves from a line at 2 to a line at 4.
  - Such a person has **neither altruism nor envy**.
- **Altruistic Anil:** indifference curves become **downward-sloping and convex**. Anil is willing to **tolerate a reduction in his own payoff** if it raises Bala's payoff enough — moving left (less for Anil) but up (more for Bala) can keep him equally satisfied.

**Applying this to the game:**
- With altruistic curves, **(I, T)** lies on a *higher* indifference curve than **(T, T)**.
  - Reasoning: moving from (T,T) to (I,T), Anil loses only **1** (2 → 1) while Bala gains **2** (2 → 4). If Anil puts enough weight on Bala's payoff, this is a net gain in his utility.
- Similarly **(I, I)** is preferred to **(T, I)**.
- ⇒ For a sufficiently altruistic Anil, **I (IPC) becomes the dominant strategy** — he plays I regardless of what Bala plays.

**Resulting equilibria depend on Bala's degree of altruism:**
- If **Bala is also altruistic**: he reasons that switching from T to I costs him 1 but gains Anil 2 ⇒ he prefers (I, I) to (I, T). Result: **(I, I)** mutual cooperation — a dominant strategy equilibrium at (3, 3).
- If **Bala remains self-interested**: he plays T. Equilibrium is **(I, T)** = Anil gets **1**, Bala gets **4**. Bala enjoys high profits while **Anil bears the monetary cost of Bala's Terminator use** — and bears it willingly because of his altruistic preferences.
- **Key takeaway:** *different equilibria are possible depending on the degree of altruism of each player.*

**Q&A raised in class:** Can both players' indifference curves go on one graph? — Yes in principle, but it becomes cluttered and requires labelling. Better to draw a second graph for Bala (with Bala's payoff on his own x-axis; the shape looks the same). If preferences are identical, both have identically shaped curves.

### E. Repeated interaction — cooperation *without* altruism
Setting: the pest-control game is played **every season**. Players now have a **history** — at the start of each season each can observe what the other played last season (in a one-shot game neither knows the other's simultaneous choice).

Scenario: **Bala played IPC last season; Anil played Terminator.** Anil's reasoning this season:
- **Temptation:** if Bala plays I again and Anil plays T, Anil gets **4** instead of **3** — a one-off gain of **1 unit** in this season only.
- **Punishment/consequence:** if Anil cheats again, Bala will stop cooperating forever; both revert to **(T, T) = (2, 2)** for the rest of their lives.
- **Trade-off:** gain of **+1 this season** vs. losing **3 instead of 2 (i.e. 1 per season) for all future seasons**.
- **Conclusion:** unless Anil is **extremely impatient** (values only today's payoff and not tomorrow's), it is in his **own self-interest** to play IPC so that Bala continues to cooperate.
- Bala reasons symmetrically: he shouldn't assume Anil will cheat again, because a rational Anil sees that cooperating yields him better long-run payoffs.
- ⇒ **Repeated interaction can generate (IPC, IPC)** even though **both players remain entirely self-interested** — no altruism is required.

### F. Other social preferences
- **Inequality aversion:** people dislike outcomes in which some receive more than others; when payoffs are equal they need not be altruistic at all.
- **Reciprocity:** "tit-for-tat" — I am kind/helpful if you are kind; I am selfish if you are selfish. Documented as a **very strong** preference in experiments; people treat others according to how they were treated.
- **Social norm:** an understanding of **how to act in situations where one's actions affect others**. People look for cues about what the norm is, and whether the other party behaved kindly *when kindness was expected*.
  - **Queue example:** queuing to board a flight or at a coffee vending machine; someone barges in / breaks the queue. Pure altruism would say "let him go ahead." In practice people are **reciprocal** and **enforce the norm by punishing** — heckling, naming and shaming.
- General result: **cooperation breeds more cooperation, and vice versa.**

## 3. New Module: The Firm and its Customers

### G. Where this module sits in the course
Progression so far:
1. Firms choosing among **alternative technologies**, raising profits by better technology.
2. **Comparative advantage** and specialisation.
3. An individual **worker choosing between free time and hours of work**.
4. **Social interactions** (game theory) — any two people.
5. **Now:** interactions between **firms and customers**.

Each side brings its own objective function:
- **Customers** (e.g. "Karim") maximise **utility**.
- **Firms** maximise **profit**.

The module asks: how do firms choose **price** and **quantity to produce**, and how do these decisions depend on **consumer demand** and **production costs**? Do they mass-produce, or make the product premium by rolling out very few units?

**Practical relevance (data-science framing):** working with company-level data to predict demand for products, or to answer "will a price change raise profits?"

### H. Gains from trade and their distribution
- Market transactions are **not charity**. Firms do CSR separately, after profits; in the market they never do charity.
- **Gains from trade:** *both* parties gain from a transaction — customers gain by buying, firms gain by selling.
- The key question is **how these gains are distributed**. In some markets customers capture a lot of the gain; in others firms price so as to retain most of it (customers may keep only the bare minimum needed to induce them to participate).
- The distribution depends on the **price elasticity of demand**:
  - **High elasticity / high sensitivity:** a small price rise makes customers cut consumption fast. Typical of **non-essential items**.
  - **Low elasticity:** **medicines, basic food without alternatives** — customers won't cut back much even if prices rise.
- ⇒ *Question to ask about your own product:* **is price elasticity of demand high or low?** This determines the firm's **profit margin**.

### I. Why are firms large? (motivating graph)
- **Graph described:** a bar chart of **number of employees across large US firms** (household names such as **Walmart, Amazon**, etc.).
- Fact cited: in the US, **more than 50% of workers** work for firms with a large number of employees — a big number from an Indian perspective.
- Questions posed: What makes large firms profitable? Why do investors get good returns from them? Why do large-firm employees typically earn more on average than small-firm employees?
- Answer previewed: it boils down to **appropriate pricing and production decisions/strategies** — and **strategies are not uniform** across products; different strategies can each be good for growth and large profits.

### J. Two contrasting strategies (worked comparison)
**Tesco (UK retailer; Indian analogue: DMart or any large retailer)**
- Founder **Jack Cohen's motto:** sell as much as possible at as low a price as possible — *"pile it high, sell it cheap."*
- Profits linked to **volumes**, not to per-unit margins.
- Even if profit per item is small, the low price attracts so many customers that **total** profit is high.
- **Operating profit margin: ~6.5%.**

**Apple**
- Sets **high prices**; premiumisation of iPhone, iPad, Mac.
- Not a mass/volume play — plays on **margin per unit sold**.
- **Operating profit per unit on iPhones: ~50–58%** (publicly available data).

**Definition used:**
> Operating profit margin ≈ (Price − Cost per unit) / Price — i.e. roughly the **price–cost margin**.

**Interpretation stressed:** This does **not** say one company is more profitable than the other. Different industries sell different types of products, face **different types of consumer demand**, and this **necessitates differential strategies**. The road to high profits is not the same everywhere — "pile it high and sell it cheap" in one case, "charge a price premium" in the other.

**Other levers besides price (mentioned, partly outside the model):**
- **Cost minimisation** — pricing comes only *after* you produce; production incurs cost.
- **Choice and design of product; innovation; ability to attract and retain customers** (Apple is "phenomenal" at both).
- **Make-or-buy:** Apple currently **manufactures nothing itself** — it designs, does R&D, uses **contract manufacturers**, then takes the product back and markets it. This lowers its cost of production.
  - Underlying principle: a firm's cost of production is likely to rise if it does **too much in house**; sometimes it is better to **buy** than to **make**.
- ⇒ The module will also study **how cost of production varies with the size of output produced**.
- **Quality** is acknowledged but the basic model does not distinguish quality; instead products are treated as **differentiated**.
- **Employee motivation / retention / getting the best from employees** — real drivers of organisational success, but covered in other management modules, not microeconomics.

### K. Differentiated products
- **Definition:** a product with **distinctive features** such that customers can identify who made it and discern its characteristics.
  - Example: holding a phone, you can tell whether it is a Samsung, a Redmi, or an Apple — by finish, by operation.
  - Sources of differentiation: **brand, perceived durability, status/association, loyalty.**
- **Counter-example (undifferentiated):** potatoes, fruit, vegetables. You don't know which farm produced them; you don't pay attention to the producer. Holding basic quality constant, you simply buy from **whoever offers the cheapest price**.
- Consequence: for sophisticated products (laptops, smartphones, cars) buyers **do not go purely by price**; there are several other attributes, so a favourite brand can raise its price or launch a costlier product without losing the customer.
- **Why the module focuses on differentiated products:** *that is where the profits are*, and where firms need to understand how to raise profits.

### L. Market power and elasticity
- **Market power** = how much **extra price over cost** a seller can charge. **Margin = Price − Cost.** Higher margin ⇒ more market power.
- **Class discussion (student answers):**
  - **FMCG** (Rahul): brand loyalty, fast-moving goods, large market share (contrast: paints are slow-moving). Instructor's response: brand loyalty exists but **only up to a point**; FMCG is a **high-volume, low-margin** business — profits come from volumes, not margins.
  - **Railways:** **no substitutes**, only one provider. Instructor: correct direction — *the fewer the substitutes, the more market power.* But railways are also an **extreme and regulated** example.
  - Other examples endorsed: **paints, telecom** — few sellers.
- **Structure terms:** **monopoly** = only one seller; **oligopoly** = a few sellers. Fewer firms ⇒ greater market power.
- Link to demand: a **monopolist faces the entire market demand** — the consumer has no other option. A vegetable seller (or a firm in a crowded FMCG market) faces so many rivals that raising price sends customers straight to other sellers.
- **Key relationship (to be derived later with a simple formula):**
  > **Market power is inversely related to the price elasticity of demand.**
  > More price-sensitive consumers ⇒ lower market power. Less price-sensitive consumers ⇒ higher market power.
- The same tools will let us measure **gains from trade** and see in which markets consumers retain more of the gain.

### M. Policy application flagged
- With these tools one can analyse **a change in taxation** — e.g. a **GST reduction**: how much of the benefit goes to **customers** vs. **producers/sellers**? I.e. **how is the surplus divided between firms and customers?**

### N. Methodological note (important framing)
- The purpose of the analysis is **not to model the manager's thought process**. In reality managers use trial-and-error **heuristics**.
- We use a **mathematical model** instead. It may look unrealistic relative to how your organisation actually prices.
- **Criterion for a good model:** it should **predict the same outcome** the manager reaches through experience. If the model arrives at the manager's conclusion, the model is good enough — it need not think the way the manager thinks.
- Goal: an **analytical tool** to analyse the relationships among consumer demand, price, profits, and cost of production, so as to make better predictions.

## 4. The Core Analytical Tools

### O. Revenue, cost, profit (breakfast cereal example)
A firm makes a brand of **breakfast cereal**.

- **Profit = Revenue − Cost**
- **Total Revenue: TR = P × Q**
- **Total Cost: TC = (unit cost) × Q**
- Assume **unit cost C = $2 per pound** ⇒ **TC = 2Q**
- With a price of $3: **TR = 3Q**
- **Profit: π = TR − TC = P·Q − C·Q = (P − C)·Q**
  - With C = 2: **π = (P − 2)Q**
- Economists denote profit by **π (pi)**.

**Simplifying assumption:** unit cost is held **constant**. This is done only to keep the analysis simple; later in the module unit cost will be allowed to change with the quantity produced (the more realistic case).

**Why is P *not* treated as a constant?**
Because **quantity sold depends on the price charged**. This is the **Law of Demand** — one of the most empirically well-founded, near-universal relationships in economics:
> **Higher the price, lower the quantity demanded (hence lower quantity sold, since you can only sell to those who wish to buy); lower the price, higher the quantity demanded and sold.**
So **Q is a function of P** — P and Q are linked, and P cannot be treated as a free constant for any Q.

### P. The Isoprofit Curve
**Definition:** An **isoprofit curve is the locus of all combinations of price P and quantity Q that give the firm the same (constant) level of profit.** "Iso" = constant, exactly as in **indifference curve**, **isoquant**, and **isocost line**.

**Graph:** **Price on the y-axis, Quantity on the x-axis.**

**(i) The zero-isoprofit curve**
- With unit cost $2, the curve for **π = 0** is a **flat horizontal line at P = 2**.
- Every point on it (10,000 units at $2; 20,000 units at $2; …) yields π = (2 − 2)·Q = **0 for any Q**.
- The **position of the zero-isoprofit line tells you the unit cost** (here, $2).

**(ii) The π = $10,000 isoprofit curve** *(worked numbers, C = $2)*
| Q (pounds) | P ($/lb) | TR = P·Q | TC = 2·Q | π = TR − TC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,000 | 7 | 14,000 | 4,000 | **10,000** |
| 5,000 | 4 | 20,000 | 10,000 | **10,000** |
| (further points on the same locus) | … | … | … | **10,000** |

**Shape and movement:**
- The pattern is **very similar to indifference curves**: **downward-sloping** — as you **increase quantity you must lower price** to stay on the same isoprofit curve.
- From (5,000, $4): raising price while holding Q = 5,000 moves you **vertically up** onto a **higher** isoprofit curve.
- From (2,000, $7): selling more than 2,000 units at $7 moves you **to the right** onto a **higher** isoprofit curve.
- If **both P and Q increase**, or if **one is held constant and the other increases**, you are on a **higher** isoprofit curve.
- The graph shown had a family of curves: **π = 0**, **π = $10,000**, **π = $60,000**, and a fourth still higher. Moving from the bottom-left toward the **north-east**, you reach curves of successively **higher profit**.

**Why are P and Q both "goods" for the firm?**
Both contribute to profit. For a given cost C, the firm wants **Q as high as possible** and **P as high as possible**, so profit is as high as possible. This parallels the consumer's two goods X and Y.

**Parallel with consumer theory:**
| Consumer | Firm |
|---|---|
| Indifference curve (utility constant) | Isoprofit curve (profit constant) |
| Utility is abstract, has no units, cannot be measured | **Profit can be measured** (e.g. exactly $10,000) |
| Wants to reach the highest indifference curve | Wants to reach the highest isoprofit curve |
| Constrained by the **feasible set / budget constraint** | Constrained by the **demand curve** |

### Q. The Demand Curve
Before choosing a price, firms need information about demand: *how much are our potential consumers willing to pay for this product?*

**Graph:** **Price on the y-axis, Quantity on the x-axis** — deliberately the **same axes as the isoprofit curve**, so the two can be drawn on one graph (which is what comes next session).

**Shape:** **downward-sloping**.
- Numbers used: at a price of about **$3** the firm sells roughly **25,000 pounds**; at a higher price this falls to **below 20,000**.
- **Reservation price** = the **highest possible price**, at the **y-intercept** of the demand curve. Above this price there is **no demand at all**.
- Each point on the curve gives the **total demand for the product at that price**.

**Law of demand (restated):** the lower the price, the more customers wish to buy the product.

### R. The central contrast (end-of-session takeaway)
> - **Isoprofit curve = the trade-off between price and quantity that the firm is *willing* to make.**
> - **Demand curve = the trade-off between price and quantity that the firm is *constrained* to make.**

Firms would like to charge as high a price as possible (be on the highest isoprofit curve — "the firm wants the moon"), but consumers have a **maximum willingness to pay** and cannot be forced to pay more. The **downward-sloping demand curve** therefore acts as the firm's constraint, exactly as the **feasible frontier/budget line** acted as the consumer's constraint.

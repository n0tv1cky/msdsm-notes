# DSM-107 Managerial Economics — Session L03

## Study Notes: Technology, Production Functions, Isocost Lines, and the Industrial Revolution

---

## 1. Overview

This session continues the discussion of **technology** — the process by which a firm converts inputs (labour, capital, energy, raw materials) into output. It begins by defining inputs/factors of production and the **production function**, using a running example of olive-oil production under a **fixed-proportions, constant-returns-to-scale** technology. Two competing technologies (traditional vs. robotic) are compared using **average product of labour (labour productivity)** and the **energy–labour ratio**, and represented graphically as **rays from the origin** in (workers, energy) space, where the slope of the ray measures energy intensity. The lecture then moves to a five-technology cloth-production example (A–E) to show how some technologies are **dominated** (engineering-inefficient) and can be eliminated _without_ any price information, while the remaining ones can only be ranked once **input prices** are known. This motivates the derivation of the **isocost line** (cost = wL + pR), its intercepts, slope (−w/p), and shifts — including the key result that a **proportional change in both input prices leaves the isocost line unchanged** (only _relative_ prices matter). Finally, the model is applied to explain the **British Industrial Revolution**: a rise in wages relative to the price of coal steepened the isocost line, making the energy-intensive technology cheaper, generating **innovation rents**, and triggering Schumpeter's **creative destruction**.

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Technology and the classification of inputs

- **Technology** = the process a firm uses to convert a set of inputs (raw materials, labour, machines) into output.
- A key managerial choice: _which_ production technology to use, because there are many alternative ways of converting inputs into the same output.
- Broad classification of inputs — two types:
  - **Labour** — workers.
  - **Capital** — in _economics_ this means **buildings, machinery, equipment** (physical capital). ⚠️ This differs from _finance_, where "capital" means money.
- Once technology is chosen, the firm must then decide _how much_ of each input to employ.

### 2.2 Input intensity — labour-intensive vs capital/energy-intensive

- Technologies differ in the **intensity** of input usage.
- Example: **olive oil production**. Historically (centuries ago, pre-mechanisation), the stages — grading olives, washing, milling, mashing, **pressing** — were largely done by human labour with primitive machines.
  - A technology using relatively more workers = **labour-intensive**.
- Modern production processes increasingly use **less labour and more capital and energy** → **capital-intensive / energy-intensive**.

### 2.3 The production function

- **Definition:** A **production function** is a relationship (a mathematical function) that tells us how much output will be produced for a given amount of inputs. It is how we _represent_ technology.
- Notation for the olive oil example:
  - **Y** = amount of olive oil produced (output)
  - **M** = number of machines
  - **N** = number of workers
  - **E** = amount of energy used

$$Y = f(M,\; N,\; E)$$

- Inputs are also called **factors of production**.

### 2.4 Fixed-proportions technology (the olive oil example)

- The specific technology assumed: inputs must be combined in **fixed proportions**.
- **The numbers:**
  - **3 machines : 1 worker : 80 kilowatt-hours of energy → 50 litres of oil**
  - Proportion = **3 : 1 : 80**
- Implications:
  - Output can only be increased in **multiples** of the input bundle.
  - Double all three inputs (6 machines, 2 workers, 160 kWh) → output doubles to **100 litres**. Triple them → output triples.
  - **There is no point in increasing only one input without increasing the others** — if one input does not rise proportionally, output does not change.
  - Analogy given: cooking — you need both labour _and_ ingredients; extra labour alone with no ingredients produces nothing more.
- **Where are the olives?** Raw material (olives) is not shown separately because its requirement is a "natural linear progression" — more oil obviously needs more olives, proportionally. The non-obvious part is the machine/worker/energy proportion, which is why the model focuses on those.

### 2.5 Constant returns to scale (CRS)

- **Definition:** If all inputs are increased by the same proportion **λ**, and output also increases by exactly that proportion **λ**, the technology exhibits **constant returns to scale**.
  - Formally: $f(\lambda M, \lambda N, \lambda E) = \lambda\, f(M,N,E)$
  - Mathematically: the production function is **homogeneous of degree one**.
- Worked illustration: workers 1→2, energy 80→160, machines 3→6 ⇒ output 50→100. ✔ CRS.
- Other cases (mentioned, to be covered later in the course under firm behaviour):
  - **Increasing returns to scale** — scaling inputs by λ gives _more_ than λ times output.
  - **Decreasing returns to scale** — scaling inputs by λ gives _less_ than λ times output.
- For this session, only **CRS** is used.

### 2.6 A second (robotic) technology

- **Robotic technology:** **1 worker + 1 robot + 400 kWh of energy → 100 litres of olive oil per day.**
- Also assumed to be a **fixed-proportions** technology (cannot increase energy without another worker; one worker can only handle a robot using up to 400 kWh).
- Also **CRS**: daily output is proportional to the number of robotic systems installed.
- Question posed: **is the robotic system better or worse?** Not straightforward — output is comparable but the input mix differs (much more energy, much less labour).

### 2.7 Comparing technologies: Average product and input ratios

**Average product of an input:**
$$AP = \frac{\text{Total output}}{\text{Quantity of that input used}}$$

**Average product of labour:**
$$AP_L = \frac{Y}{L}$$

- Also called **labour productivity** — how much output is produced per unit of labour.

**Energy–labour ratio** = ratio of the two inputs, E/L — captures **energy intensity** (units of energy per unit of labour).

**Worked comparison (numbers used in class):**

|                         | Technology A (original) | Technology B (robotic) |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Workers                 | 2                       | 1                      |
| Energy (kWh)            | 160                     | 400                    |
| Output (litres)         | 100                     | 100                    |
| **AP of labour**        | 100/2 = **50**          | 100/1 = **100**        |
| **Energy–labour ratio** | 160/2 = **80**          | 400/1 = **400**        |

- ⇒ AP of labour in the robotic technology is **twice** that of the original technology.
- ⇒ The robotic technology's energy–labour ratio is **exactly 5 times** that of the original ⇒ it is **very energy-intensive**.
- (Note: machines are "in the background" for technology A and are simply not tabulated here.)

**Key property under fixed proportions + CRS:** the **average product of labour is constant** for a given technology — it does not change with the level of output or the quantity of inputs used. It _does_ differ **between** technologies A and B.

### 2.8 Graph 1 — Rays from the origin (technology representation)

- **Axes:** x-axis = **number of workers**; y-axis = **energy**.
- The "third dimension" — output (olive oil) — is shown by **labelling points along the line**.
- Each technology is drawn as a **ray from the origin** (a straight line through the origin):
  - **Red ray = robotic technology (steeper)**
  - **Blue ray = original technology (flatter)**
- **Slope of the ray = energy–labour ratio = energy intensity.**
  - The original technology's ray is _flatter_ because its E/L ratio is only 1/5 of the robotic technology's.
  - **The steeper the ray, the more energy-intensive the technology.**
- Points on the robotic ray: Y = 100 uses 1 worker + 400 energy; Y = 200 uses 2 workers + 800 energy; and so on — linear scaling, which is **only possible because the technology is fixed-proportions with CRS**. The ray is the locus of all input combinations as you linearly scale up both inputs.

### 2.9 The trade-off and the role of relative prices

- Technology B has **higher labour productivity but uses far more energy** → there is a **trade-off**: more workers in one technology, more energy in the other.
- **Managerial implication:** the choice depends on the **relative cost of the two inputs**.
  - If labour is **cheap** relative to energy → choose the original (labour-intensive) technology A.
  - If labour is **costly** relative to energy (energy cheap) → choose the robotic technology.
- ⚠️ **The important word is _relative_, not absolute.** If both input prices rise in the same proportion so that the _ratio_ of costs is unchanged, there is **no reason to change your technology choice**.

### 2.10 Graph 2 — Five cloth-production technologies (A–E)

- **Output held fixed at 100 metres of cloth** for every technology.
- **Axes:** x-axis = **number of workers**; y-axis = **tons of coal** (energy measured in tons of coal).
- Technologies given in class:

| Technology | Workers                               | Tons of coal | Character                 |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------- | ------------ | ------------------------- |
| **A**      | 1                                     | 6            | most **energy-intensive** |
| **B**      | 4                                     | 2            |                           |
| **C**      | (more workers _and_ more coal than A) |              | **dominated by A**        |
| **D**      | (more workers and more coal than B)   |              | **dominated by B**        |
| **E**      | 10                                    | 1            | most **labour-intensive** |

- Imagine a **ray from the origin to each point** — five rays. The slope of each ray gives the **coal–labour (energy) intensity**.
  - **Flatter ray ⇒ less energy-intensive ⇒ more labour-intensive.**
  - A and E are the two extremes: A energy-intensive, E labour-intensive; B and the others lie in between.

### 2.11 Dominated (engineering-inefficient) technologies

- **Key idea:** Some technologies can be eliminated **purely on engineering grounds — without knowing any input prices.**
- **Definition:** Technology X **dominates** technology Y if X produces the same output using **less of both inputs**.
- Worked reasoning from class:
  - **A dominates C**: both produce 100 m of cloth, but C uses **more workers _and_ more coal** than A ⇒ C is inefficient ⇒ discard C.
  - **B dominates D**: B uses fewer workers _and_ less coal than D ⇒ discard D.
- **Graphical rule of thumb (the "quadrant test"):** At the point representing a technology, draw a **vertical line and a horizontal line**. Any point lying to the **north-east** of that point (i.e. in "quadrant I" formed by those lines) uses **more of both inputs** and is therefore a **dominated technology**.
- **What remains: A, B, E** — these **cannot be ranked** without price information, because moving from A to B raises workers but lowers coal; similarly for E. Ranking them requires knowing the **cost of each input**.
- **Student Q&A (scattered data):** If there are many scattered points, the graphical approach becomes impractical; you would write a simple program with if-conditions to identify dominated points.

### 2.12 Labour-saving technological change

- Construct the **coal–labour ratio** for each technology. E has a **very low** coal–labour ratio (high labour–coal ratio); moving toward A makes the technology more energy-intensive.
- If an economy currently uses **E** and shifts to **B**, it produces the same cloth with **less labour** ⇒ this is the adoption of a **labour-saving technology**.

### 2.13 The motivating question

> **Why did the Industrial Revolution happen in 18th-century England?**

- Many alternative explanations exist; this course examines the **technology-choice / relative-input-price** explanation.

### 2.14 Profit, cost, and the cost equation

- The firm chooses the technology that gives **maximum profit**.

$$\text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost}$$

- Since all remaining technologies produce the **same output (100 m of cloth)**, **revenue is identical** ⇒ differences in profit come **entirely from cost**.
- After eliminating engineering-dominated technologies, we must rely on **economic cost, i.e. prices**.

**The cost equation (two inputs):**
$$C = wL + pR$$
where

- **w** = wage (price of a worker)
- **L** = number of workers
- **p** = price of one ton of coal
- **R** = number of tons of coal

### 2.15 Deriving the isocost line (board derivation)

Write the cost equation in the general linear form $ax + by = c$, with $x = L$, $y = R$, $a = w$, $b = p$, $c = C$.

**Intercepts:**

- Set **L = 0** ⇒ $pR = C$ ⇒ **R-intercept (vertical) = C/p**
- Set **R = 0** ⇒ $wL = C$ ⇒ **L-intercept (horizontal) = C/w**

**Slope:**

- General: slope $= -a/b$
- Here: $\text{slope} = -\frac{w}{p}$
- (Equivalently, rearranging to y = mx + c form: $R = \dfrac{C}{p} - \dfrac{w}{p}L$; or in intercept form $\dfrac{L}{C/w} + \dfrac{R}{C/p} = 1$.)

**Definition — isocost line:** The locus of all input combinations (L, R) that cost **the same** total amount. "Iso" = same. Along an isocost line, you change the input mix while **holding total cost constant**.

**Graph description:** x-axis = workers (L); y-axis = tons of coal (R). The isocost line is a downward-sloping straight line with vertical intercept C/p, horizontal intercept C/w, and slope −w/p.

### 2.16 Shifts of the isocost line

- Consider a point **A = (L_A, R_A)** lying **above** the current isocost line. Since it does not lie on the line, it must cost **more**.
- To accommodate A while **holding relative prices constant** (slope still −w/p), you must draw a **parallel line** further out, with intercepts **C′/p** and **C′/w**. Since the slope and prices are unchanged, the **only** thing that can differ is the **numerator C** ⇒ **C′ > C**. This is a **geometric proof that cost has risen**.
- Similarly, a point **B = (L_B, R_B)** using **less of both inputs** lies on a **parallel line shifted inward** ⇒ **lower cost**.
- **Summary rule:**
  - **Along** an isocost line → cost is the same.
  - **Parallel shift outward / to the right** → **higher** cost.
  - **Parallel shift inward / to the left** → **lower** cost.

### 2.17 KEY RESULT — proportional change in both input prices

Suppose **both** input prices are scaled by the same factor (illustrated with factor 2):

- $w' = 2w$, $p' = 2p$

Then:

- **Slope** $= -w'/p' = -2w/2p = -w/p$ → **unchanged**.
- New cost of the _same_ input bundle: $C' = p'R + w'L = 2pR + 2wL = 2(pR + wL) = 2C$.
- New intercepts:
  - Vertical: $C'/p' = 2C/2p = C/p$ → **unchanged**
  - Horizontal: $C'/w' = 2C/2w = C/w$ → **unchanged**
- ⇒ **You end up on exactly the same isocost line as before.**

**Generalisation:** For **any λ > 0**, if both input prices are multiplied by λ, the slope and intercepts are unchanged and you remain on the same (drawn) isocost line.

**Economic meaning (emphasised strongly):**

> **In economics, what matters is _relative_ prices.** If both input prices rise in the same proportion, the manager's _choice between technologies/inputs_ is unaffected. Labour has not become more expensive than energy.

- **Student clarification accepted by instructor:** The _line_ is geometrically identical, but the cost level it represents is **higher** (e.g. cost 10 → cost 20). So the firm may decide to **shut shop** altogether (a different decision), but it will **not prefer one input over the other** differently.

### 2.18 Change in _relative_ prices — rotation of the isocost line

- Now suppose **only the wage doubles** ($w' = 2w$), while the price of coal is unchanged ($p' = p$).
- New slope $= -w'/p' = -2w/p$ ⇒ the isocost line becomes **twice as steep**.
- **Vertical intercept is unchanged** at **C/p** (because setting L = 0 removes w from the equation entirely).
- **Horizontal intercept falls** from C/w to **C/w′ = C/2w**.
- ⇒ The isocost line **rotates/pivots about the vertical intercept and becomes steeper**.
- ⇒ **This is the mechanism by which a change in relative input prices changes the firm's choice of technology.** Labour became costly ⇒ shift away from labour-intensive technologies.

_(Note: at one point in class the instructor mislabelled which variable sat on which axis on the board; the corrected convention is: **L on the x-axis, R (tons of coal) on the y-axis**, with vertical intercept C/p and horizontal intercept C/w.)_

### 2.19 Worked numerical example — isocost lines with actual prices

**Assumed prices:** wage **w = £10** per worker; price of coal **p = £20** per ton.

| Point  | Workers | Tons of coal | Cost calculation              | Total cost |
| ------ | ------- | ------------ | ----------------------------- | ---------- |
| **P1** | 2       | 3            | (2 × 10) + (3 × 20) = 20 + 60 | **£80**    |
| **P2** | 6       | 1            | (6 × 10) + (1 × 20) = 60 + 20 | **£80**    |

- P1 and P2 lie on the **same isocost line** (both cost £80) — by definition of an isocost line.
- Points **Q1, Q2** use **more of both** workers and coal ⇒ they lie on a **higher, parallel** isocost line ⇒ higher cost. (More inputs also means more output is produced with that technology — but note: **the isocost graph shows only cost, not output**; output is shown on the technology graph.)
- Slope with these prices: $-w/p = -10/20 = -1/2$.

### 2.20 Worked example — the price change that drives the Industrial Revolution

**Price change:** wage stays at **w = £10**; price of coal **falls from £20 to £5**.

- Slope changes from **1/2 to 2** (in absolute value) ⇒ **isocost line becomes steeper**.
- Old isocost line labelled **JH**; new isocost line labelled **GF**.

**Cost of technology A (1 worker, 6 tons of coal) — all technologies produce 100 m of cloth:**

| Prices               | Cost of A                                 |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| w = 10, p = 20 (old) | (1 × 10) + (6 × 20) = 10 + 120 = **£130** |
| w = 10, p = 5 (new)  | (1 × 10) + (6 × 5) = 10 + 30 = **£40**    |

- Under the **new** prices, **A costs £40**, and technologies **B and E cost MORE than £40** ⇒ **A is now the cheapest.**
- Under the **old** prices (line JH, slope 1/2), **B was cheaper than A** — A lay _above_ the JH isocost line (reachable only by a parallel outward shift ⇒ higher cost).
- After the price change (line GF, slope 2), **B lies to the right of the new isocost line** through A ⇒ **A is now cheaper than B**.

**Conclusion:** The change in **relative** input prices flipped the cost-minimising choice from the labour-intensive technology **B** to the energy-intensive technology **A**.

### 2.21 Application: the British Industrial Revolution

- "To put a very long story into one crude graph": the economy moved from isocost line **JH to GF** — i.e. **wages rose relative to the price of coal** (equivalently, coal became cheap relative to labour).
- This **incentivised firms to move from labour-intensive to energy-intensive technologies.**
- Because revenue is unchanged (same 100 m of cloth) and cost falls, **profit rises**.

### 2.22 Innovation rent

- **Innovation rent** = the extra profit earned from adopting the new, cost-reducing technology, over and above the profit from the **next best option** (the current/old technology).
  - Recall the general definition of **rent**: the extra you get over your **next best option**.
- $$\text{Innovation rent} = \text{Profit with new technology} - \text{Profit with existing technology}$$
- A firm that switches **first** to the new cost-minimising technology gains an **advantage over its competitors**.
- Innovation can operate on **either side**: reducing cost for the same output, **or** increasing output for the same cost — these are equivalent.

### 2.23 Entrepreneurship and Schumpeter's Creative Destruction

- The **first adopter** of a new technology is the **entrepreneur** — an **entrepreneurial firm** is willing to experiment with new technologies and start new businesses.
- **Incentive to experiment:** not every experiment succeeds, but a successful cost-reducing innovation yields **innovation rents (higher profits)**.
- **Joseph Schumpeter** (named economist): firms continually innovate **in search of these rents**, and this raises the **productivity of the entire economy** year on year.
- **Creative destruction — definition:** the process by which **old technologies, and the firms that do not adapt, are swept away by the new, because they cannot compete in the market.**
  - Mechanism: the innovating firm starts **dominating the market**, **cuts its prices**, and (via better design/consumer appeal) draws consumers away; firms on the old technology lag, lose business, and exit.
  - "That's the nature of capitalism" — firms are in a **constant cycle of creation and destruction**.

### 2.24 Historical background (general interest)

- **Textiles** was one of the first sectors to undergo technological change in the Industrial Revolution.
- Before the revolution, cloth-making was done by **household members**; **spinning** was a key stage done by hand, predominantly by **women** — the English word **"spinster"** originally denotes this **job**. Hand spinning was very time-consuming.
- Change came with machines powered first by **water wheels**, later by **steam engines** — i.e. **non-human (and non-renewable) energy** replaced human energy in spinning.
- **Why England first (and not France)?** A graph was shown of **wages relative to the cost of capital/energy** across countries: **England had higher wages** (relative to the price of coal/capital) than France. Therefore **English firms had a stronger incentive to adopt labour-saving technologies** — exactly the mechanism of the isocost model above.

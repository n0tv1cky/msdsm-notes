# DSM-107 Managerial Economics — Session L08
## Game Theory II: Invisible Hand vs. Prisoners' Dilemma, External Effects, and Pareto Criteria

---

## 1. Overview

This session continues the game-theory module begun in the previous class. It starts with a recap of **Nash equilibrium** using the two-player, two-strategy Anil–Bala crop-choice game (cassava vs. rice), and introduces the concept of a **dominant strategy** and how it can be used to solve a game quickly. That first game is shown to be an "**invisible hand**" game (Adam Smith, *Wealth of Nations*, 1776): pursuit of pure self-interest leads to the cell with the highest individual *and* highest total payoffs. A second game — the **pest-control game** (Integrated Pest Control vs. the chemical "Terminator"/toxic-tide) — is then solved; both players have a dominant strategy, giving a **dominant strategy equilibrium**, but it is the cell with the *lowest* payoffs. This is the **Prisoners' Dilemma**, a counter-example to the invisible hand, driven by **external effects** (players ignore the costs their actions impose on others) and by mutual mistrust / the incentive to cheat. The class then moves from *predicting* outcomes to *evaluating* them, introducing **allocations**, the **Pareto criterion**, **Pareto improvement** and **Pareto efficiency**, with a scatter-plot analysis of the four pest-control allocations. Finally it covers ways to escape the bad equilibrium: **altruism, reciprocity, repeated interaction, taxes/punishment, institutions and agreements**, with evidence from **lab and field experiments**, and shows how **altruistic preferences change the shape of indifference curves** and can make cooperation the equilibrium.

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order Taught

### 2.1 Recap: Nash Equilibrium
- **Definition:** A Nash equilibrium is *a set of strategies, one per player, such that each player's strategy is the best response to the strategies chosen by everyone else.*
- Equivalent statement: at a Nash equilibrium **no player has an incentive to deviate unilaterally**.
- Method for a 2×2 game: take each strategy of Player 1 in turn, find Player 2's best response; then take each strategy of Player 2, find Player 1's best response; a cell with **two ticks** (both best responses) is the Nash equilibrium.

### 2.2 Game 1 — The Crop Game (Anil & Bala: Cassava or Rice)
Setting: two farmers, Anil and Bala, each choose which crop to sow — **cassava** or **rice**. Payoffs depend on both choices.
Convention used: **(Anil's payoff, Bala's payoff)**; Anil = rows, Bala = columns.

Numbers actually read out in class:

| Anil ↓ / Bala → | **Rice** | **Cassava** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rice** | (4, 4) | ( — , 3) |
| **Cassava** | (**6, 6**) ← NE | ( — , 2) |

*(Bala's numbers 4, 3, 6, 2 and Anil's first-column numbers 4 and 6 were stated explicitly; Anil's second-column payoffs were not read out, only that they are lower than 6.)*

**Working through the equilibrium:**
- If Anil plays **cassava** → Bala's best response is **rice** (6 > 2).
- If Bala plays **rice** → Anil's best response is **cassava** (6 > 4).
- ⇒ **(Cassava, Rice)** is a Nash equilibrium.
- Why the others fail:
  - **(Rice, Rice):** if Anil plays rice, Bala's best response is rice (4 > 3)… but if Bala plays rice, Anil prefers cassava (6 > 4) → not stable.
  - **(Rice, Cassava):** if Anil plays rice, Bala would deviate to rice (4 > 3) → not stable.
  - **(Cassava, Cassava):** if Anil plays cassava, Bala gets a higher payoff from rice (6 > 2) → not stable.
- Only **one** (pure-strategy) equilibrium exists in this game.

**Dominant strategy**
- **Definition:** a strategy is a **dominant strategy** if it is the best response to *all* possible strategies of the other player(s).
- Here, **Bala plays rice regardless of what Anil does** (4 > 3 and 6 > 2) ⇒ **rice is Bala's dominant strategy**.
- **Short-cut logic:** Anil can see the same matrix. He knows Bala will surely play rice, so he only needs to look at the **first column** and choose between 4 (rice) and 6 (cassava) → he picks **cassava**. He never needs to examine the second column.

### 2.3 The Invisible Hand
- In this game the equilibrium cell (Cassava, Rice) has:
  - the **highest total payoff** (sum of both players' payoffs), and
  - the **highest individual payoff** for *each* player, compared to any other cell.
- **Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations* (1776)** — the idea of the **invisible hand**: if people are left free to pursue their own self-interest, without an authority directing them, the resulting social outcome can be the best for everybody.
- Neither Anil nor Bala acts out of altruism or revenge — each simply maximises his own payoff — yet they land on the best cell for both. This is a **social situation where the pursuit of self-interest is good**.
- Extension to **capitalism**: firms pursuing profit end up inventing technologies and processes that raise welfare; consumers and firms each pursue self-interest and all end up with higher incomes. No charity is involved on either side.
- **Caveat:** this is only *one class* of social situations. In others, the pursuit of self-interest is *not* good — the **social dilemma**.

### 2.4 Note on Multiple and Mixed-Strategy Equilibria
- A game may have **more than one equilibrium**.
- Some equilibria are **mixed strategy equilibria**: a player plays one strategy with some probability and another with another probability, so the outcome is probabilistic, not certain.
- **Scope statement:** *this course will only deal with **pure strategy** equilibria* — no mixed-strategy games.

### 2.5 Game 2 — The Pest-Control Game (Prisoners' Dilemma)
**Setting:** Anil and Bala have **adjacent fields** suffering a **pest attack**. Two options:
1. **Terminator / "toxic tide"** — a cheap, quick, very powerful chemical pesticide. It kills the pests but **risks contaminating groundwater** (seeps into the land).
2. **Integrated Pest Control (IPC)** — grow **beneficial insects** that eat the harmful pests. Natural, no external chemicals, sustainable in the long run, but **slower and more expensive** than buying pesticide.

**Qualitative outcomes:**
- **Both IPC:** pests eliminated naturally, **no water contamination** — best outcome.
- **One IPC, other Terminator:** the chemical user is effectively **free-riding**. The IPC user suffers because (a) pests migrate to his field (it has no strong chemical), (b) contaminated water reaches his fields, (c) some of his *beneficial* insects are killed by the neighbour's pesticide.
- **Both Terminator:** all pests die, but there is **heavy water contamination requiring costly filtration** → costs much higher than both using IPC.

**Payoff matrix (Anil's payoff, Bala's payoff); Anil = rows, Bala = columns:**

| Anil ↓ / Bala → | **IPC** | **Terminator** |
|---|---|---|
| **IPC** | (3, 3) | (1, 4) |
| **Terminator** | (4, 1) | (**2, 2**) ← NE |

Totals per cell: (IPC,IPC) = **6**; (IPC,T) = 5; (T,IPC) = 5; (T,T) = **4** (the lowest).

**Solving the game (board work):**
- Bala's best responses:
  - Anil plays IPC → Bala: IPC gives 3, Terminator gives 4 → **Terminator**.
  - Anil plays Terminator → Bala: IPC gives 1, Terminator gives 2 → **Terminator**.
  - ⇒ **Terminator is Bala's dominant strategy.**
- Anil's best responses:
  - Bala plays IPC → Anil: IPC gives 3, Terminator gives 4 → **Terminator**.
  - Bala plays Terminator → Anil: IPC gives 1, Terminator gives 2 → **Terminator**.
  - ⇒ **Terminator is also Anil's dominant strategy.**
- The cell with **two ticks** is **(Terminator, Terminator) = (2, 2)** → the Nash equilibrium.
- Because *both* players are playing dominant strategies, this is called a **dominant strategy equilibrium**.

**The dilemma:**
- **(IPC, IPC) = (3,3)** gives *both* players a higher payoff than (2,2), and a higher total (6 > 4) — yet it is **not** an equilibrium.
- **Reason — the incentive to cheat:** if Anil sticks to IPC, Bala gets **4 by defecting to Terminator** instead of 3 by keeping his promise. So a promise to play IPC is not credible.
- Anticipating this, Anil will not play IPC either (he would be left with 1). He plays Terminator to "salvage" 2.
- **Mistrust alone is enough:** even if both are honest by nature, if neither trusts the other, both end up at (Terminator, Terminator).
- **Definition — Prisoners' Dilemma:** a game with a **dominant strategy equilibrium in which playing the dominant strategy yields both lower individual payoffs and a lower total payoff** than another available outcome; the **socially optimal outcome is not achieved**.
- Illustration used: in the pesticide example both farmers end up using the more harmful pest-control method.
- **Reference in reading material:** a YouTube clip of the TV show **"Golden Balls"** — a real-life prisoners'-dilemma experiment showing how people actually behave.

**Q&A — can payoffs be negative?**
- Yes. The original Prisoners' Dilemma expresses payoffs as **years spent in jail**, so a larger number is worse — a negative payoff.
- When comparing negative payoffs, **use the value including the sign, not the absolute value**: **−3 is better than −4**.

### 2.6 External Effects (Externalities)
- Why the bad outcome is predicted: **each player only considers his own payoff** and ignores the effect of his action on the other person.
- Worked illustration from the matrix: when Anil switches from IPC to Terminator (while Bala plays IPC), **Anil's payoff rises by 1 (3 → 4)** but **Bala's payoff falls by 2 (3 → 1)**. Anil's private benefit (+1) is **smaller than the cost imposed on Bala (−2)**.
- **Definition — external effects:** benefits or costs of a person's actions that fall on *others* and that the decision-maker does **not** take into account. They are "external to our world view." They may be **negative** (pollution, littering) **or positive**.
- Policy question: how do we **internalise** external effects — i.e., make people take others' costs and benefits into account?

**Ways to internalise / sustain cooperation:**
1. **Social preferences** — morality, civic sense, altruism inculcated from childhood, via schooling, religion, norms. Example: pandemic advisories to wear a face mask and cover your mouth when coughing because others can be affected.
2. **Taxes / punishment** — individual punishment is hard, but the **state can tax** consumption that is harmful to society, making the person pay for the consequences of their action.
3. **Repeated interaction, social norms and peer punishment** — e.g., a neighbour who keeps littering: you withdraw cooperation, confront them, or retaliate. Repeated meetings make people "think twice before cheating" ("if I cheat today, how do I show my face tomorrow?").
4. **Institutions / organisations that let players coordinate in advance** — classic example: **irrigation and groundwater in a drought**. If one or two farmers over-extract groundwater, water tables fall or the aquifer dries up permanently. A **village council agreement** can cap how long a pump may be used, or cap the **maximum horsepower** of the pump.
5. Simply **letting the players talk to each other / meet often** can nudge them to (IPC, IPC).
- Real-world analogues mentioned: the **Montreal Protocol** (international agreement to control ozone-layer depletion), **village-level water-sharing agreements**.

**Anecdote:** In the film *A Beautiful Mind*, Russell Crowe (playing **John Nash**) shows his Princeton adviser a result, and the professor says *"you disproved Adam Smith."* The prisoners' dilemma is exactly that: the invisible-hand logic **does not apply every time**.

### 2.7 Evaluating Outcomes: Allocations and the Pareto Criterion
- Shift from **predicting** outcomes (finding the Nash equilibrium) to **evaluating** them (a value judgement: is one outcome better than another?).
- **Allocation:** the outcome of an interaction — *who gets how much* — i.e., the distribution among players of payoffs (or anything they value). **Each cell of a game is an allocation.**
- **Pareto criterion** (named after **Vilfredo Pareto**, Italian engineer-turned-economist):
  > **Allocation A is better than allocation B if at least one party is strictly better off with A than with B, and nobody is worse off.**
- If A satisfies this relative to B, we say **A Pareto-dominates B**, or **A is a Pareto improvement over B**.
- Note: it is fine if some players' situations are unchanged, as long as **at least one is strictly better off and none is worse off**.

**Worked example — the four pest-control allocations on a scatter plot**
- **Graph described:** x-axis = **Anil's payoff**, y-axis = **Bala's payoff**. Four points plotted:
  - (I, T) = **(1, 4)**
  - (T, T) = **(2, 2)**
  - (I, I) = **(3, 3)**
  - (T, I) = **(4, 1)**
- **(I, I) = (3,3) Pareto-dominates (T, T) = (2,2)**: moving from (2,2) to (3,3) makes **Anil better off (2→3) and Bala better off (2→3)** — a Pareto improvement.
- **General construction:** take any allocation (say (T,T) at (2,2)) and draw the **north-east quadrant** from that point (shaded region). Everything in that quadrant is a Pareto improvement over it:
  - moving **right** along the horizontal boundary raises Anil's payoff without hurting Bala;
  - moving **up** along the vertical boundary raises Bala's payoff without hurting Anil;
  - anywhere strictly inside the quadrant raises **both**.
  - (I, I) = (3,3) lies in this quadrant, hence the Pareto improvement.
- **No other Pareto comparisons are possible among these four:**
  - (I,T) = (1,4) → (I,I) = (3,3): Anil better off (1→3) but **Bala worse off (4→3)** → not a Pareto improvement.
  - (T,I) = (4,1) → (I,I) = (3,3): Bala better off but **Anil worse off (4→3)** → not a Pareto improvement.
  - Same for (I,T) vs (T,I), (T,T) vs (I,T), (T,T) vs (T,I) — none can be ranked.

**Pareto efficiency**
- **Definition:** an allocation is **Pareto efficient** if it is **not Pareto-dominated by any other allocation** — i.e., there is no alternative allocation in which at least one party would be better off and nobody worse off.
- In the pest-control game the **three Pareto-efficient allocations** are:
  - **(I, T) = (1,4)**, **(I, I) = (3,3)**, **(T, I) = (4,1)**.
- **(T, T) = (2,2) is Pareto inefficient** (it is dominated by (I,I)) — even though it is the Nash equilibrium.
- Q&A extension: if a hypothetical fifth allocation **(4,4)** existed, it would be a **Pareto improvement over all four** existing allocations.

**Summary points stated explicitly:**
1. **A dominant strategy equilibrium is not necessarily Pareto efficient.** (Pest-control game: equilibrium (2,2) is dominated by (3,3).) Contrast with the rice/cassava invisible-hand game, whose equilibrium *was* Pareto efficient.
2. **A Pareto-efficient outcome is not necessarily an equilibrium outcome.** (3,3), (1,4) and (4,1) are all Pareto efficient but none of them except the NE is an equilibrium — in fact none of the three is the equilibrium.
3. **The Pareto criterion does not tell us which Pareto-efficient allocation is best.** It only gives a partial ranking; many pairs simply cannot be compared.
4. **A Pareto-efficient allocation is not always "better" than a Pareto-inefficient one.** (T,I) is efficient and (T,T) is inefficient, yet you **cannot say (T,I) Pareto-dominates (T,T)**. (T,T) is only labelled inefficient because *some* allocation — namely (I,I) — dominates it.

### 2.8 Preferences and Experimental Evidence
Three preference types:
- **Altruism:** doing good with **no expectation** of return.
- **Reciprocity (reciprocal preferences):** doing good **in the anticipation that good behaviour will be rewarded**; and repaying cheating "in the same measure."
- **Self-interest:** caring **only** about one's own payoff; neither kind nor vengeful — this is what the standard games assume.

**How these preferences are revealed — experiments:**
- **Lab experiments** (typically in a university setting): students are given situations and asked to choose; there is often a **real monetary reward** for truthfully revealing preferences; there are no "correct" answers. Advantages: you can create **control and treatment groups**, run **multiple iterations**, **control for other variables**, and **results can be replicated** by other researchers.
- **Field experiments:** an experiment run in a **real-world setting rather than a lab**; a real-world situation is exposed to a particular initiative. Motivation: **lab results may not predict real-world decision making**.
- **Observational (secondary) data:** not experimental, but can be analysed in a **quasi-experimental** way using economists' **causal inference methods**, which mimic an experiment with non-experimental data. (Covered further in econometrics courses; flagged as a useful tool for data scientists.)

**Key experimental statistics (quoted):**
- In a **one-shot (or two-shot) two-player prisoners' dilemma**, about **20% of players choose to cooperate** (here, cooperate = choose IPC) — taken as **evidence of altruism**.
- When the game is **repeated against the same opponent** (opponent's identity kept anonymous, but known to be the same person from past rounds, so past behaviour is remembered), the **likelihood of cooperation rises to about 50%**.
- Q&A: is generosity just because subjects know they are being observed ("warm glow", looking good)? Answer: many experiments **anonymise the chooser and randomise identities**, and altruistic behaviour still appears; the reading material cites studies controlling for such confounders. Still, it is hard to fully separate true altruism from wanting to look good.

### 2.9 Altruistic Preferences and Indifference Curves
**Graph described:** x-axis = **Anil's payoff**, y-axis = **Bala's payoff**.
- **Purely self-interested Anil:** his indifference curves are **vertical straight lines** (x = 1, x = 2, x = 3, …). Bala's payoff is irrelevant, so along a vertical line he is indifferent. Moving **rightward** to a higher vertical line makes him better off.
- **Altruistic Anil:** his indifference curves become **downward-sloping lines** — Bala's payoff now enters his preferences. Implications with the game's numbers:
  - He **prefers (1, 4) over (2, 2)** — he would rather have 1 for himself if Bala gets 4, than 2 each.
  - He **prefers (3, 3) over (4, 1)** — he will give up 1 unit of his own payoff so the other person can gain 2.
- Consequence: an altruistic Anil **prefers (I,I) over (T,T), over (I,T) and over (T,I)** → **IPC becomes his preferred/dominant choice** in every case.
- If **Bala's preferences are also altruistic**, IPC becomes his dominant strategy too.
- ⇒ **(IPC, IPC) becomes the equilibrium** — the socially optimal, Pareto-efficient outcome is sustained.
- Real-world parallels: **Montreal Protocol** (nations agreeing to limit ozone depletion), **village-level water-sharing agreements** — agreeing to cooperate because the impact of one's consumption on others is taken into account.

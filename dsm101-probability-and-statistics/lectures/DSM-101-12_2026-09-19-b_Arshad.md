## 1. Overview

This session finishes the worked-example treatment of the **normal distribution** (recovering unknown parameters $\mu,\sigma$ from given probability statements using the standard normal CDF and tables; identifying a distribution from its **moment generating function**; computing a probability for a **sum of a continuous and a discrete random variable** via the law of total probability plus independence). It then steps back to describe the general **statistical modelling workflow** (plot data → propose models → estimate parameters → goodness-of-fit test → model comparison → select best model). The second half introduces **probability inequalities used when the distribution is unknown**: the **Markov inequality** (with full derivation), its **generalized form**, the **Chebyshev inequality** (derived as a special case, including the $k\sigma$ version and its "at least 75% within 2 SD" interpretation), several numerical examples showing that these bounds are often *not* sharp, and finally **convex/concave functions and Jensen's inequality**, used to prove $\operatorname{Var}(X)\ge 0$ and to bound $E[\log X]$.

## 2. Topics and concepts, in order taught

### 2.1 Recap tools used throughout
- Standardisation: if $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ then
$$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)$$
- CDF of the standard normal variate (SNV) written $\Phi(z)$ (lecturer writes it as $F(z)$ or "$\Phi$"):
  - $\Phi(0)=\tfrac12$
  - $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$, equivalently $\Phi(z)+\Phi(-z)=1$
  - $\Phi$ is **strictly monotone**, so $\Phi(a)=\Phi(b)\Rightarrow a=b$ (this is what licenses "comparing arguments").

---

### 2.2 Example 1 — Recovering $\mu$ when $\sigma^2$ is known

**Given:** $X\sim N(\mu,1)$, $\mu$ unknown, and $P(X\le 2)=\tfrac12$. Find $\mu$.

$$P\!\left(\frac{X-\mu}{1}\le \frac{2-\mu}{1}\right)=\frac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; P(Z\le 2-\mu)=\frac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; \Phi(2-\mu)=\frac12=\Phi(0)$$

By monotonicity of $\Phi$: $2-\mu=0$, hence

$$\boxed{\mu=2}$$

---

### 2.3 Example 2 — Both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ unknown; redundant conditions

**Given:** $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, with
$$P(X\le 4)=\tfrac12 \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(X\ge 4)=\tfrac12 .$$

*Condition 1:*
$$P\!\left(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\le\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac12\Rightarrow \Phi\!\left(\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\Phi(0)\Rightarrow \frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}=0$$

*Condition 2:*
$$P\!\left(Z\ge\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac12 \Rightarrow 1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac12 \Rightarrow \Phi\!\left(\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\frac12=\Phi(0)\Rightarrow \frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}=0$$

**Conclusion:** the two conditions are **redundant** (they give the same equation). We only get
$$\mu=4,\qquad \sigma>0 \text{ arbitrary}$$
— one equation, two unknowns; $\sigma$ is not identified because the statement is just the symmetry of the normal about its mean.

**Modified version:** keep $P(X\le 4)=\tfrac12$ but change the second to $P(X\ge4)=\tfrac14$.

- From the first: $\dfrac{4-\mu}{\sigma}=0$.
- From the second: $1-\Phi\!\left(\dfrac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\dfrac14 \Rightarrow \Phi\!\left(\dfrac{4-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=\dfrac34=0.75$.

**Reading the standard normal table backwards (important technique):** we look for the *probability* $0.75$ inside the body of the table. The table gives $\Phi(0.67)\approx 0.7486$ and $\Phi(0.68)\approx 0.7517$; $0.75$ lies between them, so by **linear interpolation** $z\approx 0.675$. Hence
$$\frac{4-\mu}{\sigma}=0.675\quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4-\mu=0.675\,\sigma .$$

But condition 1 forces $4-\mu=0$, which then forces $\sigma=0$. A zero variance gives a **degenerate distribution**, which is not admissible — so **this pair of probability statements is inconsistent** for a normal model. (Teaching point: different given probability setups may determine both parameters, only one, or be contradictory.)

---

### 2.4 Example 3 — Identifying a distribution from the MGF (uniqueness of MGF)

**Fact used:** if $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ then
$$M_X(t)=\exp\!\left(\mu t+\tfrac12\sigma^2 t^2\right),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.$$

**Given:** $M_X(t)=e^{5t+8t^2}$ for $t\in\mathbb{R}$. Identify the distribution.

Compare coefficients:
- coefficient of $t$: $\mu=5$
- coefficient of $t^2$: $\tfrac12\sigma^2=8\Rightarrow \sigma^2=16$

By **uniqueness of the MGF**:
$$\boxed{X\sim N(5,16)}$$

---

### 2.5 Example 4 — Mixture of a continuous and a discrete random variable

**Given:** $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ (continuous, support $\mathbb{R}$), $Y\sim \text{Binomial}(2,\tfrac12)$ (discrete, support $S_Y=\{0,1,2\}$), $X$ and $Y$ **independent**. Find $P(X+Y\le 1)$.

**Step 1 — Law of total probability** over the values of $Y$:
$$P(X+Y\le 1)=\sum_{y\in S_Y}P(X+Y\le 1,\;Y=y)$$
$$=P(X+0\le1,Y=0)+P(X+1\le1,Y=1)+P(X+2\le1,Y=2)$$
$$=P(X\le 1,Y=0)+P(X\le 0,Y=1)+P(X\le -1,Y=2)$$

**Step 2 — Independence** factorises each term:
$$=P(X\le 1)P(Y=0)+P(X\le 0)P(Y=1)+P(X\le -1)P(Y=2)$$

**Step 3 — Specialise** to $X\sim N(0,1)$ (so $P(X\le a)=\Phi(a)$) and $P(Y=y)=\binom{2}{y}\left(\tfrac12\right)^y\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2-y}$:

$$=\Phi(1)\binom{2}{0}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{0}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2}+\Phi(0)\binom{2}{1}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{1}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{1}+\Phi(-1)\binom{2}{2}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2}\!\left(\tfrac12\right)^{0}$$

$$=\Phi(1)\cdot\tfrac14+\tfrac12\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac14+\Phi(-1)\cdot\tfrac14
=\tfrac14\Big[\Phi(1)+\Phi(-1)+1\Big]$$

**Step 4 — Use $\Phi(1)+\Phi(-1)=1$** (from $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$):
$$=\tfrac14(1+1)=\tfrac14\cdot 2=\boxed{\tfrac12}$$

> Note: if $\mu,\sigma$ are not specified numerically, the answer must be left in terms of $\Phi\!\left(\frac{1-\mu}{\sigma}\right)$ etc., or evaluated from tables.

### 2.6 Digression — Why we study standard distributions (modelling workflow)

Besides the standard families (normal, binomial, Poisson, uniform, …) there are **non-standard** functions that can serve as a valid PMF/PDF. In data analysis:

1. **Plot the data** and inspect its shape.
2. **Propose candidate models** (standard and/or non-standard) whose PDF/PMF looks close to the empirical distribution.
3. **Estimate the unknown parameters** of each candidate.
4. **Validate** via a **goodness-of-fit test** — reject models that do not fit.
5. If several models survive (e.g. 3 out of 5 fit), use **model comparison criteria** to select the single best model.

### 2.7 Markov inequality

**Setup:** let $X$ be a **non-negative** random variable ($f_X(x)=0$ for $x<0$, $\int_0^\infty f_X(x)\,dx=1$).

**Derivation (as done on the board):**
$$E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx \qquad(\text{negative part contributes }0)$$
Split at any fixed $a>0$:
$$E[X]=\int_{0}^{a}x f_X(x)\,dx+\int_{a}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx \;\ge\; \int_{a}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx$$
(dropping a non-negative integral). On $[a,\infty)$ we have $x\ge a$, so $x f_X(x)\ge a f_X(x)$:
$$E[X]\;\ge\;\int_{a}^{\infty}a\,f_X(x)\,dx=a\int_{a}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=a\,P(X\ge a)$$

**Markov inequality:**
$$\boxed{P(X\ge a)\le \frac{E[X]}{a}\qquad \text{for all } a>0,\; X\ge 0}$$

**Interpretation:** gives an **upper bound on a tail probability using only the mean**. If the PDF/PMF is known you compute the probability exactly; the inequality is for when it is not known.

---

### 2.8 Generalized Markov inequality

Drop the non-negativity of $X$; instead put it on a function.

Let $X$ be **any** random variable (may take values on all of $\mathbb{R}$, e.g. normal), and let $g$ be a **non-negative function**. Then for any $a>0$:
$$\boxed{P\big(g(X)\ge a\big)\le \frac{E[g(X)]}{a}}\qquad\text{provided }E[g(X)]\text{ exists.}$$

*Proof idea:* set $Y=g(X)\ge 0$ and apply the ordinary Markov inequality to $Y$.

---

### 2.9 Chebyshev inequality (as a special case)

Take $g(x)=(x-\mu)^2$ in the generalized Markov inequality, where $\mu=E[X]$:
$$P\big((X-\mu)^2\ge a\big)\le \frac{E[(X-\mu)^2]}{a}=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{a}$$

Put $a=\varepsilon^2$ with $\varepsilon>0$, and note the equivalence of events
$$(X-\mu)^2\ge \varepsilon^2 \iff |X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon .$$

**Chebyshev inequality:**
$$\boxed{P\big(|X-\mu|\ge \varepsilon\big)\le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\varepsilon^{2}}}$$

**Complementary (lower-bound) form:**
$$\boxed{P\big(|X-\mu|<\varepsilon\big)\ge 1-\frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{\varepsilon^{2}}}$$

Both forms are called Chebyshev's inequality; one gives an **upper** bound, the other a **lower** bound. (Chebyshev proved it independently, but it follows immediately from generalized Markov.)

**$k$-sigma version:** put $\varepsilon=k\sigma$, $\sigma=\text{SD}(X)$:
$$P\big(|X-\mu|\ge k\sigma\big)\le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2}=\boxed{\frac{1}{k^{2}}}$$

**Numerical reading, $k=2$:**
$$P\big(|X-\mu|\ge 2\sigma\big)\le \tfrac14$$
i.e. the combined probability in the two tails beyond $\mu-2\sigma$ and $\mu+2\sigma$ is at most $25\%$, so **at least $75\%$ of the data lies in $(\mu-2\sigma,\ \mu+2\sigma)$ for *any* distribution** (skewed, non-symmetric, whatever).
Compare: for the **normal** distribution the exact figure within $\pm 2\sigma$ is $95.44\%$. So Chebyshev is universal but weaker.

### 2.10 Worked example — Factory production (Markov + Chebyshev)

**Given:** $X$ = number of items produced in a week, $E[X]=50$. No distribution is known.

**(a) What can be said about $P(\text{production exceeds }75)$?**
Since $X$ is a non-negative count, "exceeds 75" means $X\ge 76$. By Markov:
$$P(X\ge 76)\le \frac{E[X]}{76}=\frac{50}{76}\approx 0.6579$$
So the probability of exceeding 75 items is **at most about $0.658$** (an upper bound only).

**(b) Given additionally $\operatorname{Var}(X)=25$, bound $P(40\le X\le 60)$.**
Subtract the mean $\mu=50$:
$$P(40\le X\le 60)=P(-10\le X-\mu\le 10)=P\big(|X-\mu|\le 10\big)$$
By Chebyshev with $\varepsilon=10$:
$$P\big(|X-\mu|\le 10\big)\ge 1-\frac{25}{10^{2}}=1-\frac{25}{100}=\frac{3}{4}$$
So the weekly production lies between 40 and 60 with probability **at least $0.75$**.

### 2.11 Worked example — Uniform: bound vs. exact (bounds need not be sharp)

**Given:** $X\sim U(0,10)$. Bound and then compute $P(|X-5|>4)$.

Moments of $U(a,b)$:
$$E[X]=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{0+10}{2}=5,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}=\frac{100}{12}=\frac{25}{3}$$

**Chebyshev bound** with $\varepsilon=4$:
$$P(|X-5|>4)\le \frac{\operatorname{Var}(X)}{4^{2}}=\frac{25/3}{16}=\frac{25}{48}\approx 0.52$$

**Exact probability:**
$$P(|X-5|>4)=1-P(|X-5|\le 4)=1-P(-4\le X-5\le 4)=1-P(1\le X\le 9)$$
$$=1-\int_{1}^{9}\frac{1}{10}\,dx=1-\frac{1}{10}\big[x\big]_{1}^{9}=1-\frac{9-1}{10}=1-\frac{8}{10}=\frac{2}{10}=0.20$$

**Conclusion:** exact $=0.20$ vs. bound $=0.52$ — the Chebyshev bound here is **not sharp**. For some distributions (e.g. normal-type cases) the bound can be much closer. These inequalities are only for the situation of **very limited information** about the distribution.

### 2.12 Convex and concave functions

**Geometric definition:**
- $f$ is **convex** if, for any two points $A,B$ on the graph, the chord joining them lies **above** (or on) the graph — the chord never cuts through the curve. Such functions have a well-defined minimum, which is why convexity is central in optimisation (existence of the optimum is guaranteed).
- If joining two points produces a chord that **cuts** the curve, the function is **non-convex**.
- $f$ is **concave** if the chord lies **below** the graph.
- Relation: **$f$ convex $\iff$ $-f$ concave.**

**Analytic test (for twice-differentiable $f$):**
$$f''(x)\ge 0\ \ \forall x\in \text{domain}\;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ convex};\qquad f''(x)\le 0\ \ \forall x\in \text{domain}\;\Rightarrow\; f \text{ concave}.$$

**Examples worked:**
1. $f(x)=x^{2}$: $f'(x)=2x$, $f''(x)=2>0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$ ⇒ **convex on $\mathbb{R}$**.
2. $f(x)=e^{ax}$: $f'(x)=a e^{ax}$, $f''(x)=a^{2}e^{ax}>0$ (since $a^2>0$ and $e^{ax}>0$) ⇒ **convex on $\mathbb{R}$**.
3. $f(x)=\log x$, $x>0$: $f'(x)=\dfrac1x$, $f''(x)=-\dfrac{1}{x^{2}}<0$ ⇒ **concave** on $x>0$; consequently $-\log x$ is **convex**.

### 2.13 Jensen's inequality

Let $X$ be a random variable and $h$ a function.

$$\boxed{h \text{ convex}\;\Longrightarrow\; E\big[h(X)\big]\ \ge\ h\big(E[X]\big)}$$
$$\boxed{h \text{ concave}\;\Longrightarrow\; E\big[h(X)\big]\ \le\ h\big(E[X]\big)}$$

(The inequality simply reverses for concave $h$.) Jensen's inequality is used mainly in **theoretical proofs** rather than numerical work.

**Application 1 — variance is non-negative.** Take $h(x)=x^{2}$, which is convex:
$$E[X^{2}]\ \ge\ \big(E[X]\big)^{2}$$
and since $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$, this proves
$$\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(X)\ \ge\ 0 \quad\text{for every random variable.}$$

**Application 2 — bounding $E[\log X]$.** Take $h(x)=\log x$, which is concave:
$$E\big[\log X\big]\ \le\ \log\big(E[X]\big)$$
So even without knowing the distribution of $\log X$, knowing $E[X]$ gives an upper bound. Valid for **any** distribution (on $x>0$).

## 1. Overview

This session continues the treatment of **continuous probability distributions** begun in the previous lecture (where the uniform distribution and its use for simulating continuous data were covered). The first half develops the **exponential distribution** in full: its two equivalent parameterisations, its CDF, its moment generating function (with the convergence condition $t<\lambda$), its first four moments, skewness and kurtosis measures, and its defining **memoryless property** — the only continuous distribution possessing it (the discrete analogue being the geometric distribution). A side discussion clarifies why almost all distributions so far have support on the positive axis ("$0$ otherwise"), and introduces the **double exponential (Laplace)** density as an example of a two-sided modification. The second half introduces the **normal (Gaussian) distribution**, motivated by the Central Limit Theorem: its bell-shaped symmetric PDF, the coincidence of mean = median = mode $=\mu$, the fact that its CDF has **no closed form** (hence the need for numerical tables), the **standard normal variate** $Z=(X-\mu)/\sigma$ and the $Z$-table (with a worked table-lookup example), the **area property** ($68.27\%$ / $95.45\%$ / $99.73\%$ — the "six sigma limits"), the MGF $e^{\mu t+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2t^2}$ and the resulting moments ($\beta_2=3$, mesokurtic), the symmetry relation $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$, and finally the distribution theory needed for inference: definitions of $\bar X_n$ and $S^2$, the MGF proof that any linear combination of independent normals is normal, and the corollary $\bar X_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$.

## 2. Topics and concepts, in order taught

### 2.1 Recap / framing remark
- Last lecture: discrete distributions, then continuous distributions — in particular the **uniform distribution**, which is central to **simulating data from any continuous distribution** (inverse-CDF method).
- **Student question — why does every PDF end with "$0$ otherwise"?**
  - Answer: most standard distributions model real-life quantities (lifetimes, counts, waiting times), whose natural **support is the positive axis**. Hence $f(x)>0$ only for $x>0$ and $0$ elsewhere.
  - Distributions on the whole real line do exist (e.g. the normal, support $(-\infty,\infty)$), and one can also construct two-sided modifications. Example given — the **double exponential (Laplace) distribution**:
    $$f_X(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|x|},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}$$
  - In the discrete case, supports were positive but **countable** (e.g. binomial: $0,1,2,\dots,n$), not $0$ to $\infty$.
  - Negative data typically arise in **measurement-type** situations → modelled by the normal.

### 2.2 Exponential distribution — definition

A random variable $X$ has an **exponential distribution with rate parameter $\lambda$** if its PDF is

$$f_X(x)=\begin{cases}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}, & x>0\\[2pt] 0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad \lambda>0$$

Notation: $X\sim \mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$.

**Alternative (re-)parameterisation** seen in many textbooks:

$$f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\theta}e^{-x/\theta},\qquad x>0,\ \theta>0,\qquad\text{where }\theta=\frac{1}{\lambda}$$

- Both forms are equivalent; only the parameter differs. The support is always $(0,\infty)$.
- **In this course we use the first form**, $\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$.

**Shape of the density (graph described):**
- At $x=0$: $f(0)=\lambda e^{0}=\lambda$ (the intercept on the vertical axis).
- Strictly **decreasing in $x$**, tending to $0$ as $x\to\infty$, for **every** value of $\lambda$; $\lambda$ only rescales — hence called the **rate** (or **scale**) parameter.
- Interpretation: it is the **first/simplest lifetime model** — survival probability deteriorates monotonically over time. Used for modelling life of a system or item.

---

### 2.3 Exponential CDF

For $x\ge 0$:
$$F_X(x)=P(X\le x)=\int_0^{x}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=\lambda\left[\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{-\lambda}\right]_0^{x}=-e^{-\lambda x}+e^{0}=1-e^{-\lambda x}$$

$$\boxed{F_X(x)=\begin{cases}0,& x<0\\ 1-e^{-\lambda x}, & x\ge 0\end{cases}}$$

- **Simulation link:** as with the uniform, setting $F(x)=U$ with $U\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ lets you generate exponential data from uniform random numbers.

---

### 2.4 Exponential MGF

$$M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]=\int_0^{\infty}e^{tx}\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx=\lambda\int_0^{\infty}e^{-(\lambda-t)x}\,dx$$

- **Convergence condition:** the integral converges only if $\lambda-t>0$, i.e. **$t<\lambda$**. (If $\lambda-t\le 0$ the exponent is non-negative and the integral diverges.)

$$M_X(t)=\lambda\left[\frac{e^{-(\lambda-t)x}}{-(\lambda-t)}\right]_0^{\infty}=\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-t}$$

$$\boxed{M_X(t)=\left(1-\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{-1},\qquad t<\lambda}$$

---

### 2.5 Moments of the exponential (from the MGF)

**First derivative:**
$$\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t)=(-1)\left(1-\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{-2}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)$$
At $t=0$:
$$\mu_1'=E[X]=\frac{1}{\lambda}$$
(In the $\theta$-parameterisation, $E[X]=\theta$.)

**Second derivative:**
$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t)=(-1)(-2)\left(1-\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{-3}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)^{-3}$$
At $t=0$:
$$E[X^{2}]=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}$$

**Variance:**
$$\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[X^{2}]-\big(E[X]\big)^{2}=\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}$$

> **Key relation:** for the exponential, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\big(E[X]\big)^2$ — the variance is always the **square of the mean**.

**Higher central moments (by 3rd and 4th differentiation):**
$$\mu_1'=\frac{1}{\lambda},\qquad \mu_2=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},\qquad \mu_3=\frac{2}{\lambda^{3}},\qquad \mu_4=\frac{9}{\lambda^{4}}$$

**Shape measures:**
$$\beta_1=\frac{\mu_3^{2}}{\mu_2^{3}}=4,\qquad \gamma_1=\sqrt{\beta_1}=2\ (>0)\ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{positively skewed}$$
$$\beta_2=\frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^{2}}=9,\qquad \gamma_2=\beta_2-3=6\ (>0)\ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{leptokurtic}\ (\text{more peaked than normal})$$

---

### 2.6 Memoryless property of the exponential

**Statement.** The exponential is the **only continuous distribution** with the memoryless property (the discrete analogue is the **geometric distribution**).

**Claim:** for $X\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ and any $d\ge 0$, $x>0$,
$$P(X>d+x\mid X>d)=P(X>x)$$
i.e. the conditional probability is **independent of $d$**.

**Derivation:**
$$P(X>d+x\mid X>d)=\frac{P(X>d+x \ \cap\ X>d)}{P(X>d)}=\frac{P(X>d+x)}{P(X>d)}$$
(using that $\{X>d+x\}\subset\{X>d\}$ since $d+x>d$)
$$=\frac{1-F_X(d+x)}{1-F_X(d)}=\frac{e^{-\lambda(d+x)}}{e^{-\lambda d}}=e^{-\lambda x}=1-F_X(x)=P(X>x)$$

$$\boxed{P(X>d+x\mid X>d)=P(X>x)\quad \forall\, d\ge 0}$$

**Interpretation:** the additional lifetime $x$ does not depend on the time $d$ already elapsed/expended — the system "forgets" its age. (No integration is needed; only the CDF definition.)

### 2.7 Normal (Gaussian) distribution — motivation and definition

**Why it is the most important distribution:** because of the **Central Limit Theorem (CLT)** — for a sequence of random variables, the limiting distribution approaches the normal, so normal approximations can be used for probabilities. This makes it extremely popular in engineering applications with large data-generating processes. (CLT to be treated in a later lecture.)

**Definition.** $X$ follows a normal distribution with parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma^{2}$, written $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$, if

$$f_X(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right],\qquad -\infty<x<\infty$$
$$-\infty<\mu<\infty,\qquad \sigma>0$$

- Support is the **whole real line** (covers negative and positive values).
- $\mu$ is the **mean**, and (to be proved) also the **median** and the **mode**; $\sigma^{2}$ is the **variance**, $\sigma$ the **standard deviation**.
- Because the distribution is **symmetric**, mean = median = mode $=\mu$.

**Graph:** bell-shaped, symmetric about $x=\mu$; $50\%$ of probability on each side:
$$P(X\le \mu)=\tfrac12,\qquad P(X\ge \mu)=\tfrac12 \ \Rightarrow\ \mu \text{ is the median}$$
Maximum (mode) at $x=\mu$: differentiate $f$, set $=0$ → critical point $\mu$; second derivative confirms a maximum.

---

### 2.8 Normal CDF — no closed form

$$F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right]dt$$

- This integral **cannot be evaluated in closed form** for finite $x$. It can be computed **numerically** by algorithms, but there is **no closed-form expression** for the normal CDF. This is a major practical drawback → motivated the historical construction of **tables**.

---

### 2.9 Standardisation and the standard normal variate (SNV)

If $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$ (so $E[X]=\mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$), define

$$Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\ \sim\ N(0,1)$$

- $Z$ is called the **standard normal variate (SNV)** — a **parameter-free** distribution.
- One-to-one correspondence: $X=\sigma Z+\mu$. So probabilities/CDF values for any normal can be obtained from those of $Z$.

**PDF of the SNV** (conventional notation $\phi$ / "$f_Z$"):
$$f_Z(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^{2}/2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}$$
(obtained by putting $\mu=0$, $\sigma^2=1$ in the general PDF).

**CDF of the SNV** (conventional notation $\Phi$):
$$\Phi(z)=F_Z(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}f_Z(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{z}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,dt$$

---

### 2.10 Area property of the normal distribution ("six sigma limits")

For $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$:

$$P(\mu-\sigma\le X\le \mu+\sigma)\approx 0.6827\quad (\approx 68\%)$$
$$P(\mu-2\sigma\le X\le \mu+2\sigma)\approx 0.9545\quad (\approx 95\%)$$
$$P(\mu-3\sigma\le X\le \mu+3\sigma)\approx 0.9973\quad (>99\%)$$

- The $\mu\pm3\sigma$ range is the **six sigma limit** (three $\sigma$ to the left + three $\sigma$ to the right).
- **Numerical illustration:** out of $10{,}000$ data points, only about $27$ fall **outside** $\mu\pm3\sigma$ — points outside may or may not be genuine data.
- In SNV terms the curve is effectively concentrated on $[-3,3]$:
$$P(-3\le Z\le 3)\approx 0.997$$
- **Consequence for tables:** rather than evaluating the integral for all $z\in(-\infty,\infty)$, it suffices to tabulate
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_0^{z}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,dt \quad\text{for } 0\le z\le 3$$
and to obtain all other values via the symmetry relation.

---

### 2.11 Reading the $Z$-table

- The table shown ran from about $-3.99$ upward; **rows** give $z$ to one decimal (e.g. $-3.90$, $-1.50$), **columns** give the second decimal digit ($.00,.01,.02,\dots$).
- $\Phi(0)=0.5$ exactly.
- The particular table shown was labelled **"area to the left"** (i.e. entries are $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$) — student query clarified this.
- **Rule of thumb** given: if a tabulated value at a positive $z$ exceeds $0.5$, the table must be a left-tail ("area left") table, since $P(Z\le 0)=0.5$ is already included. Some tables instead tabulate only $\int_0^z$, i.e. the area from $0$ to $z$.
- Two-page (negative and positive) tables are provided for convenience, but **one side is enough** — use $\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$.

---

### 2.12 Worked example — probability via standardisation and table

**Given:** $X\sim N(\mu=3,\ \sigma^{2}=4)$, so $\sigma=\sqrt4=2$. **Find** $P(0<X<3)$.

**Step 1 — standardise.** $Z=\dfrac{X-3}{2}$.

**Step 2 — transform the inequality** (subtract $3$, divide by the positive number $2$ throughout):
$$P\!\left(\frac{0-3}{2}<\frac{X-3}{2}<\frac{3-3}{2}\right)=P(-1.5<Z<0)$$

**Step 3 — express via the CDF:**
$$P(-1.5<Z<0)=\Phi(0)-\Phi(-1.5)$$

**Step 4 — look up.** $\Phi(0)=0.5$; from the table at row $-1.5$, column $.00$: $\Phi(-1.50)=0.0668$.
*(In lecture the digits were read out as "0.0668 / 6681" — for $z=-1.52$ one would move to the column for the second digit "$2$".)*

**Step 5 —**
$$P(0<X<3)=0.5-0.0668=0.4332$$

**Moral:** no integration is ever required — convert to the SNV using the mean and standard deviation, then use the table.

### 2.13 MGF and moments of the normal

$$M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{tx}\,\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right]dx$$

After algebraic simplification (completing the square):

$$\boxed{M_X(t)=\exp\!\left(\mu t+\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}t^{2}\right),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}}$$

For the SNV ($\mu=0,\sigma^2=1$):
$$M_Z(t)=e^{t^{2}/2}$$

**Moments obtained from the MGF:**
$$\mu_1'=E[X]=\mu,\qquad \mu_2=\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^{2},\qquad \mu_3=0,\qquad \mu_4=3\sigma^{4}$$

**Shape measures:**
$$\beta_1=0,\quad \gamma_1=0 \ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{symmetric (zero skewness)}$$
$$\beta_2=3,\quad \gamma_2=\beta_2-3=0 \ \Rightarrow\ \textbf{mesokurtic}$$
- The normal's peak height is the **standard of comparison** for the kurtosis of all other distributions (leptokurtic vs. platykurtic).

**General moments (all orders):**
- All **odd**-order central moments vanish, by symmetry:
$$\mu_{2n+1}=0,\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$
(third, fifth, seventh, … moments are all zero)
- **Even**-order central moments:
$$\mu_{2n}=1\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdots(2n-1)\,\sigma^{2n}$$
Checks: $n=1\Rightarrow \mu_2=1\cdot\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}$; $n=2\Rightarrow \mu_4=1\cdot3\,\sigma^{4}=3\sigma^{4}$; $n=3\Rightarrow \mu_6=1\cdot3\cdot5\,\sigma^{6}=15\sigma^{6}$.
- Remark: the first four moments suffice for practical analysis; higher moments are of theoretical interest.

---

### 2.14 Symmetry relation for the standard normal CDF

Let $z>0$ and $\Phi(z)=P(Z\le z)$. By symmetry about $0$, the left tail below $-z$ equals the right tail above $z$:

$$\Phi(-z)=P(Z\le -z)=P(Z\ge z)=1-P(Z\le z)$$

$$\boxed{\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)}$$

- This is exactly why a **one-sided table suffices**: positive-side values give negative-side values and vice versa.

---

### 2.15 General interval-probability formula

For $X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$ and $a\le b$:

$$P(a\le X\le b)=P\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\le \frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\le \frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)=P\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\le Z\le \frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)$$

$$\boxed{P(a\le X\le b)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right)}$$

---

### 2.16 Data summaries: sample mean and sample variance

Given data $X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n$ from $N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$, and interest in inference about $\mu$ and $\sigma^{2}$, we first **condense the data** (dimension reduction) using statistics:

**Sample mean:**
$$\bar X_n=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i$$

**Sample variance:**
$$S^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(X_i-\bar X_n\right)^{2}$$

- Historically the divisor $\frac{1}{n}$ was used, but from the distributional properties of $S^2$ it turns out that the $\frac1n$ version is a **biased** estimator; the divisor $\frac{1}{n-1}$ gives an **unbiased** estimator — hence the modern convention.
- $\bar X_n$ is a good estimator of $\mu$; $S^{2}$ is a good estimator of $\sigma^{2}$.
- To assess "goodness" of estimators we later use **mean squared error (MSE)** and related criteria — for which we need the **sampling distributions** of $\bar X_n$ and $S^{2}$.

**Key result (to be proved below):**
$$\bar X_n\sim N\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)$$

---

### 2.17 Theorem: linear combinations of i.i.d. normals (MGF proof)

**Setup.** Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be **i.i.d.** $N(\mu,\sigma^{2})$, and define
$$Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iX_i$$
(weights $a_1$ on $X_1$, $a_2$ on $X_2$, …).

**Proof via MGF:**
$$M_Y(t)=E\!\left[e^{tY}\right]=E\!\left[\exp\!\left(t\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iX_i\right)\right]=E\!\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{t a_i X_i}\right]$$
(sum in the exponent → product of exponentials).

By **independence** (for independent $X,Y$: $E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]$, extended to any number of factors):
$$=\prod_{i=1}^{n}E\!\left[e^{(t a_i) X_i}\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{X_i}(t a_i)$$

Using the normal MGF with argument $ta_i$:
$$=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\exp\!\left(\mu t a_i+\tfrac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}a_i^{2}\right)=\exp\!\left(\mu t\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i+\tfrac12\sigma^{2}t^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{2}\right)$$

This is recognised as the MGF of a normal, hence

$$\boxed{Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iX_i\ \sim\ N\!\left(\mu\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i,\ \ \sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{2}\right)}$$

**Corollary — distribution of the sample mean.** Put $a_i=\frac1n$ for all $i$; then $Y=\bar X_n$, and
$$\sum_{i=1}^n a_i = n\cdot\frac1n=1,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n a_i^{2}=n\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac1n$$
$$\Rightarrow\ \bar X_n\sim N\!\left(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)$$

---

### 2.18 Generalisation: independent but **not** identically distributed normals

Let $X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma_i^{2})$, $i=1,\dots,n$, **independent** (different means/variances — data not collected from the same distribution). For $Y=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iX_i$:

$$\boxed{Y\sim N\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\mu_i,\ \ \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{2}\sigma_i^{2}\right)}$$

**Worked application — difference of two normals.** Let $X_1\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^{2})$, $X_2\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^{2})$, independent. Then $X_1-X_2$ is a linear combination with $a_1=1$, $a_2=-1$:
$$\text{mean}=\mu_1-\mu_2,\qquad \text{variance}=1^{2}\sigma_1^{2}+(-1)^{2}\sigma_2^{2}=\sigma_1^{2}+\sigma_2^{2}$$
$$X_1-X_2\sim N\!\left(\mu_1-\mu_2,\ \sigma_1^{2}+\sigma_2^{2}\right)$$
> Note: **means subtract, variances add.**

**Further example set as practice.** For independent normals $X_1,X_2,X_3$, identify the distribution of
$$U=X_1-2X_2+3X_3$$
using the general result ($a_1=1$, $a_2=-2$, $a_3=3$).

## 3. Flagged as important / for exams and assignments

The lecturer did not announce a formal assignment, but the following were explicitly stressed:

- **Use the course convention** for the exponential: $f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $x>0$ (not the $1/\theta$ form) — though be aware textbooks use both, with $\theta=1/\lambda$.
- **Always state the convergence/parameter conditions**: exponential MGF valid only for $t<\lambda$; normal parameters $-\infty<\mu<\infty$, $\sigma>0$.
- **Memoryless property**: exponential is the *only* continuous distribution with it; geometric is its discrete counterpart. The derivation using $1-F(\cdot)$ (no integration) was shown as the expected method.
- **Variance $=$ (mean)$^2$** for the exponential — a distinguishing relation.
- Exponential shape: $\gamma_1=2$ (positively skewed), $\gamma_2=6$ (leptokurtic). Normal: $\gamma_1=0$, $\beta_2=3$, $\gamma_2=0$ (mesokurtic, the reference standard).
- **The normal CDF has no closed form** — probabilities must be obtained by standardising to $Z$ and using the table. Practise the standardisation + table-lookup procedure (as in the $N(3,4)$ example).
- **Know the area property numbers**: $0.6827$, $0.9545$, $0.9973$ ("six sigma limits").
- **$\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$** — one table page suffices; be able to convert between sides.
- Know that **$\bar X_n\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$** and be able to derive it from the general linear-combination result via MGFs. (This is the basis of the standard error, $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.)
- Know the definitions of $\bar X_n$ and $S^2$ with the **$1/(n-1)$ divisor** and the reason (unbiasedness).

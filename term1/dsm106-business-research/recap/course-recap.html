Basecamp: experiment + survey + scenarios

Is a USD 29 / 79 project-management tool under-priced? An A/B test rules out doubling. An anchored price survey hides the headroom until you re-plot it as multiples of today's price. Scenario arithmetic then sizes the move.

THE DECISION AND THE RULES

business decision
The price that maximises customer lifetime value (CLV), even if some customers leave.
founder's four constraints
1 No price cuts, because price signals quality. 2 No feature changes: optimise price for the current feature set. 3 "Don't be afraid to be ambitious", e.g. club tiers rather than add them. 4 No historical data, although 10–12 years of it exist.
tiers (c. 2015–16)
free = 1 project · USD 29/mo · USD 79/mo · USD 3,000/yr = 250/mo. Not indexed to users or projects.
A/B test design
A field (live) experiment, not a lab experiment. Random assignment; control sees normal prices, test sees 2×. Window: 3 days, ~5,000 sign-ups. Wait 1 week for free → paid conversion. Debrief buyers and charge only the normal price.

READING THE A/B TEST

value per sign-up
$$\text{value per sign-up} = \text{conversion rate} \times \text{avg plan value}$$ control 1.47 vs test 1.27 USD
"should have doubled"
2× prices with an unchanged mix → average plan value should reach $2 \times 42.25 = 84.50$. It reached 71.14 ($1.68\times$, +68%).
double penalty
Volume falls (3.49% → 1.78%) and the mix shifts to the cheapest tier. The two don't offset → 2× is out.
limitations
Can't be repeated: customers compare prices and complain, and ~50 staff can't absorb the support load for millions of users. Lab tests on product features can be repeated. It only says +100% fails; nothing about +10% … +90%.

intersections
red × green = OPP (optimal) · orange × blue = IPP (indifference) · orange × green = highest acceptable · the lower crossing = lowest acceptable. Between them is the acceptable range. Cheap and simple next to an experiment.

ANCHORING BIAS AND THE FIX

anchoring bias
Current users anchor on what they already pay. They never say they'd pay more, and they push their stated prices down.
raw PSM says "no increase"
29 tier: lowest ≈ 20, OPP ≈ 20, highest ≈ 30 → max +1. 79 tier: OPP ≈ 45, IPP ≈ 52.50, highest ≈ 70, which is below what these users already pay.
re-plot as multiples
Express each answer as a multiple of the respondent's own price (1× = today). Draw box plots with median, quartiles and whiskers. "Average consumer" = the median in the instructor's usage.
four IQR readings
too cheap: median 0.33× (a 66% discount) · cheap: median 0.67×, Q3 ≈ 1×, whisker 1.8× · expensive: median 1.4–1.5×, Q3 2×, whisker 3.5× · too expensive: median 2×, Q3 3×, whisker 5.3×
decision rule
There is no room to raise the price if green sits near 1× and blue sits where yellow is now. Here blue = 1.5× and green = 2×, so there is room.

WHAT BASECAMP DID (S2) → TRIANGULATION

  • Killed Tier 1: no version without the company-vs-client split. "29 is too cheap for me."
  • Killed Tier 3: ~1% of customers, and dedicated support means customisation, which clashes with a high-volume, no-customisation model.
  • 79 → 99 (+25%): deliberately conservative next to the blue median of 1.5×.
  • 10% annual prepay discount: $99 \times 0.9 = 89.10$, used as 90/mo in class. That is an effective +14% over 79 ("10–14%"). Benefits: lock-in, cash up front, no monthly renewal risk.
  • Result claimed: revenue up ~20%.
  • Triangulation: the experiment rules out 2× · the survey (re-plotted) shows headroom · scenarios size the move. "Evidence-based decisions still contain guesswork."

READING THE PCA OUTPUT (S3)

first move after any survey
(1) How many groups do the questions fall into? (2) What does each group measure? Trivial at 7 items, essential at 50–70.
factor analysis = PCA
The course treats the two as the same thing.
variance
"If there is difference, there is information." No difference means no information. $\text{Var} = \text{SD}^2$.
proportion · cumulative proportion
The share of variance each component keeps, and the running total. Component 1 puts all items in one group; component 7 leaves every item alone (100%).
scree plot
Variance against component number. Look for the elbow, which here comes after component 3.
biplot
Item vectors and the 30 managers (as numbered points) share one plane. I1 I4 I5 I6 point right; I2 I3 I7 point top-left.
loadings: RC1, RC2
RC = rotated component (group 1, group 2). Magnitude runs 0–1; ignore the sign and ignore the other output columns.

FROM GROUPS TO DECISIONS

groups, not names
Factor analysis groups the items. Naming each group takes domain judgement.
Performance (RC1)
I1, I4, I5, I6: what is expected of a middle manager.
$\text{Perf} = \dfrac{I_1+I_4+I_5+I_6}{4}$
Intention to Quit (RC2)
I2, I3, I7. The label is intention, not behaviour.
$\text{ITQ} = \dfrac{I_2+I_3+I_7}{3}$
intention precedes behaviour
Intention to quit → actually quitting: lag ≈ 6 months (6 months to 1 year). In exceptional cases a person quits at once.
simple average
Chosen over the loading-weighted average, to keep things simple. 7 items → 2 parameters is dimension reduction.
organisational commitment
The literature's name for "loyalty" (it comes in several types).

THE FULL CHAIN: RUN IT ON ANY OUTPUT (S3)

  • 1. Components → proportion and cumulative variance
  • 2. Scree plot (elbow) → biplot (vector bundles)
  • 3. Loadings table → assign each item with the 0.5 rule
  • 4. Name each factor → build composite scores (two new columns)
  • 5. Compare cases on the two dimensions → the 2×2 → interventions

RULES OF THUMB, NOT ABSOLUTES

  • Cumulative > 90% → stop. Here that gives 2 components (≈93%). "There is nothing right or wrong about this."
  • Scree elbow → 3 here. The two criteria disagree. The instructor accepted 2, because component 3 adds only 4.7%.
  • |loading| > 0.5 → the item belongs; below 0.5 it does not. Stricter analysts use 0.6 or 0.7.
  • 2×2 of Performance × ITQ gives four binary cells, each with its own intervention. Continuous scores allow many more gradations.

I2 — praising another firm's culture (S2):

Before the statistics, and after it (S3):

  • All three are legitimate hypotheses. Different people use different criteria, so a statistical referee is needed. The PCA backed the two-group versions.

The I3 formality debate (S3): a student argued that being formal and official is a good thing, so I3 belongs with Performance. The instructor's reply:

  • Middle managers are the coordinating link between senior leadership and the frontline. Beyond their official duties, they have to keep up informal trust, reciprocity, mutual respect and cooperation.
  • Becoming more formal means becoming more distant. That is silent (quiet) quitting: doing only what is officially required and never going beyond it.
  • "Withdrawal from an organisation begins with withdrawal of informal participation." The loadings agree: I3 sits on RC2.

Morieux, "As work gets more complex, 6 rules to simplify" (shown in S3 to back the informality argument):

  • Krugman: "Productivity is not everything, but in the long run it is almost everything."
  • Productivity growth fell from ~5% p.a. (1950s–early 1970s) to ~3% (1973–83), ~2% (1983–95) and <1% (since 1995). At 3%, living standards double in ≈23 years (one generation). At 1% they take ≈70 years (three), and many people meanwhile end up worse off than their parents.
  • The Holy Trinity of management, clarity, measurement and accountability, has turned counterproductive.
  • The 4×100 m relay (8 finalist teams): "3.5 billion women on earth — where are the two fastest? In the US team." On summed personal bests the US was 3.2 m ahead of France (6.4 m on that year's bests), yet France won. "Those who carry the baton are slower, but their baton is faster." "This is not poetry, this is math."
  • Clarity: if my role ends at 97 m, the baton drops at 97 m, whether or not anyone is there to take it.
  • Accountability: give each hand-off its own accountable person and you get a clear line of blame, but you never win. Organisations are "designed to fail in a compliant way".
  • Measurement: energy spent in the arm and the throat is not in the legs. Cooperation is invisible to metrics, so rewarding measured individual speed makes the baton fall.
  • Cooperation is how you allocate effort, not a super-effort. It means taking a risk with your own measurable performance. "It takes to be stupid to cooperate," and people are not stupid, so they don't.
  • Interfaces, middle offices and KPIs pile up until teams waste 40–80% of their time. The prescription: go for fuzziness and overlaps, and look at the "how" (how the baton was passed), not just the "what".
  • The instructor's addition: blaming personalities instead of the system "adds injustice to ineffectiveness".

Text analysis, z-scores and cluster analysis: the attrition pipeline

Survey composites and DICTION text scores are z-standardised and k-means clustered, turning 30 middle managers into six retention segments with one HR intervention each.

CONTENT ANALYSIS (DICTION OR R)

content analysis
Systematic analysis of qualitative text. DICTION is one tool; R or any other platform also works.
DICTION procedure
Edit the text (the trial version only edits an existing file) → Standard Analysis → name the report ("Report 15") → open it in the Compare Viewer → read the five master variables at the end, each with a normal range.
500-word standardisation
A text of any length is scored as if it were 500 words long. Employee 15's appraisal is ≈ 47 words.
Activity formula
$$\begin{aligned}\text{Activity} =\;& (\text{Aggression} + \text{Accomplishment} \\ &+ \text{Communication} + \text{Motion}) \\ &- (\text{Cognitive terms} + \text{Passivity} \\ &+ \text{Embellishment})\end{aligned}$$Each term is a word dictionary. For example, the Accomplishment dictionary includes "advances", "creates", "manages" and "continuing". Context matters.
why subtract
Passivity: inert language is not creative. Embellishment: decoration, exaggeration and rhetoric are not innovation. Cognitive terms: an idea needs concrete, practical implications, not just thought.

Z-SCORES

why
The four indicators sit on different ranges (Commonality ≈ 16–78, Performance 6.78–10), so they are put on one scale.
formula
$$z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s}$$$\bar{x}$ is the sample mean and $s$ the sample SD of that variable.
Excel / R
Compute AVERAGE and STDEV, write z for row 1, lock the mean and SD cells as absolute references, then drag down. In R, four lines give z(Commonality), z(Activity), z(Performance), z(ITQ).
reading
−3 very low · −2 low · −1 below average · 0 average · +1 above average · +2 high · +3 very high
n = 30 → sign only
The graded reading needs 300, 400 or 3,000 cases. Here, $z > 0$ means high and $z < 0$ means low.
ITQ sign trap
Positive ITQ z = wants to LEAVE. In S5 a student read it the other way round and was corrected.

CLUSTER ANALYSIS (K-MEANS)

factor vs cluster
Factor analysis groups variables (7 items → Performance, ITQ). Cluster analysis groups cases (30 employees), using the four z-scores.
k-means
The simplest, "manual" form: the analyst picks k and the algorithm assigns cases. More automated methods exist.
elbow plot
y = information gained, x = number of groups. One group says little. The gain levels off at 6, so k = 6: 6 clusters × 5 = 30 employees.
profiling
Read the sign of each cluster's mean z on the four variables, then name the cluster with the typology below.
other uses
Marketing: customer segments by demographics, income, preferences, education and culture. Strategy: grouping firms by resources, size, financial strength, leadership, family-owned vs professional, and shareholding.

THE PIPELINE AND THE TAKEAWAY (S4–S5)

  • Survey: 7 items → factor analysis → Performance (I1, I4, I5, I6) and ITQ (I2, I3, I7), each a simple average.
  • Appraisal text: DICTION → Activity (creativity) and Commonality (team player).
  • Standardise all four as z-scores, then k-means with k = 6 from the elbow plot.
  • Profile clusters by z-signs → one HR intervention per segment. Not intervening and letting someone go is also a choice.
  • Mixed methods: factor + text + cluster analysis together give richer insight than any one technique. Combining qualitative and quantitative data supports concrete decisions that create business value.

Causal maps, cognitive distance and MDS

For groups of about 20 or fewer, turn each person's mental model into a signed matrix, measure how far apart two minds are, and read that distance as a diagnosis of the team.

WHEN AND HOW (S5, S7)

small sample only
≤ ~20 people: a top management team, a board, ~10 key clients, key R&D project members. Beyond ~20 it is hard to run.
the middle path
A survey gives too little autonomy (you are forced into the questionnaire). An interview gives too much discretion. A causal map lets people pick their own issues, then structures the answers as a matrix.
pipeline
interviews with 5–10 people → ≥ 50 issues → each respondent picks ~10 → draws signed arrows → matrix → compare → insight + action. Classroom demo: 15 issues, pick 5.
also an intervention
Used to build consensus, a common vision and strategic coherence (S7).
subjective
A map is one person's opinion, not objective truth. Another person would draw a different map from the same issues.

MAP → MATRIX (S5, S6)

arrow weights
$-3 \dots +3$. No arrow = 0. Issue not chosen = 100 (a placeholder; could be NA). The labels differ by session: S5 says +1 moderate, +2 high, +3 very high; S6 says +1 low, +2 moderate, +3 high.
a_ij
$a_{ij}$ = impact of row issue $i$ on column issue $j$.
directional, asymmetric
9→11 = −2 but 11→9 = −3. Reciprocal arrows can flip sign: 20→33 = +1, 33→20 = −1.
recode |a − b| (S6)
1. Take $|a-b|$ cell by cell.
2. A 100 paired with "not chosen" or with 0 → 0.
3. Residuals 97–103 (one person chose and rated, the other did not choose) → 1.
4. Genuine differences are kept: for 10→12, $|0-(-3)|=3$.
The sum of all cells is the numerator.

DISTANCE, BANDS, MDS (S6, S7)

distance ratio
$$D=\frac{\sum_i\sum_j \lvert a^*_{ij}-b^*_{ij}\rvert}{\text{den}}$$ $$\text{den}=6p_c^2+2p_c(p_{uA}+p_{uB})+p_{uA}^2+p_{uB}^2-(6p_c+p_{uA}+p_{uB})$$ $p_c$ = number of common issues; $p_{uA}, p_{uB}$ = issues unique to A and to B. D runs from 0 (think alike) to 1 (diametrically opposite).
bands
0–0.15 groupthink · 0.16–0.40 functional (ideal) · 0.41–0.70 threshold (acceptable, risky) · 0.71–1.0 dysfunctional. Both extremes are bad.
group value
The average of all pairwise distances.
MDS
Turns the distance matrix (not a correlation matrix) into a 2-D map. Points close together = people who think alike.

ISSUE IMPORTANCE (S7)

in-direction (ID)
$ID_j=\sum_{i\to j}\lvert w_{ij}\rvert$. Count incoming arrows only and ignore signs, then sum over the 12 maps. Example: node 3 has 3 incoming arrows, so ID = 8 (node 20 has 2).
rank points
$\text{points}=11-\text{rank}$, so rank 1 = 10 … rank 10 = 1. Summed over respondents.
when they conflict
ID beats rank points. Example: issue 4 had a lower ID than issue 3 but a higher rank.

READING THE RESULT (S7)

gap rule
Big gaps between successive ranked issues = clarity. Small, even gaps = confusion. Check #1 vs #2 (is there a single top issue?) and #4 vs #5 (is there a clear top four?).
how change happens
Within hierarchical levels first (trustees with trustees, top managers with top managers), then across levels.
realistic size
Expect 10–20% reductions over ~18 months, not a drop like 0.85 → 0.36.

β 0.7: a 1% market move gives a 0.7% WFM move, so WFM is less risky than the market. β 1.3 gives bigger gains and bigger losses. Betas are published, just like ratings.
Standard formula (supplementary, not given in class): $\beta = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i,R_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(R_m)}$. The class intuition $\sigma_{\text{co}}/\sigma_{\text{mkt}}$ is "not the exact formula".

value rule
ROC > WACC → creates value. If WACC > ROC → sell the business.
WFM verdict

zero debt

leverage

Valuing Whole Foods: EV/EBITDA multiples and DCF

WFM fell to USD 38.19 after weak results, yet Deutsche Bank said buy up to USD 60. Two valuation methods test that call, and both come down to one guess about the EBITDA margin.

EV, MARGIN, LEVERAGE

enterprise value
$\text{EV} = \text{market cap} + \text{debt}$. WFM: 14,481, no debt, so EV = equity value. Walmart: 251,747 + 55,245 = 306,992.
leverage
A firm's value is not just price × shares. Debt also adds to firm value.
EBITDA margin
$\dfrac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Sales}}$: WFM 9.5% · Walmart 7.5% · Costco 3.8% (about the same as conventional grocers). WFM's organic products are positioned as premium, so customers pay more.
EBITDA ≠ gross profit
The lecture called EBITDA "gross profit" (selling price − cost price). In standard accounting, gross profit = Sales − COGS. Follow the lecture's usage for this case, but keep the distinction for finance courses.

EV/EBITDA MULTIPLE

multiple
$\dfrac{\text{EV}}{\text{EBITDA}}$. WFM: 14,481 ÷ 1,222 = 11.85 ≈ 11.9. The market values WFM at 11.9 years of EBITDA.
peers
Sprouts 23.6 (EBITDA only 187, EV ≈ USD 4.4 bn) · Costco 13.6 despite a 3.8% margin · Walmart 8.6 · The Fresh Market close to WFM.
means nothing alone
Always compare with peers. Against natural/organic peers WFM looks fairly valued. Against conventional grocers, and against wholesalers other than Costco, it looks overvalued.
peer choice
A judgment call with no objective benchmark. Compare against a range of peers.
size effect
Small firms carry high multiples, which are hard to sustain at scale and tend to fall. 8.6 is a "reasonable" multiple for a giant.

recipe
$\text{EV} = \text{EBITDA} \times m$, then price = EV ÷ 372 million shares.

OPERATING RATIOS — READ BY INDUSTRY

CA turnover = Sales ÷ CA
Actuals 7.0, 5.6, 6.5 (10,108 ÷ 1,453 = 6.96). Projected 7.2, then held at 7.6. A grocer's current assets are perishables held from days up to ~2 weeks, so high is good. A turnover of 1–1.5 means goods sit, perish and cause losses. A furniture retailer can reasonably hold stock for 3–4 months.
CL turnover = COGS ÷ CL
COGS = Sales − EBITDA (the lecture's usage). CL = unpaid supplier bills. WFM must pay small organic producers fast, so its turnover is high (projected 10.5, above its CA turnover). Walmart buys in bulk on long-term contracts and pays late, so its turnover is low.
"neither is better or worse"
It depends on business strategy.
Net PP&E per store
Air conditioning, cold storage… 1,997 ÷ 311 = 6.42 → USD 6.4 m. S9 labels this 2013, but S8 used 1,997 as the FY2011 PP&E, so the year is unclear. Projected ≈ USD 5.4 m as stores get smaller.
D&A per store
≈ USD 0.89 m, roughly 15–16% of PP&E.

THE 10-STEP PROJECTION (FROM 2014)

  • 1 stores, from store growth
  • 2 sales, from sales growth
  • 3 EBITDA = sales × margin
  • 4 D&A = stores × D&A per store
  • 5 EBIT = EBITDA − D&A (terminal year ≈ 2,318)
  • 6 CA = sales ÷ CA turnover
  • 7 CL = COGS ÷ CL turnover
  • 8 NWC = CA − CL, i.e. inventory minus bills still to settle. Slightly negative is fine (sells fast, pays slowly; Walmart may be negative). WFM pays fast, so its NWC is positive.
  • 9 Net PP&E = stores × PP&E per store
  • 10 revenue per store = sales ÷ stores
  • Also NWC turnover = Sales ÷ NWC and PP&E turnover = Sales ÷ Net PP&E. Sales sit well above NWC, so there are no short-term liquidity problems. These ratios are "crucial for financial analysis".

THE DCF CHAIN

NOPAT
$\text{EBIT}\times(1-t)$ with t = 39%. Example: 976 × 0.61 ≈ 596.
FCF
$\text{NOPAT} - \Delta\text{Net PP\&E} - \Delta\text{NWC}$. Some money goes into new stores, some into working capital; what remains is cash in hand.
ROC vs cost of capital
$\dfrac{\text{NOPAT}}{\text{NWC} + \text{Net PP\&E}}$. The denominator is short- plus long-term capital, roughly the minimum a buyer would pay. Last actual ≈ 16.37%; projected 20.9% vs ≈ 6.9–7%. If competition squeezes margins, ROC falls. This is the key assumption.
PV
$PV = \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t}$. r = 7% (6.9% rounded) is the cost of raising money through shares or corporate bonds. 1/1.07 = 0.9346.
Gordon terminal value
$TV_{10} = \dfrac{FCF_{\text{term}}}{r-g}$, then discount it by $(1+r)^{10}$. g = 3.4%, the rate at which the firm grows forever: ≈ the bond rate (3–4%), US population/economic growth and retail-sector growth.
share price
$(PV_{\text{forecast}} + PV_{TV}) \div 372$ m shares. There is no debt, so equity value = EV.

Multiples, the instructor's way (S9):

  • Buying a company: its EBITDA is ₹200 crore and the seller asks ₹4,000 crore, which is 20×. The buyer says no competitor trades above 10× and offers 10 × 200 = ₹2,000 crore. "That is how negotiations work."
  • A shareholder buys one share, not the whole firm. That is why analysts turn firm value into a per-share price.
  • Sprouts at 23.6× looks heavily overvalued against its peers, so a correction (a share-price fall) is likely.

Money over time, and who pays whom (S9):

  • ₹1 crore next year ≈ ₹93 lakh today (1/1.07 = 0.9346). The lecturer put two years out at "about 0.91–0.92"; the correct factor is 1/1.07² = 0.8734.
  • Indian digression: Reliance (Jio) and Adani want agricultural law reform. Corporate farming would allow long credit terms with suppliers, which lowers CL turnover and raises profitability. Farmers fear losing their land, and that is a tension in the Indian economy.

"Everything depends on the margin projection" (S9):

  • Two methods, one hinge: both the multiples and the DCF turn on whether WFM keeps its premium margin.
  • The projection is always partly a guess, but it must be an intelligent guess. Base it on competitors' actions, customer preferences, whether the firm stays valuable to customers, and how intense the competition is.
  • The middle case lands on 8.6×, Walmart's multiple. That implies no organic premium at all, so the instructor thought it may be too pessimistic.
  • Practise on your own: make projections, check them six months later, and learn from the errors. No course teaches how to project declining margins; you combine hints from strategy, marketing and finance.

What actually happened (S9):

  • Revenue growth came in below expectations. EBITDA and operating margins fell to about 6–7%. ROC fell from 17% to 15%. WFM opened fewer stores than projected.
  • The price went from USD 48.61 (Apr 2014) to about 38. It rebounded to USD 54.55 (Feb 2015), which the market judged overvalued, then fell again.
  • WFM never recovered, and Amazon acquired it while the price was depressed.
  • The class's middle case (≈ USD 36) came closest; actual prices were slightly lower. Deutsche Bank itself later revised to about USD 30–35.

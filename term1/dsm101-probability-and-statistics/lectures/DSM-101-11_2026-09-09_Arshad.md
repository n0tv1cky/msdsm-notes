This is the first session of the second half of DSM-101, taught by Dr. Mohammad Arshad (Associate Professor, Dept. of Mathematics, IIT Indore, leads the Statistics & Data Science research group). After course logistics (module plan, session schedule, weightage, recommended books), the lecture builds the formal notion of a **random variable** as a function $X:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ that converts non-numeric experimental outcomes into meaningful real numbers. Using the two-coin-toss experiment with a possibly biased coin ($P(H)=p$), the probability distribution of $X$ = "number of heads" is derived from scratch and shown to sum to 1. The lecture then classifies random variables into **discrete**, **continuous**, and **mixed** types, gives the formal definition of the **probability mass function (PMF)** and its two characterizing properties, and then develops the **cumulative distribution function (CDF)** $F_X(x)=P(X\le x)$ — its correct piecewise form over all of $\mathbb{R}$, its graph (step function with jumps), its three characterizing properties (non-decreasing, right-continuous, limits 0 and 1), and the key idea that **jump size at a point = probability mass at that point**. Two worked CDF examples close the session: one purely discrete (jumps sum to 1) and one mixed type (jumps sum to 1/2), with a careful ε-free limit computation of $F(2^-)$, $F(1^-)$, $F(1^+)$.

## 3. Topics Covered, in Order

### 3.1 Deterministic vs. Random Experiments

- **Deterministic experiment:** everything known in advance; input → fixed output.
  - Examples given: increase the volume of a room → pressure and temperature respond in a known related way; $2+2 = 4$ always.
  - Input–output relation is fixed ⇒ nothing is random.
- **Random experiment:** same input can produce several possible outputs (output 1, output 2, output 3, …), each with **certain chances**. You know the *collection* of possible outputs but not which one occurs.

### 3.2 Outcome Space $\Omega$

- Every random experiment has an attached **outcome space** $\Omega$ (also called *sample space* in many textbooks).
- Example: toss **one** coin ⇒ $\Omega = \{H, T\}$.
- Example: toss **two** coins ⇒ $\Omega = \{HH,\ HT,\ TH,\ TT\}$, labelled $\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3, \omega_4$.
- **Problem:** the outcomes are *not numbers* (or, if they are, they may not be meaningful numbers). Mathematical machinery cannot be applied directly.

### 3.3 Definition: Random Variable

> A **random variable** is a function
> $$X : \Omega \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}$$
> mapping the outcome space into (a subset of) the real line, converting experimental outcomes into meaningful numbers.

- The image set $S = X(\Omega) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is called the **support of $X$** (also loosely called the sample space *of X*).
- **Terminology note (clarified in a student Q&A):** $\Omega$ and $S$ are both "sample spaces" but on **different domains**. To avoid confusion the instructor's convention:
  - $\Omega$ = **outcome space** (domain, the raw experimental outcomes)
  - $S$ = **support of the random variable $X$** (the set of real values with positive probability)
  - Once we map to $\mathbb{R}$, we work only on the real line.

### 3.4 Worked Example: Two Coin Tosses

Let $X$ = **number of heads in tossing two coins**.

Mapping:
| $\omega$ | $HH$ | $HT$ | $TH$ | $TT$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $X(\omega)$ | 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 |

Support: $S = \{0, 1, 2\}$.

**Probability model (coin need NOT be fair):**
- $P(H) = p$, where $p \in (0,1)$; $P(T) = 1 - p$
- $p = 1/2$ ⇒ fair coin; otherwise biased toward head or tail
- The two coins are **independent** (two physically different coins, neither influences the other) ⇒ probabilities multiply

**Derivation:**
- $P(X = 0) = P(TT) = P(T)\cdot P(T) = (1-p)^2$
- $P(X = 1) = P(HT \text{ or } TH)$. The two events are **mutually exclusive** ⇒ add; each is a product by independence:
  $$P(X=1) = P(H)P(T) + P(T)P(H) = p(1-p) + (1-p)p = 2p(1-p)$$
- $P(X = 2) = P(HH) = P(H)P(H) = p^2$

**Distribution table:**

| $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $P(X=x)$ | $(1-p)^2$ | $2p(1-p)$ | $p^2$ |

**Check total probability:**
$$(1-p)^2 + 2p(1-p) + p^2 = \big[(1-p) + p\big]^2 = 1^2 = 1$$
(using $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a+b)^2$). True for **every** $p \in (0,1)$.

**Interpretation:** total probability 1 is *distributed* among all possible values of $X$ — hence "**probability distribution of $X$**."

**Fair-coin special case ($p = 1/2$):**
$$P(X=0) = \tfrac14,\qquad P(X=1) = 2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12, \qquad P(X=2) = \tfrac14$$
Sum $=\tfrac14+\tfrac12+\tfrac14 = 1$. ✔

### 3.5 Three Types of Random Variables

**(a) Discrete type**
- $X$ has support $S$ that is a **countable set** (finite or countably infinite).
- Examples of allowed supports: $S = \{0,1,2,\dots\}$ (countably infinite), $S=\{0,\pm1,\pm2,\dots\}$, $S = \{-1, 0, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 2\}$ (finite; values need not be integers — only countability matters).
- Typical real situations: **counting** things (counts of items, counts of events), age measured in whole years.

**(b) Continuous type**
- $X$ has support of **interval form**: e.g. $[a,b]$, $(-\infty,\infty)$, $(0,\infty)$, $(-\infty,5)$.
- It **cannot take any particular single value with positive probability**.
- Typical real situations: **measurements** — measurement error, weight, time (measured in seconds), blood pressure.
- Caveat mentioned: the *recorded/observed* data are discrete numbers, but the *theoretical model* for measurements is continuous.

**(c) Mixed type**
- Neither discrete nor continuous — a **mixture of the two**.
- Requires special treatment; only an *idea* will be given in this course, not detailed development.
- **The course focuses on types (a) and (b).**

### 3.6 Probability Mass Function (PMF)

**Motivation from the graph:** for the fair two-coin example, plotting probability against $x = 0,1,2$ gives point masses of heights $1/4$, $1/2$, $1/4$ — masses sitting at isolated points. Hence the name *mass* function.

> **Definition.** A function $p_X(\cdot)$ is the **probability mass function (PMF)** of a discrete random variable $X$ with support $S$ **if and only if**
> 1. $p_X(x) \ge 0$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ (non-negativity)
> 2. $\displaystyle\sum_{x \in S} p_X(x) = 1$ (total mass one)

- "If and only if": any function satisfying (1) and (2) **is** the PMF of *some* random variable.
- Abbreviation: **PMF**.

---

### 3.7 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

Also called **distribution function** or **DF**; abbreviation **CDF**.

> **Definition.** If $X$ is a random variable (here, discrete with PMF $p_X$), its CDF is
> $$F_X(x) = P(X \le x), \qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}$$

- **Key point:** $F_X$ is defined on the **whole real line**, not just on the support. You accumulate all probability mass lying at or to the left of $x$.

**Worked construction (fair two-coin example, $p_X$: $1/4,\ 1/2,\ 1/4$ at $0,1,2$):**

Accumulated values: $F(0) = 1/4$; $F(1) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4$; $F(2) = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1$.

> ⚠️ **Instructor's warning:** writing the CDF only as a 3-column table ($x = 0,1,2$) is **NOT correct** — a table is fine for a PMF, but a CDF must be written in **piecewise-interval form over all of $\mathbb{R}$**.

**Correct form:**
$$
F_X(x) =
\begin{cases}
0, & x < 0\\[2pt]
\tfrac14, & 0 \le x < 1\\[2pt]
\tfrac34, & 1 \le x < 2\\[2pt]
1, & x \ge 2
\end{cases}
$$

**Graph:** a step function. Zero until $x=0$; sudden **jump** of $1/4$ at $x=0$; flat at $1/4$ until $x=1$; jump to $3/4$ at $x=1$; flat until $x=2$; jump to $1$ at $x=2$; flat at $1$ forever.

---

### 3.8 The Three Characterizing Properties of a CDF

1. **$F_X(x)$ is non-decreasing in $x$** — it is constant or increasing, never decreasing.
2. **$F_X(x)$ is right-continuous** for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$: $F(a) = F(a^+)$ for all $a$. (Left-continuity may fail — that failure is exactly a *jump*.)
3. **Limits:**
 $$\lim_{x\to-\infty} F_X(x) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lim_{x\to+\infty} F_X(x) = 1$$
 Motivation: at $-\infty$ no probability weight has been accumulated; at $+\infty$ all weight is exhausted.

> **Characterization (emphasized):** Any function satisfying these three properties **is** the CDF of some random variable, and the CDF **uniquely** determines the distribution. So the CDF — not just the PMF/PDF — is a fundamental and powerful object.

---

### 3.9 Refresher: Continuity, Left and Right Limits (student question)

A function $f$ is **continuous at $x = a$** iff
$$f(a) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} f(a - h) \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} f(a + h), \qquad h > 0$$

- **Left limit:** $F(a^-) := \lim_{h\to 0} F(a-h)$ (approaching from the left)
- **Right limit:** $F(a^+) := \lim_{h\to 0} F(a+h)$ (approaching from the right)
- **Function value:** $F(a)$

Then:
- $f$ continuous at $a$ ⟺ all three are equal
- **Right-continuous at $a$** ⟺ $F(a) = F(a^+)$ — we do **not** require left-continuity.

**Illustration with the coin CDF at $a=1$:** $F(1) = 3/4$, $F(1^+) = 3/4$ (right-continuous ✔), but $F(1^-) = 1/4$ ⇒ **not** left-continuous. That discrepancy is the jump.

---

### 3.10 Jump Points and Jump Sizes — the Key Identity

> **Jump point:** $a$ is a jump point of $F$ if $F(a^-) < F(a)$.
>
> **Jump size at $a$:** $\;F(a) - F(a^-)$
>
> **Fundamental identity:** $\;\;P(X = a) = F(a) - F(a^-)$
>
> i.e. **the jump size at a point equals the probability mass at that point.**

**Example (coin CDF, jump at $x=1$):**
$$F(1) - F(1^-) = \tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 = P(X=1) \ \checkmark$$

**Classification rule derived from this:**
- If $\sum(\text{all jump sizes}) = 1$ ⇒ $X$ is **discrete**.
- If $F$ has **no jumps at all** (continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$) ⇒ $X$ is **continuous type**.
- If $0 < \sum(\text{jump sizes}) < 1$ ⇒ $X$ is **neither discrete nor continuous** ⇒ **mixed type**; the remaining probability sits continuously over some interval.

---

### 3.11 Worked Example A — Recovering a PMF from a CDF (purely discrete)

Given
$$
F_X(x) =
\begin{cases}
0, & x < 1\\[2pt]
\tfrac12, & 1 \le x < 5\\[2pt]
\tfrac34, & 5 \le x < 9\\[2pt]
1, & x \ge 9
\end{cases}
$$
**Question:** find the PMF; is $X$ discrete?

**Method (stated by instructor):** either plot it, or simply identify the points where the behaviour of the function changes. Here those points are $\mathbf{1,\ 5,\ 9}$ — candidate jump points.

Compute jumps:
- $P(X=1) = F(1) - F(1^-) = \tfrac12 - 0 = \tfrac12$
- $P(X=5) = F(5) - F(5^-) = \tfrac34 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$
- $P(X=9) = F(9) - F(9^-) = 1 - \tfrac34 = \tfrac14$

Sum of jumps: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1$.

**Conclusion:** total jump size $=1$ ⇒ $X$ is a **discrete** random variable with PMF

| $x$ | 1 | 5 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|
| $P(X=x)$ | $1/2$ | $1/4$ | $1/4$ |

### 3.12 Worked Example B — A Mixed-Type Random Variable

Given
$$
F_X(x) =
\begin{cases}
0, & x < 1\\[4pt]
\dfrac{x-1}{2}, & 1 \le x < 2\\[6pt]
1, & x \ge 2
\end{cases}
$$

**Step 1 — Sketch / sanity check.**
- For $x<1$: $F=0$.
- At $x=1$: $(1-1)/2 = 0$ — matches, so **no jump at 1**.
- As $x \to 2^-$: $(2-1)/2 = 1/2$ — a straight line rising from $0$ at $x=1$ to $1/2$ (not attained) at $x=2$.
- At $x=2$: $F$ jumps to $1$ and stays there.

**Step 2 — Verify it is a valid CDF.**
- Non-decreasing ✔
- Right-continuous everywhere (including at $x=2$) ✔
- $\lim_{x\to-\infty}F = 0$, $\lim_{x\to+\infty}F = 1$ ✔

⇒ It is **not an arbitrary function; it is a valid CDF** of some random variable.

**Step 3 — Find jump points.** The only place $F$ is not continuous is $x = 2$.

$$P(X=2) = F(2) - F(2^-)$$

Compute the left limit carefully:
$$
F(2^-) = \lim_{h\to 0} F(2-h)
= \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{(2-h)-1}{2}
= \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{1-h}{2}
= \frac{1-0}{2} = \frac12
$$
(Here $h>0$, so $2-h$ falls in the interval $[1,2)$ and the middle branch applies.)

For comparison, the right limit: $F(2^+) = \lim_{h\to 0} F(2+h) = \lim_{h\to 0} 1 = 1 = F(2)$ ⇒ right-continuous ✔.

Therefore
$$P(X=2) = 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$$

**Step 4 — Check $x=1$ is NOT a jump point** (explicit verification done in class):
- $F(1) = \dfrac{1-1}{2} = 0$
- $F(1^-) = \lim_{h\to 0} F(1-h) = \lim_{h\to 0} 0 = 0$ (since $1-h < 1$)
- $F(1^+) = \lim_{h\to 0} F(1+h) = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{(1+h)-1}{2} = \lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{h}{2} = 0$

All three equal $0$ ⇒ $F$ is **continuous at $x=1$** ⇒ **no jump at 1**.

**Step 5 — Conclusion.**
Sum of all jumps $= \tfrac12 \ne 1$, and $\ne 0$.
⇒ $X$ is **neither discrete nor continuous** ⇒ **mixed type**.

- The missing weight of $1/2$ is spread **continuously over the interval $[1,2)$** — this is the "continuous part."
- The jump of $1/2$ at $x=2$ is the "discrete part."

## 4. Summary Box — Formulas to Memorize

| Concept | Statement |
|---|---|
| Random variable | $X:\Omega \to \mathbb{R}$; support $S = X(\Omega)$ |
| PMF conditions | $p_X(x)\ge 0\ \forall x\in\mathbb{R}$; $\sum_{x\in S} p_X(x) = 1$ |
| CDF | $F_X(x) = P(X\le x)$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$ |
| CDF property 1 | $F_X$ non-decreasing |
| CDF property 2 | $F_X$ right-continuous: $F(a)=F(a^+)$ |
| CDF property 3 | $F_X(-\infty)=0$, $F_X(+\infty)=1$ |
| Left / right limits | $F(a^-)=\lim_{h\to0}F(a-h)$, $F(a^+)=\lim_{h\to0}F(a+h)$ |
| Jump point | $F(a^-) < F(a)$ |
| Jump size = point mass | $P(X=a) = F(a) - F(a^-)$ |
| Discrete test | $\sum$ jump sizes $=1$ |
| Continuous test | $F$ has no jumps (continuous on all $\mathbb{R}$) |
| Mixed | $0 < \sum$ jump sizes $< 1$ |
| Two-coin example | $P(X{=}0)=(1-p)^2,\ P(X{=}1)=2p(1-p),\ P(X{=}2)=p^2$ |

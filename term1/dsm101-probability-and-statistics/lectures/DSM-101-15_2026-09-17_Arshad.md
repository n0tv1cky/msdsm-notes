### 2.A Preliminary exercise — Decimal → Binary using a Stack

- **Algorithm (repeated division method):** repeatedly divide the decimal number by $2$, record the remainder at each step, stop when the quotient reaches $0$.
- The binary equivalent is obtained by reading the remainders in **reverse order** (bottom to top).
- **Key idea:** whenever you need to reverse a sequence, use a **stack**. Push each remainder as it is produced; popping them out yields the reversed order automatically (LIFO).
- **Worked example: convert $15_{10}$ to binary**
  - $15 \div 2 \Rightarrow$ quotient $7$, remainder $1$
  - $7 \div 2 \Rightarrow$ quotient $3$, remainder $1$
  - $3 \div 2 \Rightarrow$ quotient $1$, remainder $1$
  - $1 \div 2 \Rightarrow$ quotient $0$, remainder $1$
  - Reading remainders bottom-to-top: $1111_2$
  - **Caveat noted:** $15$ is a *special case* — all remainders are $1$, so it reads the same forwards and backwards. Use a different number to actually see the reversal matter.
- **Exercise set:** write the full algorithm (with pseudocode/alphabet) for decimal→binary conversion using a stack: push each remainder, then pop all to read off the binary string.

### 2.B Doubt Session — Example 2.8 (Module 1): Finding $k$ in a piecewise CDF

**The given CDF:**

$$
F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 2 \\[4pt]
\dfrac{2}{3}, & 2 \le x < 5 \\[6pt]
\dfrac{7 - 6k}{6}, & 5 \le x < 9 \\[6pt]
\dfrac{3k^2 - 6k + 7}{6}, & 9 \le x < 14 \\[6pt]
\dfrac{16k^2 - 16k + 19}{16}, & 14 \le x \le 20 \\[6pt]
1, & x > 20
\end{cases}
$$

**Question:** find the value of the constant $k$.

#### The three defining properties of a CDF

$F_X$ is the CDF of a random variable $X$ **if and only if**:

1. **Right continuity:** $F_X$ is right-continuous at every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, i.e.
 $$F_X(x) = F_X(x^+) \quad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{R}$$
2. **Non-decreasing:** $F_X$ is a non-decreasing function (the instructor stresses *non-decreasing*, not strictly increasing, because of the flat/constant stretches).
3. **Limits at the ends:**
 $$\lim_{x \to +\infty} F_X(x) = 1, \qquad \lim_{x \to -\infty} F_X(x) = 0$$

#### Clarification on jumps vs. right continuity (the student's question)

- A **jump** at a point $x$ means
 $$F_X(x^-) < F_X(x), \qquad \text{where } F_X(x^-) = \lim_{h \to 0} F_X(x - h)$$
- The jump arises from the **left-hand limit** being strictly smaller. Right continuity is *never* violated by a properly defined CDF: at the point itself the function takes the **upper** value, and $F_X(x) = F_X(x^+)$ always.
- Graphically: at a jump the point is **filled (included)** on the upper branch, and **hollow (excluded)** on the lower branch.
- **Applied at $x = 20$:** the value of $k$ **must** be chosen so that
 $$F_X(20^-) = F_X(20) = F_X(20^+)$$
 i.e. there is **no jump at 20**; the function is already constant at $1$ from that point. This is exactly why we equate the $14 \le x \le 20$ branch to $1$.
- **Takeaway stated explicitly:** "It cannot break the right-continuity point" — in all such problems you impose $F_X(x) = F_X(x^+)$ and solve for the constant.

#### Step 1 — Use right continuity at $x = 20$

Set the fifth branch equal to $1$:
$$\frac{16k^2 - 16k + 19}{16} = 1$$

Solving gives **two candidate roots**:
$$k = \tfrac{1}{4} \quad \text{or} \quad k = \tfrac{3}{4}$$

#### Step 2 — Use the non-decreasing property to reject $k = 3/4$

Require $F_X(5^-) \le F_X(5)$:

$$\frac{2}{3} \le \frac{7 - 6k}{6}$$

$$\Rightarrow\; 7 - 6k \ge 4$$
$$\Rightarrow\; 6k \le 7 - 4 = 3$$
$$\Rightarrow\; k \le \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Since $3/4 > 1/2$, the root $k = 3/4$ **violates** the non-decreasing property and is rejected.

#### Conclusion

$$\boxed{k = \tfrac{1}{4}}$$

> **Pedagogical point emphasised by the instructor:** this example is deliberately constructed so that *one property alone is not enough*. You must check **all** the CDF properties; using only right continuity leaves an incorrect candidate in play.

### 2.D Geometric Distribution

#### Setup

Based on a sequence of independent **Bernoulli trials** with:
- $p$ = probability of success (fixed across trials)
- $q = 1 - p$ = probability of failure
- $0 < p < 1$

#### Version 1: $X$ = number of **failures before the first success**

We keep trying until the first success occurs, then stop.
- Success on the 1st attempt $\Rightarrow X = 0$
- Failure, then success $\Rightarrow X = 1$
- Failure, failure, then success $\Rightarrow X = 2$, etc.

**PMF:**
$$
P(X = x) = \begin{cases}
q^x p, & x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\qquad 0 < p < 1,\; q = 1 - p
$$

**Shape:** the probabilities $p,\, qp,\, q^2p,\, q^3p, \ldots$ form a **decreasing geometric sequence** (since $q < 1$) — hence the name.

**Verification that it is a proper PMF (normalisation):**
$$\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} p\, q^x = p \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} q^x = p \cdot \frac{1}{1-q} = p \cdot \frac{1}{p} = 1 \quad \checkmark$$

#### Real-life motivation

- A **loan company** calls customers with an offer; the customer refuses (failure), they call again, and keep calling until the loan is approved / customer agrees (success). The number of attempts required is the random variable.
- Similarly **credit-card telemarketing**: repeated calls until the customer accepts (or blocks the caller).
- The model answers: *how many attempts are required?*

#### Version 2: $Y$ = number of **trials until the first success**

$Y$ takes values $1, 2, 3, \ldots$ (at least one trial is always needed).
- Success on 1st attempt $\Rightarrow Y = 1$
- Failure then success $\Rightarrow Y = 2$, etc.

**PMF:**
$$
P(Y = y) = \begin{cases}
p\, q^{\,y-1}, & y = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

**Relationship between the two versions:**
$$Y = X + 1$$

Hence
$$E[Y] = E[X] + 1, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X + 1) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$$

(because an additive constant does not affect variance).
> **The two forms have different means but the same variance.**

---

#### Moment Generating Function of $X$ (Version 1)

$$M_X(t) = E\!\left[e^{tX}\right] = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} e^{tx} p\, q^{x} = p \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \left(q e^{t}\right)^{x}$$

**Convergence condition for a geometric series:**
$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^{k} = \frac{1}{1-a}, \qquad \text{provided } |a| < 1$$

(If $a > 1$, the series diverges — you are adding infinitely many quantities each larger than 1, so the sum is $\infty$.)

So we require:
$$q e^{t} < 1$$

Therefore:
$$M_X(t) = \frac{p}{1 - q e^{t}} = p\left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-1}$$

**Deriving the range of $t$:**
$$q e^{t} < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; e^{t} < q^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{q}$$
Take logs on both sides:
$$t \ln e < \ln\!\left(q^{-1}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; t < -\ln q$$

**Interpretation:** since $q \in (0,1)$, $\ln q < 0$, so $-\ln q > 0$. The range of $t$ is
$$t \in (-\infty,\, -\ln q), \qquad \text{which contains } 0.$$
Since the interval contains a neighbourhood of $0$, the **MGF exists**.

$$\boxed{M_X(t) = p\left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-1}, \qquad t < -\ln q}$$

---

#### Mean of $X$ (first derivative of the MGF at $t=0$)

$$\frac{d}{dt} M_X(t) = p \cdot (-1)\left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-2} \cdot \left(- q e^{t}\right) = p\, q\, e^{t} \left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-2}$$

At $t = 0$ (using $e^0 = 1$, $1 - q = p$):
$$E[X] = p\, q\, (1-q)^{-2} = p\, q\, p^{-2} = \frac{q}{p}$$

$$\boxed{E[X] = \frac{q}{p}}$$

---

#### Second moment $E[X^2]$

Differentiate again (product rule, with $pq$ factored out; first factor $e^t$, second factor $(1-qe^t)^{-2}$):

$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2} M_X(t) = pq\left[ e^{t}\left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-2} + e^{t}\cdot(-2)\left(1 - q e^{t}\right)^{-3}\cdot\left(-q e^{t}\right) \right]$$

At $t = 0$:
$$E[X^2] = pq\left[(1-q)^{-2} + 2q(1-q)^{-3}\right] = pq\left[\frac{1}{p^{2}} + \frac{2q}{p^{3}}\right]$$

$$= \frac{pq}{p^{3}}\left[p + 2q\right] = \frac{q}{p^{2}}\left(p + q + q\right) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}\left(1 + q\right)$$

$$\boxed{E[X^2] = \frac{q(1+q)}{p^{2}}}$$

---

#### Variance of $X$

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - \left(E[X]\right)^2 = \frac{q(1+q)}{p^{2}} - \left(\frac{q}{p}\right)^{2}$$
$$= \frac{q}{p^{2}} + \frac{q^{2}}{p^{2}} - \frac{q^{2}}{p^{2}} = \frac{q}{p^{2}}$$

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}}$$

- Skewness, kurtosis etc. can be computed similarly; the geometric distribution is **positively skewed**.

---

#### Mean and variance of $Y$ (Version 2)

$$E[Y] = E[X+1] = \frac{q}{p} + 1 = \frac{q + p}{p} = \frac{1}{p} \qquad (\text{since } p + q = 1)$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}$$

$$\boxed{E[Y] = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}}$$

#### Numerical illustrations of $E[Y] = 1/p$

| $p$ (prob. of success per trial) | $E[Y] = 1/p$ | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (fair game) | $E[Y] = 2$ | on average **2 trials** to first success |
| $p = \tfrac{3}{4}$ (high) | $E[Y] = \tfrac{4}{3} \approx 1.33$ | on average a little over **1 trial** |
| $p = \tfrac{1}{8}$ (low) | $E[Y] = 8$ | on average **8 trials** |

> **Business interpretation:** if a call-centre knows that on average 1 out of 8 people is convinced ($p = 1/8$), then on average 8 calls are needed per success — this lets them predict future behaviour and plan resources.

#### Memoryless Property of the Geometric Distribution

> **Key fact stated in lecture:** the geometric distribution is the **only discrete distribution** possessing the memoryless property. In the continuous case, the **exponential distribution** is the only one with this property.

**Statement.** Let $X \sim$ Geometric with $P(X = x) = p q^{x}$, $x = 0,1,2,\ldots$. Suppose the event has **not** occurred up to time $k$, i.e. we are given $X \ge k$. Define the additional waiting time $Y = X - k$. Then

$$P(Y \ge t \mid X \ge k) \;=\; P(X - k \ge t \mid X \ge k) \;=\; P(X \ge k + t \mid X \ge k) \;=\; P(X \ge t)$$

i.e. the conditional distribution is **independent of $k$** — the process "forgets" how many attempts have already been made.

**Lemma needed (tail probability).** For any $a$:
$$P(X \ge a) = \sum_{x=a}^{\infty} P(X = x) = \sum_{x=a}^{\infty} q^{x} p = p\left(q^{a} + q^{a+1} + \cdots\right) = p \cdot \frac{q^{a}}{1-q} = p\cdot \frac{q^{a}}{p} = q^{a}$$

$$\boxed{P(X \ge a) = q^{a}}$$

**Proof of memorylessness.** Using the conditional-probability definition
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}:$$

$$P(X \ge k+t \mid X \ge k) = \frac{P\left(\{X \ge k+t\} \cap \{X \ge k\}\right)}{P(X \ge k)}$$

Since $t \ge 0$ (additional time is non-negative), $\{X \ge k+t\} \subseteq \{X \ge k\}$, so the intersection is just $\{X \ge k+t\}$:

$$= \frac{P(X \ge k+t)}{P(X \ge k)} = \frac{q^{\,k+t}}{q^{\,k}} = q^{\,t} = P(X \ge t) \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Interpretation:** "Forget about whatever attempts you have already made — it doesn't matter." The distribution does not store in memory how many attempts have been spent. E.g. if you already made 3 attempts and now need 4 more, the probability is the same as needing 4 from scratch.

### 2.E Continuous Distributions — Uniform Distribution

#### Definition

$X$ follows a **uniform distribution on the interval $(a,b)$**, written $X \sim U(a,b)$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $a < b$. The name reflects that probability is **distributed uniformly (equally)** over the interval.

- Interval length $= b - a$.

**Conditions a PDF must satisfy:**
1. $f_X(x) \ge 0$ for every $x$
2. $\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\,dx = 1$

Here the density is non-zero only on $(a,b)$, so we need
$$\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx = 1$$

**Geometric derivation of the height:** the graph is a **rectangle** of base $b-a$ and height $h$; the area under the curve must equal 1, so
$$h \cdot (b-a) = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; h = \frac{1}{b-a}$$

**PDF:**
$$
f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{b-a}, & a < x < b \\[6pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

---

#### Probability of a sub-interval

For $a < c < d < b$:

**By integration:**
$$P(c < X < d) = \int_{c}^{d} \frac{1}{b-a}\,dx = \frac{1}{b-a}\left[x\right]_{c}^{d} = \frac{d-c}{b-a}$$

**By geometry (no integration needed):** area of the sub-rectangle $=$ (base) $\times$ (height) $= (d-c)\cdot\dfrac{1}{b-a} = \dfrac{d-c}{b-a}$.

> **Practical note:** for the uniform distribution you never need to integrate — read probabilities directly off the graph as rectangle areas.

---

#### CDF of the uniform distribution

$F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$.

- For $x < a$: $F_X(x) = 0$
- For $x \ge b$: $F_X(x) = 1$
- For $a \le x < b$:
$$F_X(x) = \int_{a}^{x} \frac{1}{b-a}\,dt = \frac{1}{b-a}\left[t\right]_{a}^{x} = \frac{x-a}{b-a}$$

(Note: dummy variable $t$ is used because $x$ is already taken as the upper limit.)

$$
F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < a \\[4pt]
\dfrac{x-a}{b-a}, & a \le x < b \\[6pt]
1, & x \ge b
\end{cases}
$$

---

#### Moments of the uniform distribution

The distribution is **symmetric**, so mean = median = mode = the midpoint of the interval.

$$\mu_1' = E[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}$$

$$\mu_2 = \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^{2}}{12}$$

$$\mu_3 = 0 \qquad (\text{by symmetry})$$

$$\mu_4 = \frac{(b-a)^{4}}{80}$$

> Stated in lecture: the uniform distribution is always **symmetric** and **platykurtic**.

*(Note: the $\mu_4$ value was spoken as "$(b-a)^4$ divided by 80"; verify against the module.)*

---

#### MGF of the uniform distribution

$$M_X(t) = E\!\left[e^{tX}\right] = \int_a^b e^{tx}\cdot\frac{1}{b-a}\,dx = \frac{1}{b-a}\left[\frac{e^{tx}}{t}\right]_a^b = \frac{e^{bt} - e^{at}}{t\,(b-a)}$$

This requires $t \ne 0$ (division by $t$ is undefined at $t=0$). For $t = 0$:
$$M_X(0) = E\!\left[e^{0\cdot X}\right] = E[1] = 1$$

**Complete MGF:**
$$
M_X(t) = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{t\,(b-a)}\left(e^{bt} - e^{at}\right), & t \ne 0 \\[8pt]
1, & t = 0
\end{cases}
\qquad (a < b)
$$

---

#### Standard Uniform $U(0,1)$

Put $a = 0$, $b = 1$: $X \sim U(0,1)$, called the **standard uniform distribution**.

$$
f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
1, & 0 < x < 1 \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

$$
F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 0 \\
x, & 0 \le x < 1 \\
1, & x \ge 1
\end{cases}
$$

> **Remark on applicability:** mathematically the uniform is a very simple distribution, and in real life it rarely arises directly — but it is extremely useful in **simulation**.

### 2.F Probability Integral Transform (PIT) and Simulation

#### Theorem (Probability Integral Transform)

Let $X$ be a **continuous random variable** with CDF $F_X$. Define
$$Y = F_X(X)$$
Then
$$Y \sim U(0,1)$$

> This is the result that makes $U(0,1)$ so powerful: it lets you simulate data from **any** continuous distribution whose CDF you know.

#### The simulation algorithm (inverse transform method)

1. Generate $U_1, U_2, \ldots, U_n$ from $U(0,1)$ — there are many efficient algorithms/generators for uniform numbers on $(0,1)$.
2. Since $U = F_X(X)$, set
 $$u_i = F_X(x_i), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n$$
3. Invert:
 $$\boxed{x_i = F_X^{-1}(u_i), \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n}$$
4. The resulting $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ are simulated observations from the target distribution.

---

#### Worked example — simulating from an exponential-type distribution

**Given CDF:**
$$
F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 0 \\
1 - e^{-x}, & x \ge 0
\end{cases}
$$

**Step 1 — set $u_i = F_X(x_i)$:**
$$u_i = 1 - e^{-x_i}$$

**Step 2 — invert algebraically:**
$$e^{-x_i} = 1 - u_i$$
$$-x_i = \ln(1 - u_i)$$
$$\boxed{x_i = -\ln(1 - u_i)}, \qquad i = 1,\ldots,n$$

**Step 3 — plug in the uniform sample.** Take five uniform draws:
$$u_1 = 0.1,\quad u_2 = 0.2,\quad u_3 = 0.3,\quad u_4 = 0.4,\quad u_5 = 0.5$$

Then:

| $i$ | $u_i$ | $x_i = -\ln(1-u_i)$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $0.1$ | $-\ln(0.9)$ |
| 2 | $0.2$ | $-\ln(0.8)$ |
| 3 | $0.3$ | $-\ln(0.7)$ |
| 4 | $0.4$ | $-\ln(0.6)$ |
| 5 | $0.5$ | $-\ln(0.5)$ |

These five numbers constitute a **simulated sample from the distribution with the given CDF** (i.e. the standard exponential).

## 4. Quick Formula Sheet

**CDF properties**
$$F_X(x) = F_X(x^+)\ \forall x; \quad F_X \text{ non-decreasing}; \quad \lim_{x\to\infty}F_X(x)=1,\ \lim_{x\to-\infty}F_X(x)=0$$

**Conditional probability**
$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

**Geometric series**
$$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} a^{k} = \frac{1}{1-a}, \quad |a| < 1$$

**Geometric distribution — $X$ = failures before 1st success**
$$P(X=x) = q^{x}p,\ x=0,1,2,\ldots \qquad q = 1-p$$
$$M_X(t) = p\left(1-qe^{t}\right)^{-1},\quad t < -\ln q$$
$$E[X] = \frac{q}{p}, \qquad E[X^2] = \frac{q(1+q)}{p^{2}}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}$$
$$P(X \ge a) = q^{a}$$
$$P(X \ge k+t \mid X \ge k) = P(X \ge t) \quad \text{(memoryless)}$$

**Geometric distribution — $Y$ = trials until 1st success, $Y = X+1$**
$$P(Y=y) = p\,q^{\,y-1},\ y=1,2,3,\ldots$$
$$E[Y] = \frac{1}{p}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \frac{q}{p^{2}}$$

**Uniform $U(a,b)$**
$$f_X(x) = \frac{1}{b-a},\ a<x<b; \qquad F_X(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a},\ a \le x < b$$
$$P(c<X<d) = \frac{d-c}{b-a}$$
$$E[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}, \quad \mu_3 = 0, \quad \mu_4 = \frac{(b-a)^4}{80}$$
$$M_X(t) = \frac{e^{bt}-e^{at}}{t(b-a)}\ (t \ne 0); \qquad M_X(0) = 1$$

**Standard uniform $U(0,1)$**
$$f_X(x)=1,\ 0<x<1; \qquad F_X(x)=x,\ 0\le x<1$$

**Probability Integral Transform**
$$X \text{ continuous with CDF } F_X \;\Longrightarrow\; Y = F_X(X) \sim U(0,1)$$
$$\text{Simulation: } x_i = F_X^{-1}(u_i),\quad u_i \sim U(0,1)$$

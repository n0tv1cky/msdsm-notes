## 1. Overview

This session continues the study of random variables, moving from the discrete case (covered previously: PMF, CDF) to the **continuous case**. The lecture motivates why point probabilities fail for measurement-type variables (speed, blood pressure, sugar level) — the support is uncountable, so assigning positive probability to individual points would make total probability infinite. The formal definition of a continuous random variable via an integral representation of its CDF is given, along with the two defining properties of a **probability density function (PDF)**, the differentiation/integration relationship between PDF and CDF, and the crucial warning that **a PDF is *not* a probability** (its value may exceed 1). Two worked examples are done: (i) recovering a PDF from a piecewise CDF and computing $P(1/2 < X < 3/2)$ by two different methods (integrating the PDF, and differencing the CDF), and (ii) finding a normalising constant $k$ for the PDF $k - |x|$. The second half introduces **transformations** $Y = g(X)$ of a discrete random variable, splitting into the one-to-one case (direct inverse substitution) and the not-one-to-one case (point-by-point accumulation of pre-image probabilities), with three worked examples including $Y = X+1$, $Y = X^2$ on a support containing negative values, and $Y = X^2$ on a non-negative support. The session ends with a preview of upcoming topics.

## 2. Topics Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap of previous lecture
- Random variables classified into:
  - **Discrete** type
  - **Continuous** type
  - **Mixed** type
- For discrete RVs: **probability mass function (PMF)** and **cumulative distribution function (CDF / distribution function)**, denoted $F_X$.

### 2.2 Motivation for continuous random variables
- Arises with **measurement** situations: speed of a car, blood pressure, sugar level — values are not obtained by counting.
- $X$ takes values in an interval $[a,b]$ or $(-\infty, \infty)$.
- Key problem: an interval such as $[0,100]$ is a subset of the real line containing **uncountably many** points.
  - If we assign a positive probability to each point and sum over an uncountable set, the total probability becomes **infinite** — contradiction.
- Resolution: do **not** assign probabilities to single points. Instead define a function that gives **probabilities of sub-intervals** of the support (cumulative probability).

### 2.3 Definition: Continuous random variable and PDF

> **Definition.** A random variable $X$ is said to be of **continuous type** if there exists a function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ (a real-valued function) such that its distribution function $F_X$ satisfies
> $$F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt, \qquad x \in \mathbb{R}$$
> where $f$ satisfies:
> 1. $f(t) \ge 0$ for every $t \in \mathbb{R}$ (non-negativity)
> 2. $\displaystyle \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,dt = 1$ (total area $=1$)
>
> Then $f$ is called the **probability density function (PDF)** of $X$, written $f_X$.

- Note on property 2: by definition
  $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,dt = F(\infty) = \lim_{x \to \infty} F_X(x) = 1.$$
- Geometric reading: the graph of $f_X$ lies **on or above the horizontal axis**, and the **total area under the curve is exactly 1**.

### 2.4 PDF is NOT a probability (important conceptual point)
- For a **PMF**, values are genuine probabilities: each value $\le 1$, and they sum to 1.
- For a **PDF**, the value at a point is **not** a probability:
  - It may be **greater than 1** at some points.
  - It may only never be negative, and its total integral must be 1.
- **Counterexample given in class:** let
  $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 2, & 0 < x < \tfrac12 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
  - Check 1: $f(x) \ge 0$ for all $x$ (value is either 2 or 0). ✔
  - Check 2: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \int_{0}^{1/2} 2\,dx = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1.$ ✔
  - So it is a **valid PDF**, yet its value $2 > 1$. Hence a PDF cannot be a probability.
- Summary statement from lecture: *"PDF is just a function which helps us compute probabilities; the PMF is a probability."*

### 2.5 Relationship between PDF and CDF
1. If $F_X$ is the CDF of a continuous RV $X$, the PDF is obtained by differentiation:
   $$f_X(x) = \frac{d}{dx} F_X(x)$$
2. If the PDF $f_X$ is known, the CDF is obtained by integration:
   $$F_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt = P(X \le x)$$
- Visual interpretation: $F_X(a) = \int_{-\infty}^{a} f_X(x)\,dx$ = area under the density curve to the left of $a$ = $P(X \le a)$.
- **Intuition for differentiating (student question):** the CDF is an accumulated area under the density up to $x$; since integration and differentiation are inverse ("anti") operations like $+$ and $-$, differentiating the accumulated area recovers the "small effect" (the density) at each point.

### 2.6 Identifying continuous vs. discrete from the CDF
- **Discrete type:** the CDF is a **step/jump function**; the total of all jump sizes equals 1.
- **Continuous type:** the CDF is **continuous everywhere** (no jumps).
- Also: in the continuous case, **single-point probability is always zero**, i.e. $P(X = a) = 0$. Hence
  $$P(a < X < b) = P(a \le X \le b) = P(a \le X < b) = P(a < X \le b).$$
  In the **discrete** case, one must carefully track left/right limits when computing probabilities from the CDF.

### 2.7 Worked Example 1 — From CDF to PDF, then compute a probability

**Given.** $X$ has CDF
$$F_X(x) = \begin{cases}
0, & x < 0\\[2pt]
\dfrac{x^2}{2}, & 0 \le x < 1\\[6pt]
\dfrac{x}{2}, & 1 \le x < 2\\[6pt]
1, & x \ge 2
\end{cases}$$

**Step 1 — Verify continuity (so that $X$ is continuous type).**
- At $x=0$: $\lim_{h \to 0^+} F(0+h) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(0+h)^2}{2} = 0$; $F(0) = 0$; $F(0^-) = 0$. No jump ⇒ continuous at 0.
- At $x=1$: from the left, $\frac{1^2}{2} = \frac12$; $F(1) = \frac12$; from the right, $\frac{1}{2} = \frac12$. No jump ⇒ continuous at 1.
- At $x=2$: from the left, $\frac{2}{2} = 1$; $F(2) = 1$; from the right $=1$. No jump ⇒ continuous at 2.
- ⇒ $F_X$ is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$ ⇒ **$F_X$ is the CDF of a continuous random variable.**

**Step 2 — Differentiate to get the PDF.**
- Differentiability: the instructor states (verified, not required from students) that $F$ **is** differentiable at $x = 0$ but **is not** differentiable at $x = 1$ and $x = 2$.
- **Rule used:** if the function fails to be differentiable at finitely many points, we ignore those points and differentiate on each interval. At the exceptional points we may assign **any non-negative value** we like.
- Differentiating piecewise:
  - $x < 0$: derivative $=0$
  - $0 < x < 1$: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x^2}{2}\right) = \frac{2x}{2} = x$
  - $1 < x < 2$: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) = \frac12$
  - $x > 2$: derivative $= 0$
- General form of the answer (with arbitrary non-negative constants at the exceptional points):
  $$f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
  x, & 0 < x < 1\\
  \tfrac12, & 1 < x < 2\\
  a, & x = 0\\
  b, & x = 1\\
  c, & x = 2\\
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}\qquad a,b,c \ge 0 \ \text{(any non-negative reals)}$$
- **Simplest choice:** take $a = b = c = 0$, giving the final PDF
  $$f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
  x, & 0 < x < 1\\[3pt]
  \dfrac12, & 1 < x < 2\\[6pt]
  0, & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}$$

**Graph description.** On $(0,1)$ the density is the line $y = x$ rising from 0 to 1; on $(1,2)$ it is the constant $\frac12$; zero elsewhere. (At $x=0,1,2$ we set the value to 0.) Note the PDF here is **discontinuous** even though the CDF is continuous — answering a student's question: *the CDF of a continuous RV is always continuous, but its PDF need not be continuous.*

**Step 3 — Compute $P\left(\tfrac12 < X < \tfrac32\right)$.**

*Method A — integrate the PDF (split at the point $x=1$ where the formula changes):*
$$P\!\left(\tfrac12 < X < \tfrac32\right) = \int_{1/2}^{3/2} f_X(x)\,dx = \int_{1/2}^{1} x\,dx + \int_{1}^{3/2} \tfrac12\,dx$$
$$= \left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_{1/2}^{1} + \frac12\Big[x\Big]_{1}^{3/2}
= \frac12\left(1 - \frac14\right) + \frac12\left(\frac32 - 1\right)$$
$$= \frac12 \cdot \frac34 + \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = \frac{3}{8} + \frac14 = \frac{3+2}{8} = \frac{5}{8}$$

*Method B — difference the CDF:*
$$P\!\left(\tfrac12 < X < \tfrac32\right) = F\!\left(\tfrac32\right) - F\!\left(\tfrac12\right)$$
- $\frac32 \in [1,2)$ ⇒ $F(3/2) = \frac{3/2}{2} = \frac34$
- $\frac12 \in [0,1)$ ⇒ $F(1/2) = \frac{(1/2)^2}{2} = \frac{1/4}{2} = \frac18$
$$\Rightarrow \frac34 - \frac18 = \frac{6-1}{8} = \frac{5}{8}$$
- **Same answer by two completely different routes.** (In the continuous case, strict vs. non-strict inequalities do not matter, since $P(X=a)=0$.)

### 2.8 Worked Example 2 — Finding an unknown constant in a PDF

**Given.**
$$f(x) = \begin{cases} k - |x|, & |x| < \tfrac12 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
Find $k$.

**Solution.** Since $f$ is a PDF it must satisfy (i) $f(x) \ge 0\ \forall x$, and (ii) $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$. Use (ii):

Note $|x| < \tfrac12 \iff -\tfrac12 < x < \tfrac12$.
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{-1/2} 0\,dx + \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} (k - |x|)\,dx + \int_{1/2}^{\infty} 0\,dx$$
$$= \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} k\,dx - \int_{-1/2}^{1/2} |x|\,dx$$
- First term: $k\Big[x\Big]_{-1/2}^{1/2} = k\left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\right) = k$.
- Second term: $|x|$ is an **even function** over a **symmetric interval**, so
  $$\int_{-1/2}^{1/2} |x|\,dx = 2\int_{0}^{1/2} x\,dx = 2\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^{1/2} = 2 \cdot \frac12\left(\frac12\right)^2 = \frac14.$$
- Therefore $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = k - \frac14$.

Set equal to 1:
$$k - \frac14 = 1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \boxed{k = 1 + \frac14 = \frac54}$$

**General lesson:** whenever a PDF or PMF contains an unknown constant, determine it from the normalisation condition ($\int f = 1$ or $\sum p = 1$), and then all required probabilities follow.

## 3. Transformations of Random Variables (discrete case)

### 3.1 Setup
- Start with a random variable $X$ with known PMF $f_X$ (and/or CDF $F_X$) and known support $S_X$.
- Define a **new** random variable $Y = g(X)$ for some function $g$.
- Under suitable restrictions, $Y$ is again a random variable.
- **Goal:** find the PMF (and support / CDF) of $Y$.

### 3.2 Why transform? (student question)
- Real data frequently require derived quantities: e.g. we record **weight** but want **BMI**, which is a function of weight (and height).
- More generally, indices are functions/combinations of several individual random variables; we then need the distribution of the transformed variable.

### 3.3 Support of $Y$ is countable
- $S_X$, the support of a discrete RV, is **finite or countably infinite** (e.g. $\{-5,-1,1,2,3,7\}$ or $\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}$).
- Under $y = g(x)$, the image set $S_Y$ has at most as many points as $S_X$ (two different $x$ values may map to the same $y$).
- Hence $S_Y$ is again countable ⇒ **if $X$ is discrete, $Y = g(X)$ is discrete.**

### 3.4 Case 1: $g$ is one-to-one (on $S_X$)
If $g$ is one-one, the inverse $g^{-1}$ exists on $S_Y$, and the PMF of $Y$ is
$$h(y) = P(Y = y) = P\big(g(X) = y\big) = P\big(X = g^{-1}(y)\big) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big), \qquad y \in S_Y$$
- i.e. **simply substitute the inverse function into the PMF of $X$.**
- **Important caveat:** "one-one" must be checked **on $S_X$**, not on all of $\mathbb{R}$. E.g. $y = x^2$ is many-to-one on $\mathbb{R}$, but one-to-one if $S_X$ contains only non-negative values.

### 3.5 Case 2: $g$ is NOT one-to-one
- No single formula. Work **point by point** over $S_Y$: for each $y \in S_Y$, collect **all** pre-images $x$ with $g(x)=y$ and **add** their probabilities:
  $$h(y) = P(Y=y) = \sum_{x:\, g(x)=y} f_X(x)$$
- Pre-images that lie outside $S_X$ contribute probability 0.

## 4. Worked Transformation Examples

### Example A — $Y = X + 1$ (one-to-one)

**Given.** $f_X(x) = \left(\frac12\right)^{x}$ for $x = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, so $S_X = \{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}$.
Define $Y = X + 1$.

- **Support of $Y$:** $x=1 \to y=2$, $x=2 \to y=3$, … ⇒ $S_Y = \{2,3,4,5,\ldots\}$.
- **Inverse:** $Y = X+1 \Rightarrow X = Y-1$.
- For $y \in S_Y$:
  $$h(y) = P(Y=y) = P(X+1 = y) = P(X = y-1) = \left(\frac12\right)^{y-1}$$
  (Note: for any $y \in S_Y$, $y-1 \in S_X$, so the probability is well defined.)
- **Final PMF:**
  $$h(y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2^{\,y-1}}, & y = 2,3,4,\ldots\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

---

### Example B — $Y = X^2$ with a support containing negative values (NOT one-to-one)

**Given.**
$$f_X(x) = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{7}, & x \in \{-2,\,-1,\,0,\,1\}\\[6pt]
\dfrac{3}{14}, & x \in \{2,\,3\}\\[6pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$$
So $S_X = \{-2,-1,0,1,2,3\}$ (six points). Define $Y = X^2$.

**Support of $Y$:** $(-2)^2 = 4,\ (-1)^2 = 1,\ 0^2 = 0,\ 1^2 = 1,\ 2^2 = 4,\ 3^2 = 9$
$$\Rightarrow S_Y = \{0,\,1,\,4,\,9\}$$
Number of points is reduced ($6 \to 4$) ⇒ the map is **not one-to-one** (both $-1,1 \mapsto 1$ and $-2,2 \mapsto 4$).

**Point-by-point computation:**

- $P(Y=0) = P(X^2 = 0) = P(X = 0) = \dfrac{1}{7}$
- $P(Y=1) = P(X^2 = 1) = P(X = \pm 1) = P(X=-1) + P(X=1) = \dfrac17 + \dfrac17 = \dfrac{2}{7}$
- $P(Y=4) = P(X^2 = 4) = P(X=-2) + P(X=2) = \dfrac17 + \dfrac{3}{14} = \dfrac{2+3}{14} = \dfrac{5}{14}$
- $P(Y=9) = P(X^2 = 9) = P(X=-3) + P(X=3) = 0 + \dfrac{3}{14} = \dfrac{3}{14}$
  (since $-3 \notin S_X$, its probability is 0)

**Resulting PMF of $Y$:**

| $y$ | 0 | 1 | 4 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $P(Y=y)$ | $\frac{1}{7}$ | $\frac{2}{7}$ | $\frac{5}{14}$ | $\frac{3}{14}$ |

**Check (total must be 1):**
$$\frac17 + \frac27 + \frac{5}{14} + \frac{3}{14} = \frac{2}{14} + \frac{4}{14} + \frac{5}{14} + \frac{3}{14} = \frac{2+4+5+3}{14} = \frac{14}{14} = 1 \ \checkmark$$

### Example C — $Y = X^2$ on a non-negative support (one-to-one) — Binomial-type PMF

**Given.**
$$f_X(x) = \binom{2}{x}\left(\frac14\right)^{x}\left(\frac34\right)^{2-x}, \qquad x = 0,1,2$$
(i.e. a Binomial $n=2$, $p=1/4$ PMF). $S_X = \{0,1,2\}$. Define $Y = X^2$.

- **Support of $Y$:** $0^2=0,\ 1^2=1,\ 2^2=4 \Rightarrow S_Y = \{0,1,4\}$ — **three points, same count as $S_X$** ⇒ $g$ is **one-to-one on $S_X$** (even though $x \mapsto x^2$ is many-to-one on $\mathbb{R}$).
- **Inverse (positive root only, since $S_X \ge 0$):** $X = \sqrt{Y}$.
- $$P(Y=y) = P(X^2 = y) = P(X = \sqrt{y})$$
- **Final PMF (one-line solution):**
  $$h(y) = \begin{cases}\dbinom{2}{\sqrt{y}}\left(\dfrac14\right)^{\sqrt{y}}\left(\dfrac34\right)^{2-\sqrt{y}}, & y \in \{0,1,4\}\\[10pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

**Related remark from class discussion:** if in Example A ($f_X(x)=2^{-x}$, $S_X=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}$) we instead set $Y = X^2$, then $S_Y = \{1,4,9,16,\ldots\}$, the map is one-to-one, $X = \sqrt{Y}$, and
$$h(y) = \frac{1}{2^{\sqrt{y}}}, \qquad y \in \{1,4,9,16,\ldots\}$$

## 5. Key Conceptual Clarifications from Q&A

- **Q: Can the CDF be continuous while the PDF is not?**
  Yes. For a continuous RV, the CDF is always continuous, but the PDF has **no guarantee of continuity** (Example 1's PDF jumps at $x=1$ and $x=2$). The PDF is merely a function used to compute interval probabilities.
- **Q: Why write $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,dt = F(\infty)$?**
  This is only **notation**. By convention in statistics:
  $$F(\infty) := \lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = 1, \qquad F(-\infty) := \lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = 0$$
  Interpretation: at $+\infty$ all points are covered ⇒ probability 1; at $-\infty$ no points are covered ⇒ probability 0.
- **Q: Does restricting the range of $g$ change $S_X$?**
  **No.** $S_X$ is fixed once $X$ is defined — changing it would change $X$'s probability function. The choice/restriction of $g$ affects only $S_Y$, and hence the PMF/PDF/CDF of $Y$. Whether $g$ is one-one must always be judged **relative to $S_X$**.
- **Q: What is really happening when we differentiate the CDF?**
  The CDF is the accumulated area under the density up to $x$. Integration and differentiation are inverse operations, so differentiating the accumulated area recovers the local density ("small effect") at $x$.

## 8. Formula Sheet (quick revision)

- **Continuous RV definition:** $F_X(x) = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x} f(t)\,dt$, $x \in \mathbb{R}$
- **PDF conditions:** $f(t) \ge 0\ \forall t \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,dt = 1$
- **CDF from PDF:** $F_X(x) = \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{x} f_X(t)\,dt = P(X \le x)$
- **PDF from CDF:** $f_X(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}F_X(x)$
- **Limits of the CDF:** $F(-\infty) = \lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x) = 0$, $F(\infty) = \lim_{x\to\infty}F(x) = 1$
- **Interval probability (continuous):** $P(a < X < b) = \displaystyle\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$
- **Point probability (continuous):** $P(X = a) = 0$
- **Even-function shortcut:** $\displaystyle\int_{-c}^{c} |x|\,dx = 2\int_0^c x\,dx$
- **Transformation, one-one case:** $h(y) = P(Y=y) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)$, $y \in S_Y$
- **Transformation, general discrete case:** $h(y) = \displaystyle\sum_{x:\,g(x)=y} f_X(x)$
- **PMF used in Example C (Binomial $n=2$, $p=1/4$):** $f_X(x) = \dbinom{2}{x}\left(\frac14\right)^{x}\left(\frac34\right)^{2-x}$, $x=0,1,2$

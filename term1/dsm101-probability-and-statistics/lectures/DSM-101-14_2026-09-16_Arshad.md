## 1. Overview

This session continues from the previous lecture on expectations and moments (including skewness and kurtosis) by introducing the **moment generating function (MGF)** as a tool that "generates" all the moments of a distribution. The MGF is defined, its existence conditions discussed, and two methods of extracting moments from it are derived: (i) reading off the coefficient of $t^k/k!$ in the series expansion, and (ii) repeated differentiation evaluated at $t=0$. A full worked example derives the MGF, mean, and variance of the binomial PMF. The **uniqueness property** of the MGF is then stated, which leads into a careful discussion of what "same distribution" means — including a counter-example (number of heads vs. number of tails in 2 tosses of a fair coin) showing $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$ does **not** imply $X = Y$. This motivates the idea of **distribution fitting** (matching an empirical histogram to a theoretical model), and hence the need to study **standard probability models**. The lecture then begins the catalogue of standard discrete distributions: **Bernoulli**, **Binomial** (with its MGF, mean, variance, skewness $\gamma_1$, kurtosis $\gamma_2$, and the classification of shapes by $p$), and **Poisson** (derived as the limiting case of the binomial when $n\to\infty$, $p\to 0$, $np\to\lambda$), including the time-scaled form $\lambda t$ and a worked call-centre example. The session ends with exam logistics.

### 2.2 Definition of the Moment Generating Function (MGF)

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a random variable with PDF/PMF $f_X(x)$. The moment generating function of $X$ is

$$M_X(t) = E\!\left[e^{tX}\right], \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}$$

where $t$ is a (one-)parameter.

Expanding the expectation:

$$
M_X(t) =
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\sum_{x \in S_X} e^{tx} f_X(x) & \text{if } X \text{ is discrete } (S_X = \text{support of } X)\\[2ex]
\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx} f_X(x)\,dx & \text{if } X \text{ is continuous}
\end{cases}
$$

**Existence.**
- The MGF does **not** always exist.
- It is enough for the MGF to exist on an interval, not all of $\mathbb{R}$: we require $|t| < h$ for some $h > 0$, i.e. $-h < t < h$.
- **Crucial condition:** whatever interval is used, it **must contain $0$** (i.e. must include negative values, zero, and positive values).
- If the MGF exists, very strong results follow.

---

### 2.3 Method 1 — Moments from the series expansion

Use the Maclaurin series
$$e^{tx} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(tx)^k}{k!} = 1 + \frac{tx}{1!} + \frac{(tx)^2}{2!} + \cdots$$

Then
$$
M_X(t) = E\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(tx)^k}{k!}\right]
= E\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^k}{k!}X^k\right]
= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^k}{k!}E[X^k]
= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^k}{k!}\,\mu_k'
$$

(using linearity of expectation; $t^k/k!$ is a constant, only $X$ is random), valid for $-h < t < h$.

**Result:** $\mu_k'$ (the $k$-th **moment about the origin**) is the **coefficient of $t^k/k!$** in the expansion of $M_X(t)$.

*Limitation noted in class:* most of the time we cannot easily perform the expansion, hence Method 2.

---

### 2.4 Method 2 — Moments by differentiation

Differentiation and expectation may be interchanged (permissible when the expectation exists).

**First derivative:**
$$\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t) = \frac{d}{dt}E[e^{tX}] = E\!\left[X e^{tX}\right]$$
Set $t = 0$:
$$\left.\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t)\right|_{t=0} = E[X e^{0}] = E[X] = \mu_1'$$

**Second derivative:**
$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t) = E\!\left[X^2 e^{tX}\right]$$
Set $t = 0$:
$$\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t)\right|_{t=0} = E[X^2] = \mu_2'$$

**General formula:**
$$\boxed{\;\mu_k' = \left.\frac{d^k}{dt^k}M_X(t)\right|_{t=0}, \qquad k = 1,2,3,4,\dots\;}$$

- In practice we usually need the **first four moments** (for mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis).
- Advantage: we get mean, variance, and higher moments **without going back to the distribution** each time.

---

### 2.5 Worked Example — MGF, mean and variance of the Binomial PMF

**Given:** $X$ discrete with PMF
$$f(x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n, \quad 0 < p < 1$$

**Step 1 — MGF:**
$$
M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} e^{tx}\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}
= \sum_{x=0}^{n}\binom{n}{x}\left(pe^{t}\right)^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}
$$

Recall the binomial theorem:
$$(a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}a^k b^{\,n-k}$$

With $a = pe^{t}$, $b = 1-p$:
$$\boxed{\;M_X(t) = \left(pe^{t} + 1 - p\right)^{n}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}\;}$$
(valid for all real $t$ since $a,b$ are any numbers).

**Step 2 — Mean:**
$$\frac{d}{dt}M_X(t) = n\left(pe^{t}+1-p\right)^{n-1}\cdot p e^{t}$$
At $t=0$ (using $e^0 = 1$):
$$E[X] = n\,(p + 1 - p)^{n-1}\,p = n\cdot 1^{n-1}\cdot p = np$$
$$\boxed{\;E[X] = \mu_1' = np\;}$$

**Step 3 — Second moment:** differentiate the first derivative $np\,(pe^t+1-p)^{n-1}e^{t}$ by the product rule (first function $(pe^t+1-p)^{n-1}$, second function $e^t$):

$$\frac{d^2}{dt^2}M_X(t) = np\Big[(n-1)\left(pe^{t}+1-p\right)^{n-2}pe^{t}\cdot e^{t} \;+\; \left(pe^{t}+1-p\right)^{n-1}e^{t}\Big]$$

At $t = 0$ (bracket terms $(p+1-p)^{n-2}=1$, $(p+1-p)^{n-1}=1$, $e^0=1$):
$$E[X^2] = \mu_2' = np\big[(n-1)p + 1\big] = n(n-1)p^2 + np$$

**Step 4 — Variance:**
$$\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = \mu_2' - (\mu_1')^2$$
$$= n(n-1)p^2 + np - (np)^2 = n^2p^2 - np^2 + np - n^2p^2$$
$$= np - np^2 = np(1-p)$$
$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p) = npq, \quad q = 1-p\;}$$

*Conclusion:* this PMF is the **binomial distribution** with parameters $(n, p)$; we have derived its MGF, mean and variance.

### 2.6 Uniqueness Property of the MGF

**Statement.** If two random variables $X_1$ and $X_2$ have the same MGF, i.e.
$$M_{X_1}(t) = M_{X_2}(t) \quad \text{for all } t,$$
then **they have the same distribution.**

Consequences / interpretation:
- The MGF is **unique** to a distribution. Two different random variables cannot have the same MGF unless they have the same distribution.
- Practical use: if you compute an MGF in some problem and recognise it as the MGF of a standard distribution (binomial, Poisson, …), you can immediately identify the distribution.
- If $X_1$ is discrete then $X_2$ must be discrete, with the **same support** and the **same PMF**. If $X_1$ is continuous then $X_2$ must be continuous with the **same PDF**.

### 2.7 Equality in Distribution vs. Equality of Random Variables

**Definition (equality in distribution).** If $X$ has CDF $F_X$ and $Y$ has CDF $F_Y$, then $X$ and $Y$ are **equal in distribution**, written
$$X \stackrel{d}{=} Y,$$
if
$$F_X(a) = F_Y(a) \quad \text{for every } a \in \mathbb{R}.$$

**Key logical point:**
- If $X = Y$ (identical random variables) then trivially $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$.
- But $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$ does **NOT** imply $X = Y$. They may take unequal values.

**Counter-example (worked in class): tossing a coin twice**

Setup: toss a coin twice, $P(\text{head}) = p$, $P(\text{tail}) = 1-p = q$.
- $X$ = number of **heads** in 2 trials; $X \in \{0,1,2\}$
- $Y$ = number of **tails** in 2 trials; $Y \in \{0,1,2\}$

PMFs:
$$P(X = x) = \binom{2}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{2-x}, \quad x = 0,1,2$$
$$P(Y = y) = \binom{2}{y}q^{y}(1-q)^{2-y}, \quad y = 0,1,2$$

Now take a **fair coin**, $p = 1/2 \Rightarrow q = 1/2$:
$$P(X = x) = \binom{2}{x}\left(\tfrac12\right)^{x}\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2-x} = \binom{2}{x}\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2}$$
$$P(Y = y) = \binom{2}{y}\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2}$$

Both have the **same PMF**, same support $\{0,1,2\}$, hence $F_X(a) = F_Y(a)$ for all $a\in\mathbb{R}$, i.e. $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$.

**But** by construction $Y = 2 - X$, so $X \ne Y$ as random variables:
- $X=0 \Rightarrow Y=2$
- $X=1 \Rightarrow Y=1$ (here they coincide)
- $X=2 \Rightarrow Y=0$

*Moral:* "same in distribution" only asserts equality of PMF/PDF and CDF — not that the random variables are the same object.

### 2.8 Motivation: Distribution Fitting

- In practice we collect real data $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$.
- We bin the data and plot a **histogram** (frequency plot) → gives an idea of the shape/structure of the **empirical distribution**.
- We compare this with a **standard theoretical model**, e.g. Binomial$(n,p)$, whose shape is known.
- If the two shapes are close, we say the data distribution can be modelled by the theoretical distribution — this is **fitting a distribution**.
- Formal **goodness-of-fit tests** exist to check closeness.
- Therefore: **we must first know the standard probability models** before we can fit them.

### 2.9 Standard Probability Distributions — Classification

Called "standard" because they act as **benchmarks**: when real data resembles one, we pick it, fit it, and then proceed to prediction, estimation, regression, etc.

Two classes:
1. **Discrete standard distributions** — classified by their **PMF**
2. **Continuous standard distributions** — classified by their **PDF**

### 2.10 Bernoulli Distribution

**Origin.** Named after the scientist Bernoulli. Consider an experiment $E$ with an **event of interest** $E$. There are only two possibilities:
- $E$ occurs → probability $P(E) = p$ ("**success**")
- $E$ does not occur → probability $P(E^c) = 1 - p$ ("**failure**")

(The labels "success"/"failure" are just tags; whichever event you are interested in can be called success.)

**Random variable.** $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ with
$$X = \begin{cases}1 & \text{if } E \text{ occurs}\\ 0 & \text{if } E^c \text{ occurs}\end{cases}$$

$$P(X = 1) = p, \qquad P(X = 0) = 1 - p = q$$
with $0 < p < 1$ and total probability $= 1$.

**Compact PMF form:**
$$\boxed{\;P(X = x) = p^{x}(1-p)^{1-x}, \qquad x \in \{0,1\},\ p \in (0,1)\;}$$

Check: $x = 0 \Rightarrow 1-p$; $x = 1 \Rightarrow p$. Both probabilities are combined into one functional form.

---

### 2.11 Binomial Distribution

**Construction.** Repeat the Bernoulli experiment under **identical conditions** $n$ times — these are called **Bernoulli trials**. Define
$$X = \text{number of successes in } n \text{ independent Bernoulli trials, each with the same probability } p \text{ of success.}$$

Support: $x = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n$ where $n$ is any natural number.

**PMF:**
$$\boxed{\;P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}, \qquad x = 0,1,2,\dots,n,\quad p \in (0,1)\;}$$

**Reasoning for the form:** $p^x$ gives the probability of $x$ successes, $(1-p)^{n-x}$ the probability of $n-x$ failures; the successes need not be consecutive (e.g. S, F, S, F, …), so $\binom{n}{x}$ counts the number of ways to choose which $x$ of the $n$ places are successes. Summing all probabilities gives 1.

**Notation:** $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, p)$.

**Relationship to Bernoulli:** $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(1, p)$ **is** the Bernoulli distribution. Bernoulli is a particular case of the binomial with $n = 1$; all properties carry over.

#### Properties of Binomial (summary table)

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| MGF | $M_X(t) = \left(pe^{t} + 1 - p\right)^{n},\ t\in\mathbb{R}$ |
| Mean | $E[X] = np$ |
| Variance | $\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p) = npq$ |
| $\beta_1$ (skewness) | $\beta_1 = \dfrac{(q-p)^2}{npq}$ |
| $\gamma_1$ | $\gamma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = \dfrac{q-p}{\sqrt{npq}}$ |
| $\beta_2$ (kurtosis) | $\beta_2 = 3 + \dfrac{1-6pq}{npq}$ |
| $\gamma_2$ | $\gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3 = \dfrac{1-6pq}{npq}$ |

#### Shape classification by skewness $\gamma_1$

Since $npq > 0$ always, the sign of $\gamma_1$ is the sign of $(q - p)$.

- **Symmetric:** $\gamma_1 = 0 \iff q = p \iff 1 - p = p \iff p = 1/2$
- **Positively skewed:** $\gamma_1 > 0 \iff q > p \iff 1-p > p \iff 2p < 1 \iff p < 1/2$
- **Negatively skewed:** $\gamma_1 < 0 \iff p > 1/2$

Number-line picture:
```
0 -------------- 0.5 -------------- 1
   positively    symmetric   negatively
     skewed                    skewed
```

*Practical use:* if count data (number of successes/failures in an experiment) looks symmetric → fit binomial with $p \approx 1/2$; if positively skewed → $p < 1/2$; if negatively skewed → $p > 1/2$.

#### Shape classification by kurtosis $\gamma_2$

Set $\gamma_2 = 0$:
$$\frac{1-6pq}{npq} = 0 \iff 1 - 6p(1-p) = 0 \iff 6p - 6p^2 = 1 \iff 6p^2 - 6p + 1 = 0$$

Solving this quadratic gives the two roots:
$$p_0 \approx 0.2113, \qquad p_1 \approx 0.7887$$

Classification:
- For $p$ **outside** the interval $(p_0, p_1)$ (i.e. in $(0, 0.2113)$ or $(0.7887, 1)$): **leptokurtic** (peak higher than normal)
- For $p$ **inside** $(0.2113, 0.7887)$: **platykurtic** (peak lower than normal)
- At exactly $p = 0.2113$ or $p = 0.7887$: **mesokurtic** (same height as normal)

*(Note: this was the classification as stated in the lecture — the height of the binomial relative to the normal is either equal to, larger than, or smaller than normal depending on $p$.)*

### 2.12 Poisson Distribution — as a limit of the Binomial

**Limiting setup:**
$$n \to \infty \ (\text{number of trials large}), \qquad p \to 0 \ (\text{success probability small}),$$
$$np \to \lambda \quad \text{where } \lambda \text{ is a finite positive number.}$$
($\lambda$ must be positive because $n > 0$ and $p > 0$; and finite.)

**Practical interpretation:** huge number of trials, each with a very small success probability ("**rare events**"), but a **finite average**.

**Motivating example (given in class):** $X$ = number of accidents on a particular road. A huge number of people cross the road (each crossing = a trial), the probability that any one person has an accident is tiny, but the average number of accidents per day is finite (1, 3, or 5 per day, say).

**Computational problem:** With
$$P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}, \qquad \binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!\,(n-x)!}$$
when $n$ is huge, $n!$ overflows, and $p^x \to 0$ — so this probability is very hard to compute directly.

**Poisson's limit:**
$$\lim_{\substack{n\to\infty,\ p\to 0 \\ np \to \lambda}} \binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x} = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!}$$

This limit is non-negative and sums to 1, so it is a valid PMF.

**Poisson PMF:**
$$\boxed{\;P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!}, \qquad x = 0,1,2,\dots,\infty,\quad \lambda > 0\;}$$

#### MGF of the Poisson

$$
M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} e^{tx}\,\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!}
= e^{-\lambda}\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\lambda e^{t}\right)^{x}}{x!}
= e^{-\lambda}\,e^{\lambda e^{t}}
$$
(using the exponential series $\sum_{x\ge0} z^x/x! = e^z$)

$$\boxed{\;M_X(t) = e^{\lambda\left(e^{t}-1\right)}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}\;}$$

**Uniqueness illustration (worked in class):**
- If $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(3)$, then $M_X(t) = e^{3(e^{t}-1)}$.
- Conversely, if $X$ is discrete with $M_X(t) = e^{\,e^{t}-1}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$, then comparing with the standard Poisson MGF gives $\lambda = 1$, so $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(1)$. This identification is a consequence of the **uniqueness property of the MGF**.

#### Mean and variance of the Poisson

Differentiate the MGF and set $t=0$:
$$\boxed{\;E[X] = \lambda, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda\;}$$

**Intuitive check via the limit:** For $X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$, $E[X] = np \to \lambda$ and $\mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \to \lambda \cdot 1 = \lambda$ (since $p \to 0 \Rightarrow 1-p \to 1$). So mean and variance both converge to $\lambda$ — consistent with the Poisson result.

#### When to use Poisson vs. Binomial (practical)

- **Poisson**: count distribution on $\{0,1,2,\dots,\infty\}$ for **rare events**. Examples given:
  - number of accidents on a road
  - number of patients at a hospital emergency department
  - number of telephone calls at a call centre in a given time period
  - Rationale: many events occur in a specific time period with very low occurrence probability; the binomial calculation is infeasible because $n$ is too large and $p$ too small.
- **Binomial**: use when $n$ is moderate.

### 2.13 Poisson with a time/rate parameter — the $\lambda t$ form

**Important caution raised in class:** $\lambda$ is a **rate per unit time**. The rate must be **aligned with the time period** used in the question.

If $\lambda$ is given as a **per-unit-time rate** and we observe over time $t$, then

$$\boxed{\;P\big(X(t) = x\big) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}\,(\lambda t)^{x}}{x!}\;}$$

Here $\lambda t$ becomes the mean over the interval of length $t$. "That's why we take $\lambda t$, not $\lambda$" — the mean changes with the observation period.

**Worked call-centre example (numbers from class):**
- Rate $\lambda = 6$ calls per minute
- Observation window $t = 10$ minutes
- So $\lambda t = 6 \times 10 = 60$ (expected number of calls in 10 minutes)

Then:
- $P(X = 60) = \dfrac{e^{-60}\,60^{60}}{60!}$
- $P(X = 50) = \dfrac{e^{-60}\,60^{50}}{50!}$

(Student question answered: yes, you can use this to find the probability of a given number of calls in the next 10 minutes — just use $\lambda t$ rather than $\lambda$, and note that the maximum conceivable in 10 minutes at 6/min would be 60 on average, but $x$ ranges over $0,1,2,\dots$.)

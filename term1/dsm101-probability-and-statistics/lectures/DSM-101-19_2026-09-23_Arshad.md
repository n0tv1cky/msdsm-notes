## 1. Overview

This session moves from **discrete** bivariate distributions (finished last time) to **continuous** bivariate random vectors. The lecture covers:

- The definition of a joint PDF, its two defining properties, and how the joint PDF and joint CDF are related.
- Computing probabilities over regions by double integration, with a uniform-on-a-square example.
- Marginal PDFs, and a careful motivation of the **conditional PDF**. The naive definition fails because $P(Y=y)=0$, so a limiting $\varepsilon$-window argument is used instead.
- Two worked examples:
  - a uniform $X$ with a conditional distribution for $Y$, giving marginal $f_Y(y)=-\ln y$;
  - the uniform distribution on the unit disk (marginals, conditionals, and a probability computed by an area ratio).
- **Independence** of random variables: equivalent CDF/PMF/PDF/MGF conditions, the factorization criterion, and the "rectangular support" necessary condition.
- **Expectations** of functions of $(X,Y)$, **product moments** $\mu'_{r,s}$, and **covariance** with its properties and variance-of-linear-combination formulas.
- The key fact that independence implies zero covariance, but not conversely. The counterexample is $X\sim U(-1,1)$, $Y=X^2$.
- Next lecture continues with the correlation coefficient and linear relationships.

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Definition: continuous bivariate random vector and joint PDF

- A bivariate random vector $(X,Y)$ is of **continuous type** if there exists a non-negative function $f(x,y)$ such that, for every $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$:
$$F_{X,Y}(x,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y} f(u,v)\,dv\,du$$
- $f(x,y)$ is then called the **joint probability density function (PDF)** of $(X,Y)$.

- **Properties of a joint PDF** (analogous to the univariate case):
  1. $f(x,y)\ge 0$ for all $(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = 1$.

- **Order of integration** can be swapped because the integral is finite:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$
- Conversely, **any** function satisfying properties 1 and 2 is the joint PDF of some random vector $(X,Y)$.

### 2.2 Relation between joint CDF and joint PDF

- From PDF to CDF: integrate, using the formula in 2.1.
- From CDF to PDF: if $f$ is continuous at $(x,y)$, then
$$f(x,y) = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}F(x,y)$$

### 2.3 Probability of a region

- For any region $A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$:
$$P\big((X,Y)\in A\big) = \iint_{A} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$
- Once the joint PDF is known, any required probability can be computed.

**Worked example (uniform on a square):**
$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\tfrac14, & -1<x<1,\ -1<y<1\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

- The support is the square $[-1,1]\times[-1,1]$, which has area 4. This is a 2-D extension of the uniform distribution.
- Find $P\left(\tfrac14<X<\tfrac34,\ \tfrac14<Y<\tfrac34\right)$:
$$\int_{1/4}^{3/4}\int_{1/4}^{3/4}\tfrac14\,dy\,dx = \tfrac14\cdot\left(\tfrac34-\tfrac14\right)\int_{1/4}^{3/4}dx = \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \frac{1}{16}$$

### 2.4 Marginal PDFs

- To get a marginal, integrate the joint PDF over the *other* variable:
$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dy, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dx$$

### 2.5 Conditional PDF: motivation and definition (flagged as important)

**Why the naive definition fails:**

- In the discrete case, $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$.
- For the continuous case, the tempting definition is
$$F_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \frac{P(X\le x,\ Y=y)}{P(Y=y)}$$
- This is **wrong**, because $P(Y=y)=0$ for a continuous random variable.

**The fix: use a small window around $y$.**

- Take a small $\varepsilon>0$ such that $P(y-\varepsilon<Y\le y+\varepsilon)>0$.
- Then define
$$F_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\frac{P(X\le x,\ y-\varepsilon<Y\le y+\varepsilon)}{P(y-\varepsilon<Y\le y+\varepsilon)} = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_{X\mid Y}(t\mid y)\,dt$$

**Resulting definitions:**

- Conditional PDF of $X$ given $Y=y$:
$$f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}, \quad \text{provided } f_Y(y)>0$$
- Conditional PDF of $Y$ given $X=x$:
$$f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) = \frac{f(x,y)}{f_X(x)}, \quad \text{provided } f_X(x)>0$$
- A density is a *rate*, not a probability; it is a tool for computing probabilities.

**A conditional PDF is itself a PDF.** For a fixed $y$:

1. $f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\ge 0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.
2. $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,dx = 1$.

**Multiplication rule:**
$$f(x,y) = f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x) = f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\,f_Y(y)$$

### 2.6 Worked example: joint from marginal times conditional

**Setup:**

- $X\sim U(0,1)$, so $f_X(x)=1$ for $0<x<1$ and $0$ otherwise.
- For fixed $x\in(0,1)$:
$$f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) = \begin{cases}\dfrac1x, & 0<y<x\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
- **Goal:** find the marginal of $Y$.

**Step 1: joint PDF.**
$$f(x,y) = f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,f_X(x) = \begin{cases}\dfrac1x, & 0<y<x<1\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

**Step 2: marginal of $Y$.**

- For a fixed $y\in(0,1)$, $x$ ranges from $y$ to $1$. (The lecturer first said "$x$ to 1", then corrected it to "$y$ to 1".)
$$f_Y(y) = \int_{y}^{1}\frac1x\,dx = \big[\ln x\big]_y^1 = \ln 1-\ln y = -\ln y$$
- Therefore:
$$f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}-\ln y, & 0<y<1\\ 0, & y\le 0 \text{ or } y\ge 1\end{cases}$$

### 2.7 Worked example: uniform distribution on the unit disk

- $(X,Y)$ is uniform on $D=\{(x,y): x^2+y^2<1\}$.
- Whether the boundary is included does not matter in the continuous case.

**Finding the constant.**

- Uniform means $f(x,y)=c$ on $D$, where $c = \dfrac{1}{\text{area}(D)}$.
- The area is $\pi r^2 = \pi(1)^2=\pi$. (The transcript misstates this once as "1"; the correct value is $\pi$.)
- So:
$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac1\pi, & x^2+y^2<1\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
- **General rule:** uniform on any region means density $=\dfrac{1}{\text{area of region}}$.

**Marginal of $X$.**

- The support of $X$ is $S_X=(-1,1)$.
- For a fixed $x$, the condition $y^2<1-x^2$ gives $-\sqrt{1-x^2}<y<\sqrt{1-x^2}$.
$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\frac1\pi\,dy = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}, \quad -1<x<1$$
- $f_X(x)=0$ otherwise.

**Marginal of $Y$ by symmetry.**

- Since $f(y,x)=f(x,y)$, the density is symmetric in $x$ and $y$. So:
$$f_Y(y)=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-y^2}, \quad -1<y<1$$
- $f_Y(y)=0$ otherwise.

**Conditional of $Y$ given $X=x$** (for fixed $x\in(-1,1)$):
$$f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x) = \frac{1/\pi}{\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \quad -\sqrt{1-x^2}<y<\sqrt{1-x^2}$$

- Equivalently, the range is $|y|<\sqrt{1-x^2}$.
- So $Y\mid X=x$ is uniform on $\left(-\sqrt{1-x^2},\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)$.
- Each value of $x$ gives a different conditional distribution (for example $x=0$ versus $x$ near $\pm1$).

**Probability via area ratio.**

- Find $P\left(X^2+Y^2\le\tfrac14\right)$. This is the disk of radius $\tfrac12$.
$$P = \frac{\text{area}(A)}{\text{area}(D)} = \frac{\pi(1/2)^2}{\pi(1)^2} = \frac{\pi/4}{\pi} = \frac14$$
- **Tip:** for uniform densities, probability = (area of required region) / (total area). This is the easiest method.

### 2.8 Independent random variables

**Definition.**

- $X$ and $Y$ are **independent** if and only if, for all $(x,y)$:
$$F_{X,Y}(x,y) = F_X(x)\,F_Y(y)$$
- This definition applies to any type of random variable.

**Equivalent conditions** (any one suffices):

| Setting | Condition |
|---|---|
| Discrete | $p_{X,Y}(x,y) = p_X(x)\,p_Y(y)$ for all $x,y$ |
| Continuous | $f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$ for all $x,y$ |
| MGF | $M_{X,Y}(t_1,t_2) = M_X(t_1)\,M_Y(t_2)$ for all $t_1\in(-h_1,h_1)$, $t_2\in(-h_2,h_2)$ |

**Factorization criterion.**

- If $f(x,y)=g(x)\,h(y)$ for all $x,y$, with $g$ a function of $x$ only and $h$ a function of $y$ only, then $X$ and $Y$ are independent.
- $g$ and $h$ need **not** be PDFs or PMFs themselves. They may be constant multiples of them, i.e. non-normalized.
- You do not need to compute the marginals to check independence.

**Examples:**

1. $f(x,y)=\dfrac1x$ on $0<y<x<1$ (from 2.6; stated in lecture as "$0<x<y<1$").
   - The support ties $x$ and $y$ together, so they cannot be separated.
   - **Not independent.**
2. $f(x,y)=x+y$ on $0<x<1,\ 0<y<1$.
   - This is a valid joint PDF, and its support is rectangular.
   - But $x+y$ cannot be written as $g(x)h(y)$.
   - **Not independent.**
3. Joint PMF $p(x,y)=\dfrac{xy^2}{30}$ for $x=1,2,3$ and $y=1,2$.
   - Check that it sums to 1: $\frac{(1+2+3)(1+4)}{30}=1$.
   - The ranges are not linked, and $g(x)=\frac{x}{30}$, $h(y)=y^2$ give $p(x,y)=g(x)h(y)$.
   - **Independent.**
4. $f(x,y)=c\,\dfrac{x}{y}$ on $0<x<y<1$.
   - The function is separable, but the support is triangular.
   - **Not independent.**

**Discussion points (student Q&A):**

- A product or quotient form like $xy$ or $x/y$ is separable.
- Sums and differences ($x+y$, $x-y$) are generally not separable.
  - Occasionally, an expression with sums simplifies to a product form.
- **The range must also be separable.**
- **Rectangular support is necessary but not sufficient** for independence.
  - Triangular or circular supports imply dependence.
  - Example 2 shows a rectangular support that is still not independent.
- **Procedure:**
  1. Check whether the support is rectangular.
  2. Check whether the function factorizes.
  3. If both hold, $X$ and $Y$ are independent; otherwise they are not.

### 2.9 Expectation of a function of $(X,Y)$

- For a function $g(X,Y)$:
$$E[g(X,Y)] = \begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_x\sum_y g(x,y)\,p(x,y), & (X,Y)\text{ discrete}\\[2ex] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x,y)\,f(x,y)\,dy\,dx, & (X,Y)\text{ continuous}\end{cases}$$
- This extends in the same way to any fixed dimension.

### 2.10 Product moments

- The **$(r,s)$-th product moment about the origin** is:
$$\mu'_{r,s} = E\left[X^r Y^s\right], \quad r,s=0,1,2,\dots$$

| Moment | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $\mu'_{1,0}=E[X]=\mu_X$ | mean of $X$ |
| $\mu'_{0,1}=E[Y]=\mu_Y$ | mean of $Y$ |
| $\mu'_{1,1}=E[XY]$ | first product moment |
| $\mu'_{2,0}=E[X^2]$ | second moment of $X$ |

- As in the univariate case, we are interested in the mean, variance, skewness and kurtosis of each variable.
- The new ingredient is **joint behaviour**, measured by covariance.

### 2.11 Covariance

**Definition:**
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = E\big[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)\big]$$

**Derivation of the computational formula:**
$$E[XY - X\mu_Y - Y\mu_X + \mu_X\mu_Y] = E[XY] - \mu_Y E[X] - \mu_X E[Y] + \mu_X\mu_Y = E[XY]-\mu_X\mu_Y$$

**Computational formula:**
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY]-E[X]\,E[Y] = \mu'_{1,1}-\mu'_{1,0}\,\mu'_{0,1}$$

**Properties:**

1. $\operatorname{Cov}(X,X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2=\operatorname{Var}(X)$.
2. $\operatorname{Cov}(aX+b,\ cY+d) = ac\,\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$. Added constants have no effect.
3. $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1+X_2,\ Y) = \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,Y)+\operatorname{Cov}(X_2,Y)$. Signs carry through.
4. Bilinear expansion:
$$\operatorname{Cov}(aX_1+bX_2,\ cY_1+dY_2) = ac\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,Y_1)+ad\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,Y_2)+bc\operatorname{Cov}(X_2,Y_1)+bd\operatorname{Cov}(X_2,Y_2)$$

**Variance of a linear combination:**
$$\operatorname{Var}(aX+bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)+b^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)+2ab\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$$

- Special case:
$$\operatorname{Var}(X\pm Y)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(Y)\pm2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)$$
- The variance terms are always positive; only the covariance term takes the sign.

### 2.12 Independence implies zero covariance, but not conversely

**Independence implies zero covariance.** If $X$ and $Y$ are independent:
$$E[XY]=\int\!\!\int xy\,f_X(x)f_Y(y)\,dy\,dx = \left(\int x f_X(x)\,dx\right)\left(\int y f_Y(y)\,dy\right)=E[X]\,E[Y]$$

- Hence $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$.

**The converse is false.**

- **Counterexample:** $X\sim U(-1,1)$ and $Y=X^2$. These are clearly dependent by construction.
$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E[X\cdot X^2]-E[X]\,E[X^2] = E[X^3]-E[X]\,E[X^2]$$
- For $U(a,b)$, $E[X]=\frac{a+b}{2}=\frac{-1+1}{2}=0$. So the second term is 0, and $E[X^2]$ need not be computed.
- The first term is also 0:
$$E[X^3]=\int_{-1}^{1}x^3\cdot\tfrac12\,dx=\tfrac12\left[\tfrac{x^4}{4}\right]_{-1}^{1}=0$$
  - This holds because $x^3$ is an odd function on a symmetric interval. The lecturer said "even", but it is $x^4$ that is even, which is why the evaluation cancels.
- Therefore $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=0$, even though $Y=X^2$ depends on $X$.

**Takeaway:** covariance (and the correlation coefficient, coming next lecture) only measures **linear** relationships. It cannot detect nonlinear dependence.

## 1. Overview

This session completes the treatment of **transformations of random variables** for the *continuous* case and then opens the next major block: **expectation and moments of a distribution**. The first half presents two techniques for finding the distribution of $Y = g(X)$ when $X$ is continuous — the general **distribution function (CDF) technique** and the restricted **Jacobian of transformation technique** (valid only when $g$ is one-to-one on the support of $X$) — each illustrated with a worked example using $Y = X^2$. A key conceptual point is stressed: if $X$ is continuous, $Y$ may turn out continuous, discrete, or mixed, so the continuity of $F_Y$ must always be checked before differentiating. The second half defines the **expected value** of a random variable (discrete and continuous cases), the **existence condition** $E|X| < \infty$, with a divergent example (a $3/(\pi^2x^2)$ PMF) and a convergent one (a Laplace-type density solved using the **Gamma function**). It closes by defining $E[g(X)]$, **raw moments** $\mu_r' = E(X^r)$ and **central moments** $\mu_r = E[(X-\mu_1')^r]$, explaining what the first four moments measure (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis), and deriving the **unit-free coefficients** $\beta_1, \beta_2, \gamma_1, \gamma_2$ used to classify skewness and kurtosis.

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Setup: transformation of a continuous random variable
- Let $X$ be a **continuous type** random variable with PDF $f_X$ (or CDF $F_X$).
- $S_X$ = **support** of $X$. For a continuous r.v. the support is *not* a set of discrete points — it is a subset of $\mathbb{R}$ made up of **intervals**, e.g. $(1,2)$, $(0,\infty)$, $(-\infty,\infty)$.
- Given $Y = g(X)$, the goal: find the PDF / CDF of $Y$.
- **First step always: find $S_Y$**, the support of $Y$, then proceed with the calculation.

### 2.2 The two methods
| Method | Scope | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Distribution function technique** | **General** — any function $g$ | Start from the CDF of $Y$, then differentiate to get the PDF |
| **Jacobian of transformation** | **Restricted** — only if $g$ is **one–one** (a bijection between $X$ and $Y$ on the support) | Go directly from PDF of $X$ to PDF of $Y$ |

- Name "distribution function technique" comes from the fact that we **begin with the distribution (CDF) of $Y$**.

---

### 2.3 Worked Example 1 — Distribution function technique

**Given:** $X$ continuous with
$$f_X(x)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12, & -1<x<1\\[2pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
**Transformation:** $Y = X^2$. **Find** the PDF of $Y$.

**Step 1 — Support.**
$S_X = (-1,1)$. Under $Y = X^2$ (a parabola through the origin), $S_Y = (0,1)$.
- Whether $0$ is included or not does not matter for probability, because for a continuous r.v. $P(Y=0)=0$.
- (Discussed in Q&A: writing $[0,1)$ or $(0,1)$ is both acceptable; instructor's final preference for the PDF is the **open interval $(0,1)$**, because at $y=0$ the density blows up.)

**Step 2 — CDF, case by case.**

*Case (i): $y < 0$*
$$F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=0$$
because $y$ lies below (to the left of) the support — no probability there.

*Case (ii): $y \ge 1$*
$$F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=1$$
all values are exhausted, so total probability = 1.

*Case (iii): $0 \le y < 1$*
$$F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)=P(X^2\le y)=P\!\left(-\sqrt{y}\le X\le \sqrt{y}\right)$$
(The pre-images of $y$ under the parabola are the two points $-\sqrt{y}$ and $+\sqrt{y}$.)
Since $0<y<1 \Rightarrow 0<\sqrt y<1$, these limits lie inside $S_X$, so:
$$F_Y(y)=\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}\tfrac12\,dx=\tfrac12\Big[x\Big]_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}=\tfrac12\left(\sqrt y+\sqrt y\right)=\tfrac{2\sqrt y}{2}=\sqrt y$$

**Result — CDF:**
$$F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0, & y<0\\ \sqrt y, & 0\le y<1\\ 1, & y\ge 1\end{cases}$$

**Step 3 — Check continuity of $F_Y$ (compulsory step).**
- On $(0,1)$: $y^{1/2}$ is a polynomial-type / power function → continuous.
- On $y<0$: constant $0$ → continuous. On $y\ge1$: constant $1$ → continuous.
- At $y=0$: $F_Y(0)=\sqrt0=0$, left limit $=0$, right limit $=0$ → all three agree → continuous.
- At $y=1$: value $=1$, $F_Y(1^-)=\sqrt1=1$, $F_Y(1^+)=1$ → continuous.
- $\Rightarrow F_Y$ is continuous for **every** $y\in\mathbb{R}$ $\Rightarrow$ $Y$ **is a continuous type random variable.**

**Step 4 — Differentiate to get the PDF.**
$$f_Y(y)=\frac{d}{dy}F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt y}, & 0<y<1\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
(derivative of $\sqrt y = \tfrac12 y^{-1/2}$; derivatives of the constants $0$ and $1$ are $0$).

**Note on strict vs. non-strict inequalities:** in the CDF we wrote $0\le y<1$, but in the PDF we write $0<y<1$, because at $y=0$, $1/(2\sqrt y)=\infty$. Convention used in the course:
- **Densities:** write with **strict inequalities** (avoid the corner points).
- **CDFs:** write in the **right-continuous** form (function value = value from the plus side).

### 2.4 General rule after obtaining $F_Y$ (very important)
- If $X$ is **discrete**, $Y=g(X)$ is always discrete.
- If $X$ is **continuous**, $Y$ can be **continuous, discrete, or mixed** — all three are possible. Hence continuity of $F_Y$ *must* be checked.
- Algorithm after computing $F_Y$:
  - $F_Y$ continuous everywhere → $Y$ is continuous; differentiate to get $f_Y$.
  - $F_Y$ has jumps → compute the **jump sizes**:
    - **Total jump size = 1** → $Y$ is **discrete type**.
    - **Total jump size < 1** (jumps in some places, continuous pieces elsewhere) → $Y$ is **mixed type**.

---

### 2.5 Jacobian of transformation technique — the 4-step algorithm

**Assumption:** $Y=g(X)$ is a **one–one** function (on $S_X$), and $g$ continuous.

1. **Find the support of $Y$**, $S_Y$.
2. **Solve for the inverse function:** from $y=g(x)$ get $x=g^{-1}(y)$.
3. **Compute the derivative** $\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\dfrac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)$.
4. **Write the PDF of $Y$ directly:**
$$f_Y(y)=f_X\!\left(g^{-1}(y)\right)\cdot\left|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|,\qquad y\in S_Y$$
The factor $\left|\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|$ is the **Jacobian of the transformation** (hence the name). The **absolute value** is used because the derivative can be negative while a density must be non-negative.

---

### 2.6 Worked Example 2 — Jacobian technique

**Given:**
$$f_X(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-x}, & x>0\\ 0,& \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$
**Transformation:** $Y=X^2$.

- Here $S_X=(0,\infty)$. On this support $y=x^2$ **is one–one** (it is not one–one on all of $\mathbb R$, but it is on the support) → assumption satisfied.

**Step 1 — Support:** $x\in(0,\infty)\Rightarrow y=x^2\in(0,\infty)$, so $S_Y=(0,\infty)$ (unchanged).

**Step 2 — Inverse:** $y=x^2 \Rightarrow x=\sqrt y$.
The root $x=-\sqrt y$ is **discarded** because the function is one–one (unique inverse image) and $-\sqrt y \notin S_X$.

**Step 3 — Derivative:**
$$\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)=\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt y=\tfrac12 y^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$$

**Step 4 — PDF:**
$$f_Y(y)=f_X(\sqrt y)\left|\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}\right| = e^{-\sqrt y}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}$$
$$\boxed{f_Y(y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt y}\,e^{-\sqrt y}, & y>0\\[6pt] 0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}}$$

**Advantage:** no need to compute the CDF or check continuity — if $g$ is one–one and continuous, $Y$ is automatically continuous, and we go PDF → PDF directly.
**Limitation:** restrictive; only applies to one–one $g$ on the support.

## 3. Expectation of a Random Variable

### 3.1 Definition
Let $X$ be a random variable with $f_X$ = its PMF (discrete) or PDF (continuous). Then
$$E(X)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S_X} x\, f_X(x), & X \text{ discrete with PMF } f_X\\[10pt] \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, f_X(x)\,dx, & X \text{ continuous with PDF } f_X\end{cases}$$
— **provided $E(X)$ exists.**

### 3.2 Existence condition
$$E(X) \text{ exists} \iff E|X| < \infty$$
i.e. check the **absolute** series / integral:
- Discrete: $\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S_X}|x|\,f_X(x)<\infty$
- Continuous: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|\,f_X(x)\,dx<\infty$

**Important convention clarified in class (student question):** the modulus is applied **only to the value / the function of $x$**, never to the PMF/PDF — because $f_X\ge 0$ always. So $E|X| = \sum |x| f_X(x)$, *not* $\sum |x| f_X(|x|)$. More generally $E[g(X)] = \sum g(x) f_X(x)$ — the density argument is never altered.

---

### 3.3 Worked Example 3 — expectation that does **not** exist (discrete)

**Given PMF:**
$$f_X(x)=\frac{3}{\pi^2 x^2},\qquad x=\pm1,\pm2,\pm3,\dots;\quad 0 \text{ otherwise}$$

**Check existence:**
$$E|X|=\sum_{x\in S_X}|x|\,f_X(x)=\sum_{x\in S_X}|x|\cdot\frac{3}{\pi^2x^2}$$
The summand is an **even function**, so the negative half and positive half contribute equally:
$$=2\sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x\cdot\frac{3}{\pi^2x^2}=\frac{6}{\pi^2}\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}$$

**p-series test (standard result from mathematics):**
$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^p}=\begin{cases}\infty, & p=1 \ (\text{divergent — harmonic series})\\ \text{finite}, & p\ge 2 \ (\text{e.g. } \sum 1/n^2 \text{ converges})\end{cases}$$
Here $p=1 \Rightarrow$ the series diverges.

$$\therefore\ E|X|=\infty \ \Rightarrow\ E(X) \textbf{ does not exist.}$$

*(Comment: for most standard distributions the expectation is finite; this is a counter-example.)*

---

### 3.4 Worked Example 4 — expectation of a continuous r.v. (uses the Gamma function)

**Given:**
$$f_X(x)=\frac{e^{-|x|}}{2},\qquad -\infty<x<\infty$$

**Step A — Check existence:**
$$E|X|=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|\,\frac{e^{-|x|}}{2}\,dx = 2\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,\frac{e^{-x}}{2}\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,e^{-x}\,dx$$
(even function, so $(-\infty,0)$ and $(0,\infty)$ contribute equally; the 2's cancel.)

**Gamma function (standard result used to avoid integration by parts):**
$$\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\,dx,\qquad \alpha>0$$
and if $\alpha$ is a **positive integer** ($\alpha\in\mathbb{N}$),
$$\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)!$$
*(The range of the integral must be $0$ to $\infty$ for this form.)*

Here the exponent is $x^{1}=x^{2-1}$, so $\alpha = 2$:
$$E|X| = \Gamma(2) = (2-1)! = 1! = 1 < \infty$$
$$\therefore\ E(X) \textbf{ exists.}$$
*(Alternative: integrate by parts — same answer, 1.)*

**Step B — Compute $E(X)$:**
$$E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\cdot\frac{e^{-|x|}}{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,e^{-|x|}\,dx$$
The integrand $x\,e^{-|x|}$ is an **odd function** of $x$ over the **symmetric range** $(-\infty,\infty)$:
$$\Rightarrow\ E(X)=0$$
So the **mean of this distribution is 0**. ($E(X)$ is called the **mean** of the distribution.)

---

### 3.5 Expectation of a function of $X$
Instead of first finding the distribution of $Y=g(X)$ (lengthy) and then its mean, use directly:
$$E[Y]=E[g(X)]=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{x\in S_X} g(x)\,f_X(x), & X \text{ discrete}\\[10pt]\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g(x)\,f_X(x)\,dx, & X \text{ continuous}\end{cases}$$
Only $x$ is replaced by $g(x)$; the density/PMF is **unchanged**.

## 4. Moments of a Distribution

### 4.1 Raw moments (moments about the origin, i.e. about 0)
For $r = 1,2,3,4,\dots$
$$\mu_r' = E\left(X^r\right)$$
- $r=1$: $\mu_1' = E(X)$ = **mean**
- $r=2$: $E(X^2)$, $r=3$: $E(X^3)$, $r=4$: $E(X^4)$
- Defined mathematically for all $r$, but **in practice only the first four are of interest.**

### 4.2 Central moments (moments about the mean)
$$\mu_r = E\Big[\big(X - E(X)\big)^r\Big] = E\Big[\big(X-\mu_1'\big)^r\Big]$$
(Note the notation: **dash = about origin**, **no dash = about mean**.)

**First central moment is always zero:**
$$\mu_1 = E[X-E(X)] = E(X)-E(X)=0$$
because expectation is a **linear operator** and $E(X)$ is a **constant** (expectation converts a random quantity into a number). Hence $\mu_1$ carries no information and is never used.

### 4.3 The four quantities actually used
| Quantity | Symbol | Measures |
|---|---|---|
| 1st raw moment | $\mu_1'$ | **Mean** (location) |
| 2nd central moment | $\mu_2$ | **Variance** (dispersion) |
| 3rd central moment | $\mu_3$ | **Skewness** (lack of symmetry) |
| 4th central moment | $\mu_4$ | **Kurtosis** (peakedness) |

**Why four?** Together, the first four moments give the **structure/shape of the data distribution** without going into full detail.

### 4.4 Variance ($\mu_2$) — interpretation
$$\mu_2 = E\big[(X-\mu_1')^2\big]$$
- Measures **average squared deviation from the mean**.
- $\mu_2 \ge 0$, and strictly $>0$ in general (squares are used precisely because the *unsquared* deviation $\mu_1$ is always 0).
- Larger $\mu_2$ → data more spread out / flatter; smaller $\mu_2$ → data more concentrated.
- $\mu_2 = 0$ ⟺ distribution is **degenerate** at the point $\mu_1'$ (all data sit exactly at the mean).

### 4.5 Skewness ($\mu_3$) — interpretation
Skewness = **lack of symmetry** of the distribution curve.
- $\mu_3 = 0$ → data are **symmetric**
- $\mu_3 > 0$ → **positively skewed** (also called *left-skewed* in the terminology used in class)
- $\mu_3 < 0$ → **negatively skewed** (also called *right-skewed* in the terminology used in class)

The **sign** of $\mu_3$ identifies the direction of skewness.

### 4.6 Kurtosis ($\mu_4$) — interpretation
Kurtosis = **peakedness** of the distribution.
- Standard/moderate peak ⟶ the **normal distribution** is taken as the reference standard: **mesokurtic**
- More peaked than normal ⟶ **leptokurtic**
- Flatter than normal ⟶ **platykurtic**

*(Analogy given in class: calling a person "tall" requires a reference standard; the normal curve is our reference standard for peakedness.)*

## 5. Unit-free Coefficients of Skewness and Kurtosis

### 5.1 Why normalise? (the scaling problem — worked illustration)
Suppose $X$ is measured in **centimetres**, and $\mu_3 = E[(X-E(X))^3]$ (a population formula; an analogous sample formula exists).
Now re-measure in **metres**: $Y = X/100$. Then
$$\mu_3^* = E\big[(Y-E(Y))^3\big]=E\left[\left(\frac{X}{100}-E\!\left(\frac{X}{100}\right)\right)^{3}\right]=\frac{1}{10^{6}}\,E\big[(X-E(X))^3\big]=\frac{\mu_3}{10^{6}}$$
So the sign is the same but the **numerical value changes drastically with the unit** (cm³ vs m³). ⟹ we need **unit-free (dimensionless) numbers**.

### 5.2 Beta coefficients
$$\beta_1 = \frac{\mu_3^{\,2}}{\mu_2^{\,3}} \qquad\text{(skewness)}$$
$$\beta_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^{\,2}} \qquad\text{(kurtosis)}$$
Reason for the powers: $\mu_3$ has unit (cm)³, so $\mu_3^2$ has (cm)⁶; $\mu_2$ has (cm)², so $\mu_2^3$ has (cm)⁶ → the ratio is **unit-free**. Same balancing logic for $\beta_2$: $\mu_4 \sim$ (cm)⁴, $\mu_2^2 \sim$ (cm)⁴.

**Defect of $\beta_1$:** $\beta_1 \ge 0$ always (it is a square over a positive quantity). So
- $\mu_3=0 \Rightarrow \beta_1=0$ (symmetric)
- $\mu_3 \ne 0 \Rightarrow \beta_1 > 0$ — **cannot distinguish positive from negative skew.**

### 5.3 Gamma coefficients (the preferred measures in this course)
$$\gamma_1 = \sqrt{\beta_1} = \frac{\mu_3}{\mu_2^{\,3/2}}$$
- Retains the **sign of $\mu_3$**, and is unit-free.
- $\gamma_1 = 0$ → symmetric; $\gamma_1 > 0$ → positively skewed; $\gamma_1 < 0$ → negatively skewed.
- **This is the more appropriate measure of skewness and the one used in this class.**

$$\gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3$$
- For a **normal distribution, $\beta_2 = 3$ exactly** — this is where the "3" comes from (asked explicitly in Q&A). It is the reference standard.
- Classification:

| Curve | $\beta_2$ | $\gamma_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| **Mesokurtic** (normal peak) | $\beta_2 = 3$ | $\gamma_2 = 0$ |
| **Leptokurtic** (more peaked) | $\beta_2 > 3$ | $\gamma_2 > 0$ |
| **Platykurtic** (flatter) | $\beta_2 < 3$ | $\gamma_2 < 0$ |

Subtracting 3 makes the classification centre on **zero**, which is easier to read.

## 6. Flagged as Important (exams / assignments / study advice)

- **Must check continuity of $F_Y$** before concluding that $Y$ is a continuous r.v. Explicitly called "important" in lecture. If it is not continuous, identify the jumps and use the total-jump-size rule (=1 → discrete; <1 → mixed).
- **Jacobian technique is only valid for one–one $g$ on the support** — stated as the crucial assumption; must be verified (as in Example 2, where $x^2$ is one–one on $(0,\infty)$ though not on $\mathbb{R}$).
- **Existence of expectation must be verified via $E|X|<\infty$** before computing $E(X)$.
- **Writing conventions:** densities with strict inequalities; CDFs in right-continuous form. In Example 1 the point $y=0$ must be excluded from the density (else $1/(2\sqrt y)=\infty$).
- **Formulas to memorise** (explicitly stated in Q&A that these are standard definitions that cannot be re-derived each time and **need to be memorised**):
  - $\beta_1 = \mu_3^2/\mu_2^3$, $\beta_2 = \mu_4/\mu_2^2$, $\gamma_1 = \mu_3/\mu_2^{3/2}$, $\gamma_2 = \beta_2-3$
  - $\beta_2 = 3$ for the normal distribution.
- **Useful standard results to have ready:**
  - $p$-series: $\sum_{n\ge1} n^{-p}$ diverges for $p=1$, converges for $p\ge2$.
  - Gamma function $\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}dx$, and $\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)!$ for positive integer $\alpha$ — this is a shortcut that avoids integration by parts and is expected to be used.
  - Odd function over a symmetric range integrates to 0; even function → twice the half-range integral.
- **Course style note:** the instructor will **skip theorem proofs** (mixed-background class) and focus on results + computation — but students are responsible for the results/formulas.

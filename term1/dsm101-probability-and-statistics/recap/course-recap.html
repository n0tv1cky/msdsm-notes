Why probability: inverse problems and two schools

Conceptual framing; not examined directly, but it's the vocabulary for everything after.

The inverse problem

forward problem
Model + parameters known → predict data. Easy ("two lines of Python" for a fair coin).
inverse problem
Data (effect) known → infer model/cause. Hard: different causes explain the same data.
a theory earns credibility
by surviving independent datasets across scales.

Three types of inverse problem

1 · hypothesis testing
Is a statement consistent with data? ("markets always crash")
2 · parameter estimation
What value does θ take? (TED spread threshold before 2008)
3 · model selection
Which of several fitting models? Hardest — needs the evidence term.

Where uncertainty comes from

  • Noise in the measurement
  • Intrinsic randomness (no two petals alike)
  • Designed randomness of complex systems (lottery drum)
  • Epistemic stance: causes unknown (age of universe ± error). Aleatory: games of chance, "40% rain tomorrow".
FrequentistBayesian
Probability isrelative frequency over an infinite ensemble of identical experimentsdegree of belief of an observer, given their information
Natureobjectivesubjective (observer-dependent, but grounded in data)
Outputa point value per parameter (best-fit $m, c$)a distribution (posterior) per parameter, with widths
One-off eventsbreaks down (needs hypothetical ensemble)fine (life on Mars, Drake equation)
New datarefit; estimate may jump (bias–variance)update; old posterior becomes new prior
PeopleBoole, Venn, Fisher, von MisesBayes, Laplace, Keynes, Jeffreys, Cox, Jaynes

Majumdar's pictures of the inverse problem:

  • Newton's apple: "gravity" and "magic" both explain one falling apple; only gravity survives the Earth–Sun, galaxy and cluster scales.
  • Tycho's Mars: naked-eye declination data (1582–1600) → Kepler's ellipses. Seeing ellipses in a wiggly plot is the hard part. Dots are data; a curve is a model or denser data — never just join the dots.
  • Hungry shopper: "empty stomach → more chips and Coke" is a bias until you survey hungry vs not-hungry buyers.
  • The ant and the finger: from limited data the ant infers a giant tooth; new data (squashed, or climbs over) forces an update — Bayes foreshadowed.

Why the Bayesian answer differs — "you always have more prior information than you think":

  • Supernova: 4 naked-eye supernovae in 10 centuries → frequentist 4/10 = 40%; see one in 1987 → 5/10 = 50%. Ignores who was looking up, population, industrialisation, night-sky darkness.
  • Bus No. 15: 3 buses in the last hour → "every 20 min". Bayesian: peaked at 20 but 15–25 possible, because you know about night schedules, jams, accidents ("latent observations").
  • Magician's coin / caveman and rain: same proposition, different information → different degrees of belief.
  • Straight-line fit: same model $y=mx+c$; frequentist gives best-fit $m,c$, Bayesian gives posteriors — like a standard ANN vs a Bayesian neural net.
  • History flip-flops: Pascal & Fermat (1654, a gambler's split-the-stakes question) built the calculus; Bernoulli's insufficient reason gives each of $n$ outcomes $1/n$ but can't update; Bayes supplied the update; Gauss gave least squares, Gaussian errors and the CLT.
  • Updating never ends, but it isn't a loop: Sun around Earth → ellipses → non-coplanar spirals; Newton → general relativity (GPS needs it). More data, sharper model.

Probability rules and Bayes' theorem

Write the conditional probability you want, expand it with Bayes, and never drop the prior.

The calculus of probability

bounds · sum rule
$0\le P\le1$, $\;\sum_i P(i)=1$, $\;\int P(x)\,dx=1$
product rule
$P(X,Y)=P(X\mid Y)P(Y)=P(Y\mid X)P(X)$
independent
$P(X,Y)=P(X)P(Y)$ — only then multiply marginals
mutually exclusive
$P(x_1\cup\dots\cup x_n)=\sum P(x_i)$

Total probability → Bayes

total probability
$P(E)=\sum_i P(E\mid A_i)P(A_i)$
Bayes (two hypotheses)
$$P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E\mid H)P(H)+P(E\mid H^c)P(H^c)}$$
the maths is trivial
It's a rearranged product rule. Bayes' contribution was the interpretation: a way to update belief.

Inference form

$P(\theta\mid D,I,M)=\dfrac{P(D\mid\theta,I,M)\,P(\theta\mid I,M)}{P(D\mid I,M)}$
posterior
belief about θ after the data — what you want
likelihood
P(data | θ); product over independent data points only (apples from different, randomly chosen trees)
prior
everything known before the experiment
evidence
$\int P(D\mid\theta)P(\theta)\,d\theta$ — all ways the data could arise

Working rules

  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior for estimation and testing. Need the evidence only for model selection.
  • θ is meaningless without its model $M$; I and M are always silently conditioned on.
  • Flat prior $P(\theta)=$ const when you know nothing.

Updating

$P(\theta\mid D_1,D_2)\propto P(D_2\mid\theta)\,P(\theta\mid D_1)$
Yesterday's posterior is today's prior. Old data is never thrown away (needs $D_1, D_2$ independent).
prior-sensitivity test
If a different reasonable prior changes the answer a lot → you need more data.

Traps

  • $P(C\mid G)+P(C\mid G^c)\ne1$ in general. Only $P(C\mid G)+P(C^c\mid G)=1$.
  • Dropping implicit conditioning ("you chose door $a$", "a theft did happen").
  • Rule 1: write down exactly what you want to know. "There are no rules for $n>1$."

The prior is what intuition forgets (Majumdar, S7–S8, S10):

  • Steve, shy and tidy (Kahneman & Tversky): "librarian" feels right, but farmers outnumber librarians 20:1 → only 16.7% librarian. Our brains evolved for reflex "thinking fast" and skip the base rate.
  • Repeat offenders: even with a 50% re-offence rate, 80% of new crimes come from people with no record. Ignoring the prior → biased action → social disorder.
  • COVID testing: a "99% effective" test with 1% false positives at 0.5% prevalence is right only 1 in 3 times — hence rapid test, then a slow low-FPR confirmation.
  • MCQ exams: with 5 options, a correct answer means 5/6 "knew it" — why objective exams are trustworthy.

Samples, histograms and density

We only ever hold a finite sample; statistics compress it and estimate the population.

Population vs sample

population
infinite, holds the truth; never accessible
sample
finite $n$ draws; gives estimates (finite sums), not calculations (integrals)
statistic
any summary of a finite sample, no model assumed: mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, correlation

Histogram → PDF

  • Equal-width bins; height = count in the bin ∝ probability density.
  • Smooth the bin tops → approximate PDF ("a poor person's PDF").
  • Hard edges, empty bins; converges to the true PDF as $n$ grows.
  • Try several bin widths — the apparent shape changes.

Density, joint, marginal

density
probability mass per unit length/area/volume, e.g. $\dfrac{dB}{dH\,dW}$
"average density"
$=1/V$ — meaningless; only local density informs
marginalise
$P(X)=\int P(X,Y)\,dY$ — integrate out what you don't want
joint needed when
the observable depends on several variables (BMI from $h,w$)

How many samples are enough?

  • Plot the running estimate (mean, variance) against $n$; enough when it plateaus.
  • Coin: 102 flips → 0.49/0.51. Die: running mean settles near $3.5$ by ~100 rolls.
  • Cards 1–10: true variance $\frac{10^2-1}{12}=8.25$ (quoted ≈8.5 in class); simulation 8.32 at ~300 draws.
  • Few outcomes → hundreds suffice; high-dimensional → far more.

Always quote the error

  • Continuous values are never exact: a 1 cm tape → $\pm1$ cm.
  • Report $m\pm\Delta m$, $c\pm\Delta c$ — clients can't judge a strategy without it.
  • For continuous $X$: $P(X=a)=0$; ask for $P(a\le X\le a+dx)$ instead.

Majumdar's pictures:

  • Five points, eight numbers: joining 5 points with 4 segments needs 8 parameters (no compression); one fitted line needs 2. A client with a million Netflix users wants the trend, not the scatter.
  • Bins are boxes: sort every number from a bag into its box, then count.
  • Mountainous cake: $P(h,w)$ is a 3-D mountain drawn as contours; cutting a slab out of it is a joint probability.
  • More dead than living: the "true" BMI population would need everyone who ever lived — the population is effectively infinite.
  • LLM training set = a finite sample; a good one represents the underlying distribution. Insurance premiums and sample surveys (when the census is late) hinge on sampling strategy.

Random variables: PMF, PDF, CDF and transformations

Read a CDF, classify the variable, recover the PMF/PDF, find unknown constants, transform.

Definitions

random variable
$X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$; support $S=X(\Omega)$
PMF (discrete)
$p(x)\ge0$, $\sum_{S}p(x)=1$ — values are probabilities
PDF (continuous)
$f\ge0$, $\int f=1$ — not a probability; can exceed 1 ($f=2$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$)
link
$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$, $\;f=F'$ (ignore finitely many kinks; set 0 there)

CDF $F(x)=P(X\le x)$

  • Defined on all of ℝ: write it piecewise, never as a 3-column table.
  • Valid CDF ⇔ (1) non-decreasing, (2) right-continuous $F(a)=F(a^+)$, (3) $F(-\infty)=0$, $F(\infty)=1$.
  • Unknown $k$: impose right-continuity for candidates, then non-decreasing to reject the wrong root.
  • $P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$.

Jumps classify $X$

jump size = point mass
$P(X=a)=F(a)-F(a^-)$, $\;F(a^-)=\lim_{h\to0}F(a-h)$
Σ jumps = 1
discrete
no jumps
continuous: $P(X=a)=0$, so $<$ vs $\le$ doesn't matter
0 < Σ jumps < 1
mixed (rest spread over an interval)

Transform a discrete $X$: $Y=g(X)$

  • Find $S_Y$ first. $Y$ is always discrete.
  • One-one on $S_X$: $P(Y=y)=p_X(g^{-1}(y))$.
  • Not one-one: add all pre-images, $P(Y=y)=\sum_{g(x)=y}p_X(x)$ (pre-images outside $S_X$ give 0).
  • One-one is judged on $S_X$: $x^2$ is one-one on $\{0,1,2\}$.

Transform a continuous $X$

CDF technique (any $g$)
$F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)$ case by case → check continuity of $F_Y$ ($Y$ may be continuous, discrete or mixed) → differentiate.
Jacobian (g one-one on $S_X$)
$f_Y(y)=f_X(g^{-1}(y))\left|\dfrac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|$
convention
densities with strict inequalities; CDFs in right-continuous form

Arshad's framing: counting → discrete (accidents, items); measuring → continuous (speed, blood pressure, sugar level). Uncountably many points can't each carry positive probability, or the total would be infinite — so continuous variables get probability only on intervals. Transformations exist because we record weight but want BMI.

Expectation, moments and the MGF

Check the mean exists, get moments from the MGF, and identify a distribution by its MGF.

Expectation

$E(X)$
$\sum x\,p(x)$ or $\int x\,f(x)\,dx$
exists only if
$E|X|<\infty$ — check first (modulus on $x$, never on $f$)
$E[g(X)]$
$\sum g(x)p(x)$ — swap $x$ for $g(x)$, keep the density
Gamma shortcut
$\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}dx=\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)!$
odd / even
odd over symmetric range → 0; even → 2× half

Moments

raw (about 0)
$\mu_r'=E(X^r)$; $\mu_1'$ = mean
central (about mean)
$\mu_r=E[(X-\mu_1')^r]$; $\mu_1=0$ always
variance
$\mu_2=\mu_2'-(\mu_1')^2=E[X^2]-(E X)^2$; $=0$ ⇔ degenerate
skewness
$\gamma_1=\mu_3/\mu_2^{3/2}$ — keeps the sign
kurtosis
$\beta_2=\mu_4/\mu_2^2$, $\gamma_2=\beta_2-3$ (normal: $\beta_2=3$)

Reading $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$

  • $\gamma_1>0$: long right tail (positive skew). $\gamma_1<0$: long left tail. $=0$: symmetric.
  • $\beta_1=\mu_3^2/\mu_2^3\ge0$ loses the sign — prefer $\gamma_1$.
  • $\gamma_2>0$ leptokurtic (more peaked), $=0$ mesokurtic, $<0$ platykurtic.
  • Unit-free: cm → m scales $\mu_3$ by $10^{-6}$; ratios cancel it.

Caution: in the 12 Sep lecture $\mu_3>0$ was called "left-skewed". The standard convention (and the one textbooks use) is that positive skew means the tail points right. Go by the sign.

Moment generating function

$M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]$
must exist on some $|t|<h$ — the interval must contain 0; $M(0)=1$
$\mu_k'=M^{(k)}(0)$
differentiate $k$ times, set $t=0$
or series
$\mu_k'$ = coefficient of $\frac{t^k}{k!}$ in $M_X(t)$

Uniqueness

  • Same MGF ⇒ same distribution. Recognise $M$ → name the distribution → read off the mean/variance.
  • $X\overset{d}{=}Y$ means $F_X=F_Y$ everywhere — not $X=Y$. Heads vs tails in 2 fair tosses: same PMF, but $Y=2-X$.
  • Memorise each PMF/PDF + MGF; derive the rest (Arshad).

Moments are the minimum set of numbers that pin down a distribution (Majumdar). A Gaussian needs only $(\mu,\sigma)$ — only $\sigma$ once centred; every other shape is a "distortion of the Gaussian" measured by skewness, kurtosis and higher moments. Arshad on kurtosis: calling someone "tall" needs a reference, and the normal curve is that reference.

The limit chain

Binomial → Poisson
$n\to\infty$, $p\to0$, $np=\lambda$ finite ($x\ll n$)
Poisson → Normal
also $\lambda\gg1$, look near the mean, and treat the variable as continuous
Poisson error bar
var = mean, so a count $n$ is quoted $n\pm\sqrt n$ (5 accidents/h → $5\pm\sqrt5$)

Shapes

  • Binomial $\gamma_1=\frac{q-p}{\sqrt{npq}}$: $p<\tfrac12$ right-tailed, $p=\tfrac12$ symmetric.
  • Binomial $\gamma_2=\frac{1-6pq}{npq}$: leptokurtic for $p<0.2113$ or $p>0.7887$, platykurtic between.
  • Poisson: small λ piles at 0; larger λ → symmetric.
  • Exponential: $\gamma_1=2$, $\gamma_2=6$. Uniform: symmetric, platykurtic, $\mu_4=\frac{(b-a)^4}{80}$.

Normal distribution, CLT and sampling distributions

Standardise, use Φ, and first ask: is this one value, a mean of $n$, or a sum of $n$?

Normal toolkit

standardise
$Z=\dfrac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)$
interval
$P(a\le X\le b)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}\sigma\right)-\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}\sigma\right)$
symmetry
$\Phi(0)=\tfrac12$, $\;\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)$ — one table side is enough
no closed-form CDF
hence tables; read them backwards too ($\Phi(z)=0.75$ → $z\approx0.675$ by interpolation)

Area rule

$\mu\pm1\sigma$
68.27% (34.1% each side)
$\mu\pm2\sigma$
95.45% (+13.6% each side)
$\mu\pm3\sigma$
99.73% — only ~27 in 10,000 fall outside
moments
odd central = 0; $\mu_4=3\sigma^4$; $\mu_{2n}=1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)\sigma^{2n}$

Central Limit Theorem

  • Sum/mean of many i.i.d. draws from any parent with finite variance → Gaussian. Infinite variance ("pathological") → no CLT.
  • Sample size 1 = no summing = no CLT (you just get the parent back).
  • Bigger sample size → narrower and faster; skewed parents converge slower.
  • Learn the implications; the derivation isn't examined.

Sample statistics

$\bar X_n=\frac1n\sum X_i\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)$
exact for normal data (MGF proof with $a_i=1/n$); approximate otherwise (CLT)
$S^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$
$n-1$ makes it unbiased; $1/n$ is biased

Rarity of a result

  • Where does the new value sit in the tail? Beyond ~3σ → probably not from the same distribution.
  • Accidents: $\mu=100$, $\sigma=5$ → normal band 85–115; a day with 200 is 20σ out → something changed (the road was rebuilt).
  • Null hypothesis: start from the negation ("no force pulls the apple", "obesity isn't linked to processed food", "accidents here are rare"). A strong violation rejects it — your theory becomes credible, not proven.
  • No standard sample size exists — always quote the mean and its spread.

Why Gaussians are everywhere (Majumdar, S9–S10):

  • Everything is a sum: height (genes + food + …), ocean waves, a shopper's basket (hungry? payday?), Sensex ticks — each is many random influences added up.
  • Convolution proof: slide one box across another and record the overlap → a triangle; triangle ∗ box → a bell; keep going → a Gaussian. It's the same smoothing your phone camera applies, and why early AI images looked too smooth.
  • Accurate vs certain: 10 samples can hit the true mean by luck, but only large $n$ shrinks the $\sigma/\sqrt n$ band enough to be confident.

Inequalities: Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen

Bounds for when you know only the mean (and maybe the variance), not the distribution.

Markov

$X\ge0$, $a>0$
$P(X\ge a)\le\dfrac{E X}{a}$
generalised
any $X$, $g\ge0$: $P(g(X)\ge a)\le\dfrac{E[g(X)]}{a}$
proof idea
drop $\int_0^a$, then $x\ge a$ on $[a,\infty)$

Chebyshev

upper
$P(|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$
lower
$P(|X-\mu|<\varepsilon)\ge1-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$
kσ form
$\le1/k^2$: at least 75% within 2σ for any distribution (normal: 95.45%)
from Markov
$g=(x-\mu)^2$, $a=\varepsilon^2$

Convexity and Jensen

test
$f''\ge0$ convex (chord above), $f''\le0$ concave; $f$ convex ⇔ $-f$ concave
Jensen
convex: $E[h(X)]\ge h(EX)$; concave: $\le$
uses
$x^2$ → $E X^2\ge(EX)^2$ so Var ≥ 0; $\log$ → $E\log X\le\log EX$

Arshad's caveat: these bounds are for "very limited information". If you know the distribution, compute the exact probability — the bound can be loose (0.52 vs an exact 0.20 below).

Why probability: inverse problems and two schools

Conceptual framing; not examined directly, but it's the vocabulary for everything after.

The inverse problem

forward problem
Model + parameters known → predict data. Easy ("two lines of Python" for a fair coin).
inverse problem
Data (effect) known → infer model/cause. Hard: different causes explain the same data.
a theory earns credibility
by surviving independent datasets across scales.

Three types of inverse problem

1 · hypothesis testing
Is a statement consistent with data? ("markets always crash")
2 · parameter estimation
What value does θ take? (TED spread threshold before 2008)
3 · model selection
Which of several fitting models? Hardest — needs the evidence term.

Where uncertainty comes from

  • Noise in the measurement
  • Intrinsic randomness (no two petals alike)
  • Designed randomness of complex systems (lottery drum)
  • Epistemic stance: causes unknown (age of universe ± error). Aleatory: games of chance, "40% rain tomorrow".
FrequentistBayesian

Majumdar's pictures of the inverse problem:

  • Newton's apple: "gravity" and "magic" both explain one falling apple; only gravity survives the Earth–Sun, galaxy and cluster scales.
  • Tycho's Mars: naked-eye declination data (1582–1600) → Kepler's ellipses. Seeing ellipses in a wiggly plot is the hard part. Dots are data; a curve is a model or denser data — never just join the dots.
  • Hungry shopper: "empty stomach → more chips and Coke" is a bias until you survey hungry vs not-hungry buyers.
  • The ant and the finger: from limited data the ant infers a giant tooth; new data (squashed, or climbs over) forces an update — Bayes foreshadowed.

Why the Bayesian answer differs — "you always have more prior information than you think":

  • Supernova: 4 naked-eye supernovae in 10 centuries → frequentist 4/10 = 40%; see one in 1987 → 5/10 = 50%. Ignores who was looking up, population, industrialisation, night-sky darkness.
  • Bus No. 15: 3 buses in the last hour → "every 20 min". Bayesian: peaked at 20 but 15–25 possible, because you know about night schedules, jams, accidents ("latent observations").
  • Magician's coin / caveman and rain: same proposition, different information → different degrees of belief.
  • Straight-line fit: same model $y=mx+c$; frequentist gives best-fit $m,c$, Bayesian gives posteriors — like a standard ANN vs a Bayesian neural net.
  • History flip-flops: Pascal & Fermat (1654, a gambler's split-the-stakes question) built the calculus; Bernoulli's insufficient reason gives each of $n$ outcomes $1/n$ but can't update; Bayes supplied the update; Gauss gave least squares, Gaussian errors and the CLT.
  • Updating never ends, but it isn't a loop: Sun around Earth → ellipses → non-coplanar spirals; Newton → general relativity (GPS needs it). More data, sharper model.

Probability rules and Bayes' theorem

Write the conditional probability you want, expand it with Bayes, and never drop the prior.

The calculus of probability

bounds · sum rule
$0\le P\le1$, $\;\sum_i P(i)=1$, $\;\int P(x)\,dx=1$
product rule
$P(X,Y)=P(X\mid Y)P(Y)=P(Y\mid X)P(X)$
independent
$P(X,Y)=P(X)P(Y)$ — only then multiply marginals
mutually exclusive
$P(x_1\cup\dots\cup x_n)=\sum P(x_i)$

Total probability → Bayes

total probability
$P(E)=\sum_i P(E\mid A_i)P(A_i)$
Bayes (two hypotheses)
$$P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E\mid H)P(H)+P(E\mid H^c)P(H^c)}$$
the maths is trivial
It's a rearranged product rule. Bayes' contribution was the interpretation: a way to update belief.

Inference form

$P(\theta\mid D,I,M)=\dfrac{P(D\mid\theta,I,M)\,P(\theta\mid I,M)}{P(D\mid I,M)}$
posterior
belief about θ after the data — what you want
likelihood
P(data | θ); product over independent data points only (apples from different, randomly chosen trees)
prior
everything known before the experiment
evidence
$\int P(D\mid\theta)P(\theta)\,d\theta$ — all ways the data could arise

Working rules

  • Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior for estimation and testing. Need the evidence only for model selection.
  • θ is meaningless without its model $M$; I and M are always silently conditioned on.
  • Flat prior $P(\theta)=$ const when you know nothing.

Updating

$P(\theta\mid D_1,D_2)\propto P(D_2\mid\theta)\,P(\theta\mid D_1)$
Yesterday's posterior is today's prior. Old data is never thrown away (needs $D_1, D_2$ independent).
prior-sensitivity test
If a different reasonable prior changes the answer a lot → you need more data.

Traps

  • $P(C\mid G)+P(C\mid G^c)\ne1$ in general. Only $P(C\mid G)+P(C^c\mid G)=1$.
  • Dropping implicit conditioning ("you chose door $a$", "a theft did happen").
  • Rule 1: write down exactly what you want to know. "There are no rules for $n>1$."

The prior is what intuition forgets (Majumdar, S7–S8, S10):

  • Steve, shy and tidy (Kahneman & Tversky): "librarian" feels right, but farmers outnumber librarians 20:1 → only 16.7% librarian. Our brains evolved for reflex "thinking fast" and skip the base rate.
  • Repeat offenders: even with a 50% re-offence rate, 80% of new crimes come from people with no record. Ignoring the prior → biased action → social disorder.
  • COVID testing: a "99% effective" test with 1% false positives at 0.5% prevalence is right only 1 in 3 times — hence rapid test, then a slow low-FPR confirmation.
  • MCQ exams: with 5 options, a correct answer means 5/6 "knew it" — why objective exams are trustworthy.

Samples, histograms and density

We only ever hold a finite sample; statistics compress it and estimate the population.

Population vs sample

population
infinite, holds the truth; never accessible
sample
finite $n$ draws; gives estimates (finite sums), not calculations (integrals)
statistic
any summary of a finite sample, no model assumed: mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, correlation

Histogram → PDF

  • Equal-width bins; height = count in the bin ∝ probability density.
  • Smooth the bin tops → approximate PDF ("a poor person's PDF").
  • Hard edges, empty bins; converges to the true PDF as $n$ grows.
  • Try several bin widths — the apparent shape changes.

Density, joint, marginal

density
probability mass per unit length/area/volume, e.g. $\dfrac{dB}{dH\,dW}$
"average density"
$=1/V$ — meaningless; only local density informs
marginalise
$P(X)=\int P(X,Y)\,dY$ — integrate out what you don't want
joint needed when
the observable depends on several variables (BMI from $h,w$)

How many samples are enough?

  • Plot the running estimate (mean, variance) against $n$; enough when it plateaus.
  • Coin: 102 flips → 0.49/0.51. Die: running mean settles near $3.5$ by ~100 rolls.
  • Cards 1–10: true variance $\frac{10^2-1}{12}=8.25$ (quoted ≈8.5 in class); simulation 8.32 at ~300 draws.
  • Few outcomes → hundreds suffice; high-dimensional → far more.

Always quote the error

  • Continuous values are never exact: a 1 cm tape → $\pm1$ cm.
  • Report $m\pm\Delta m$, $c\pm\Delta c$ — clients can't judge a strategy without it.
  • For continuous $X$: $P(X=a)=0$; ask for $P(a\le X\le a+dx)$ instead.

Majumdar's pictures:

  • Five points, eight numbers: joining 5 points with 4 segments needs 8 parameters (no compression); one fitted line needs 2. A client with a million Netflix users wants the trend, not the scatter.
  • Bins are boxes: sort every number from a bag into its box, then count.
  • Mountainous cake: $P(h,w)$ is a 3-D mountain drawn as contours; cutting a slab out of it is a joint probability.
  • More dead than living: the "true" BMI population would need everyone who ever lived — the population is effectively infinite.
  • LLM training set = a finite sample; a good one represents the underlying distribution. Insurance premiums and sample surveys (when the census is late) hinge on sampling strategy.

Random variables: PMF, PDF, CDF and transformations

Read a CDF, classify the variable, recover the PMF/PDF, find unknown constants, transform.

Definitions

random variable
$X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$; support $S=X(\Omega)$
PMF (discrete)
$p(x)\ge0$, $\sum_{S}p(x)=1$ — values are probabilities
PDF (continuous)
$f\ge0$, $\int f=1$ — not a probability; can exceed 1 ($f=2$ on $(0,\tfrac12)$)
link
$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt$, $\;f=F'$ (ignore finitely many kinks; set 0 there)

CDF $F(x)=P(X\le x)$

  • Defined on all of ℝ: write it piecewise, never as a 3-column table.
  • Valid CDF ⇔ (1) non-decreasing, (2) right-continuous $F(a)=F(a^+)$, (3) $F(-\infty)=0$, $F(\infty)=1$.
  • Unknown $k$: impose right-continuity for candidates, then non-decreasing to reject the wrong root.
  • $P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)$.

Jumps classify $X$

jump size = point mass
$P(X=a)=F(a)-F(a^-)$, $\;F(a^-)=\lim_{h\to0}F(a-h)$
Σ jumps = 1
discrete
no jumps
continuous: $P(X=a)=0$, so $<$ vs $\le$ doesn't matter
0 < Σ jumps < 1
mixed (rest spread over an interval)

Transform a discrete $X$: $Y=g(X)$

  • Find $S_Y$ first. $Y$ is always discrete.
  • One-one on $S_X$: $P(Y=y)=p_X(g^{-1}(y))$.
  • Not one-one: add all pre-images, $P(Y=y)=\sum_{g(x)=y}p_X(x)$ (pre-images outside $S_X$ give 0).
  • One-one is judged on $S_X$: $x^2$ is one-one on $\{0,1,2\}$.

Transform a continuous $X$

CDF technique (any $g$)
$F_Y(y)=P(g(X)\le y)$ case by case → check continuity of $F_Y$ ($Y$ may be continuous, discrete or mixed) → differentiate.
Jacobian (g one-one on $S_X$)
$f_Y(y)=f_X(g^{-1}(y))\left|\dfrac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y)\right|$
convention
densities with strict inequalities; CDFs in right-continuous form

Arshad's framing: counting → discrete (accidents, items); measuring → continuous (speed, blood pressure, sugar level). Uncountably many points can't each carry positive probability, or the total would be infinite — so continuous variables get probability only on intervals. Transformations exist because we record weight but want BMI.

Expectation, moments and the MGF

Check the mean exists, get moments from the MGF, and identify a distribution by its MGF.

Expectation

$E(X)$
$\sum x\,p(x)$ or $\int x\,f(x)\,dx$
exists only if
$E|X|<\infty$ — check first (modulus on $x$, never on $f$)
$E[g(X)]$
$\sum g(x)p(x)$ — swap $x$ for $g(x)$, keep the density
Gamma shortcut
$\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}dx=\Gamma(\alpha)=(\alpha-1)!$
odd / even
odd over symmetric range → 0; even → 2× half

Moments

raw (about 0)
$\mu_r'=E(X^r)$; $\mu_1'$ = mean
central (about mean)
$\mu_r=E[(X-\mu_1')^r]$; $\mu_1=0$ always
variance
$\mu_2=\mu_2'-(\mu_1')^2=E[X^2]-(E X)^2$; $=0$ ⇔ degenerate
skewness
$\gamma_1=\mu_3/\mu_2^{3/2}$ — keeps the sign
kurtosis
$\beta_2=\mu_4/\mu_2^2$, $\gamma_2=\beta_2-3$ (normal: $\beta_2=3$)

Reading $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$

  • $\gamma_1>0$: long right tail (positive skew). $\gamma_1<0$: long left tail. $=0$: symmetric.
  • $\beta_1=\mu_3^2/\mu_2^3\ge0$ loses the sign — prefer $\gamma_1$.
  • $\gamma_2>0$ leptokurtic (more peaked), $=0$ mesokurtic, $<0$ platykurtic.
  • Unit-free: cm → m scales $\mu_3$ by $10^{-6}$; ratios cancel it.

Caution: in the S13 lecture $\mu_3>0$ was called "left-skewed". The standard convention (and the one textbooks use) is that positive skew means the tail points right. Go by the sign.

Moment generating function

$M_X(t)=E[e^{tX}]$
must exist on some $|t|<h$ — the interval must contain 0; $M(0)=1$
$\mu_k'=M^{(k)}(0)$
differentiate $k$ times, set $t=0$
or series
$\mu_k'$ = coefficient of $\frac{t^k}{k!}$ in $M_X(t)$

Uniqueness

  • Same MGF ⇒ same distribution. Recognise $M$ → name the distribution → read off the mean/variance.
  • $X\overset{d}{=}Y$ means $F_X=F_Y$ everywhere — not $X=Y$. Heads vs tails in 2 fair tosses: same PMF, but $Y=2-X$.
  • Memorise each PMF/PDF + MGF; derive the rest (Arshad).

Moments are the minimum set of numbers that pin down a distribution (Majumdar). A Gaussian needs only $(\mu,\sigma)$ — only $\sigma$ once centred; every other shape is a "distortion of the Gaussian" measured by skewness, kurtosis and higher moments. Arshad on kurtosis: calling someone "tall" needs a reference, and the normal curve is that reference.

Rarity of a result

  • Where does the new value sit in the tail? Beyond ~3σ → probably not from the same distribution.
  • Accidents: $\mu=100$, $\sigma=5$ → normal band 85–115; a day with 200 is 20σ out → something changed (the road was rebuilt).

Why Gaussians are everywhere (Majumdar, S9–S10):

  • Everything is a sum: height (genes + food + …), ocean waves, a shopper's basket (hungry? payday?), Sensex ticks — each is many random influences added up.

  • Accurate vs certain: 10 samples can hit the true mean by luck, but only large $n$ shrinks the $\sigma/\sqrt n$ band enough to be confident.

Bivariate random variables: joint, marginal, conditional, covariance

Two measurements on one outcome, $(X,Y):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^2$. Everything univariate gets a joint version, plus one new question: do the two move together?

Joint and marginal PMF

joint PMF
$p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\ge0$, $\;\sum_x\sum_y p(x,y)=1$
marginals
$p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)$ (row sums), $\;p_Y(y)=\sum_x p(x,y)$ (column sums)
dependent ranges
swapping the order keeps the answer but changes the limits: $\sum_{y=0}^{2}\sum_{x=0}^{y}=\sum_{x=0}^{2}\sum_{y=x}^{2}$

Joint CDF

definition
$F(x,y)=P(X\le x,\,Y\le y)$: the lower-left quadrant
properties 1–4
non-decreasing and right-continuous in each coordinate; $\to0$ as $x\to-\infty$ or $y\to-\infty$; $\to1$ as both $\to\infty$
5 · rectangle inequality
$F(x_2,y_2)-F(x_2,y_1)-F(x_1,y_2)+F(x_1,y_1)\ge0$. Not implied by 1–4.
marginal CDF
$F_X(x)=\lim_{y\to\infty}F(x,y)$: send the other variable to $+\infty$

Joint PDF

definition
$F(x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(u,v)\,dv\,du$; $\;f\ge0$, $\iint f=1$
back to PDF
$f=\dfrac{\partial^2F}{\partial x\,\partial y}$ where $f$ is continuous
regions
$P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f\,dy\,dx$; $\;f_X(x)=\int f(x,y)\,dy$
uniform on a region
$f=\dfrac1{\text{area}}$, so probability $=$ area ratio

Take the whole quadrant under $(x_2,y_2)$, remove the strip below and the strip to the left, then add back the corner removed twice. What's left is $P(x_1<X\le x_2,\;y_1<Y\le y_2)$, and no rectangle may have negative probability. In 1-D this collapses to "non-decreasing", which is why it's new.

From Arshad's framing (S18–S19):

  • Height and weight, rainfall and temperature: one experiment, two readings. The step from here is regression, where $Y$ depends on $X$.
  • Joint vs marginal tell different stories: in the triangle example the joint is flat ($\tfrac16$ everywhere it lives), yet $X$ piles up at 0 and $Y$ at 2.
  • A density is a rate, not a probability: that's why the conditional PDF can't be $P(\cdot)/P(Y=y)$ and needs a limit instead.
  • Covariance only sees straight lines: $Y=X^2$ is completely determined by $X$ yet has zero covariance.

Where data come from and how samples are drawn

Population vs sample, primary vs secondary data, the errors that creep in between the population and the responses, and the ten sampling designs (three probability, seven non-probability).

POPULATION vs SAMPLE (S21)

population · N
The universe: every element we want to draw conclusions about. E.g. all lawyers in the country, all Tamil-speaking women in India, all engineering graduates of MP.
parameter · μ, σ
A feature of the population. Needs a census (measure everyone), which is costly in time and money.
sample · n
The part of the population available for analysis. It must be representative. Laptops: $N = 1{,}000{,}000$ made, $n = 500$ tested.
statistic · x̄, s, p̂
A measure computed from the sample. Its job is to estimate a parameter.
workflow
$$\text{sample} \to \text{statistic}\ (\bar x, s) \to \text{estimate parameter}\ (\mu, \sigma)$$

DESCRIPTIVE vs INFERENTIAL

descriptive
Summarises only the sample: mean, median, s, proportions, range, plus graphs and tables. A class of 35 students sits an exam; the teacher computes the average, median and s, and makes no claim about the university.
inferential (later)
Uses statistics to draw conclusions about parameters. E.g. $n = 1{,}000$ heights → $\mu$ for ~300 million people.
hypothesis preview (S23)
$H_a$ / $H_1$ = your claim. $H_0$ = its negation. If the evidence supports you, say "the null is rejected". The whole inferential half of the course rests on this.

PRIMARY vs SECONDARY DATA (S21)

primary
Compiled by you, for your question: survey, experiment, in-depth interview, observation, focus group. Social-science experiments are rarely controlled.
secondary
Compiled by someone else: Census (every 10 yrs), NSSO (every 4–5 yrs), IHDS (latest public round 2012), IMD, Forest Survey of India.
relative to the user
Census data are primary for the Ministry of Statistics and secondary for us. Rule: data you collect for your own research are primary.
metadata
"Data about the data": the collector's purpose, how households were selected, n and N, sampling ratio and method. Check that their purpose matches yours.

PRIMARY DATA: + / −

  • + More accurate. Purpose known, so variables are recorded for your question (villages near vs far from a forest). Easier to replicate next year.
  • − Time-consuming, expensive, often a one-time study. Errors or gaps are hard to identify afterwards.

SECONDARY DATA: + / −

  • + Quick, cheap, lets you build on past research across survey rounds. Lets you build panels: districts × years (1990, 1995, 2000, …), which is nearly impossible with primary data.
  • − May be outdated. Definitions differ (Census "occupation"). Units differ. Scale and aggregation differ: state data hides agro-climatic zones.

NESTED CIRCLES → ERRORS (S22)

population
Set by the research question. ≈ 5–10 lakh students.
sampling frame
The eligible elements, each with a chance of selection. ≈ 1,000 students with a physical disability. Wrong frame → frame error.
sample
100 drawn from the frame. Unrepresentative draw → chance error.
response
What is measured. Recording mistakes → response error.
taxonomy (lecturer's)
$\text{Sampling} = \text{frame} + \text{chance} + \text{response}$
$\text{Total} = \text{sampling} + \text{non-sampling (measurement)}$
Textbooks often file frame and response error under non-sampling. Use this course's taxonomy in the exam.

SURVEY ERRORS (S22)

fix the instrument first
Remove measurement error first: the instrument must be reliable, valid, well constructed. Only then work on sampling error.
5 response errors
① subject lies ② subject makes a mistake ③ interviewer makes a mistake ④ interviewer cheats ⑤ interviewer effects
instrument error
The respondent reads a term differently from the researcher, so the questionnaire is at fault. A response error happens even when the question is clear and understood.
nonresponse error
Not wrong answers: many people not answering. Those who do answer may differ systematically from those who don't.

SURVEY MODES (S21) — see table

  • Mail: cheap, wide reach, but no control over who replies.
  • Personal: direct, but expensive, and suffers from the interviewer effect.
  • Telephone: fast and cheaper, but you can't see whether the respondent is engaged.
  • Web: fastest and cheapest, but the number and truthfulness of responses are uncertain.
  • Over-surveyed groups and long questionnaires → low web response.

PROBABILITY SAMPLING (S22) — known, non-zero chance

  • Simple random (SRS): equal chance for every element. Use a random-number table or generator. Suits large populations where most units are relevant.
  • Systematic: random first element, then every k-th, with $k = N/n$. Needs N and n known in advance.
  • Stratified: non-overlapping, internally homogeneous strata, then SRS within each. Guarantees each group's share of the sample.

NON-PROBABILITY (S23) — can't claim representativeness

  • Convenience / purposive: ease of access or purpose. Used for exploratory work. (The lecturer treats these as synonyms; many textbooks separate them.)
  • Judgment: the researcher picks units judged representative.
  • Quota: proportional strata, but selection within a stratum is not random.
  • Cluster / area: take one or a few whole groups and survey every unit in them.
  • Multi-stage: states → districts → smaller units, filtered by criteria.
  • Sequential: n not fixed in advance; stop at saturation.
  • Snowball: each unit refers you to the next. For hard-to-find or hidden groups.

Statistic → parameter, and choosing data sources (S21):

  • Election polls ask about 1,000 people and predict the winner. "Why 1,000? That also we will see." This is answered by the sample-size formula in m14.
  • LED bulbs: 1 million made, 500 tested, 5 defective. $\hat p = 5/500 = 0.01$, which estimates $0.01 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 10{,}000$ defective bulbs. A statistic estimates a parameter.
  • Interviewer effect: a senior, well-dressed interviewer asks students about exam cheating and gets guarded answers. A young, student-like interviewer would get more honest ones.
  • Natural experiment: an unemployment-transfer policy introduced in 2016. Compare NSSO consumption ~3 years before and ~3 years after for ~1,000 households. A before/after treatment, built from secondary data.
  • IHDS: the latest public round (2012) is outdated for poverty work published in 2026, especially after COVID pushed non-poor households into poverty.
  • Temperature (a student's example): asking people gives subjective answers ("very hot" vs "not really"), so use IMD data instead.
  • Myntra reusing its own customer feedback for a new launch: still primary, because the company collected it, even though the original purpose was different.
  • Infrastructure vs forest conservation: India is too big for a primary survey. Forest Survey of India data (30–50 yrs) identify the top forest states. Pick Madhya Pradesh, narrow to districts, sample by forest proximity, then run a primary survey. Most research combines both kinds of data.

Errors, one story each (S22):

  • Smartboard with live captions: treatment = the captioning board; treatment group vs control group in conventional classrooms; response = exam marks.
  • Frame error: the frame says "physically disabled" students, which includes visually impaired students who can't use on-screen captions.
  • Chance error: the right number of units, but every one drawn from a single region.
  • Interviewer cheats: a hired field investigator ticks "no fridge, no motorbike" without asking, because the house "looks poor".
  • Interviewer effects: an elderly, conservatively dressed woman vs a young interviewer in jeans with tattoos and a nose ring, both asking young people about drugs.
  • Response errors from class: hotel ratings given with staff standing nearby; an HR burnout survey where employees under-report; "3 hours of exercise a day" when the real figure is under 1.
  • Walking into a village "randomly" is not random. Use a random-number table.
  • COVID: sampling hospital records of confirmed patients can be SRS. Contact tracing (one case leads to the next) is non-probability.
  • Diabetes drug: with 7–10% prevalence, probability sampling spends most of its effort on the 90–93% without diabetes.

Non-probability designs in the field (S23):

  • Uncontacted Andaman community: an exploratory convenience study builds the hypothesis ($H_a$: exposed to mainstream society; $H_0$: not exposed).
  • Travel Cost Method at a tiger-reserve gate: interview willing tourists in the queue about travel, lodging, food and entry costs. This values the park in money terms, using a convenience sample.
  • "Roadside research": the first village by the highway gets surveyed again and again. Joke: "Kerala is a convenience sample."
  • Why car showrooms are "distributors": diminishing marginal utility means buyers pay most for their first unit, so sellers sell a first unit to as many buyers as possible. New features let a buyer be "first" again. (Judgment example: 20 of 100 Honda distributors.)
  • Flood cluster + comparison households: flooded households lose assets and livelihood; unflooded ones lose only livelihood (no river crossing in the rains). The comparison separates the flood effect.
  • Morbi ceramic cluster (Gujarat): survey every ceramic manufacturer there. A cluster can be defined by a shared feature, not only by an impact.
  • Multi-stage: general-category states with >30% of area under agriculture (she first said 50%, then settled on 30%), then districts above 30%. She calls it non-probability because districts in excluded states get zero chance. Textbooks often call it probability when each stage is random.
  • WTP for clean water: prior range ₹100–1,000; answers fall between ₹80–90 and about ₹900–1,000. Stop when new answers keep landing in that range (saturation), or at 500/5,000 = 10% coverage.
  • Snowball for bribe-taking. The Amway analogy: each customer connects you to the next.

  • "Bigger n is not better; representativeness is." Even n → N doesn't guarantee accuracy. 50 well-spread respondents beat 250 clustered in one area.

Types of data and levels of measurement

First fix the variable, then the kind of data it yields, and only then the scale. Nominal → ordinal → interval → ratio: each step adds one property and unlocks more statistics.

KINDS OF DATA (S24)

variable
An entity whose value varies, either across cases at one time (HDI for many countries in 2025–26) or for one case over time (India, 2000–01 to 2025–26).
qualitative | quantitative
Categorical responses, also called nominal / categorical (e.g. which gender box was ticked) | numerical responses.
discrete | continuous
Countable: courses taken this semester (1, 2, 3, never 1.5), cricket runs, store footfall | from a measuring process, with as many decimals as you like: your weight, temperature, the weight of six-month-old babies.
primary | secondary
A different classification, by the source of the data.

ASKING THE QUESTION (S24)

  • Define your variables first. Make questions as specific as possible and response options exhaustive and unambiguous.
  • Employment: "Employed / Unemployed" alone is ambiguous. Specify the period (last financial year) and set a threshold: > 180 days worked → employed, ≤ 180 days → not. Then add part-time for someone working 4 h/day.
  • Gender: older research treated male / female as exhaustive. Now add third gender and prefer not to disclose. Religion options have widened in the same way.
  • Order of decisions: variable → kind of data → scale. Never pick the scale first.

INTERVAL vs RATIO — THE ZERO TEST

interval
Equal gaps but no true zero. The zeros of different units don't coincide: $0^\circ\text{C} \neq 0^\circ\text{F} \neq 0\ \text{K}$. So no ratios.
ratio
A true (absolute) zero that every unit shares: 0 kg = 0 lb, 0 km = 0 miles = 0 ft, 0 L = 0 gallons. Ratios work: 100 lb = 2 × 50 lb, 100 kg = 2 × 50 kg, USD 100 = ½ × USD 200.
temperature
°C and °F are interval. Kelvin is ratio (absolute zero): $150\ \text{K} = \tfrac12 \times 300\ \text{K}$.
reference (not stated in lecture)
$F = \tfrac{9}{5}C + 32$ and $K = C + 273.15$, so $0^\circ\text{C} = 32^\circ\text{F} = 273.15\ \text{K}$.

COLLECT AT THE HIGHEST LEVEL POSSIBLE (S25)

  • Higher levels allow more analysis. You can always step down later, but you can never step up.
  • Smoking: "Do you smoke?" (Yes/No) is nominal and gives frequency and mode only. "How many cigarettes in the past 3 days (72 h)?" is ratio and gives mean, median, mode and frequencies.
  • Soft drinks (Coke, Pepsi, 7 Up, Sprite, Dr. Pepper): ranking 1–5 is ordinal. It forces someone who dislikes all five to rank one 1st, and it can't separate "dislike all" from "neutral to all".
  • Rating each drink Excellent / Very good / Good / Fair / Poor / Very poor / Awful (7 categories) is usually treated as interval, so means are easy to compute. Strictly it sits between ordinal and interval. A respondent who hates them all can tick Awful five times.

Numbers that are only labels (S24):

  • Jersey numbers identify a player and carry no numeric meaning.
  • Religion in a class of 50 (20 Muslim + 30 Hindu): code Muslim = 1 and Hindu = 2. The codes can't be multiplied, divided or averaged, so an "average religion of the class" is meaningless. All you can do is count: 30 > 20, so the mode is Hindu.
  • Nominal data is the easiest type to collect, but almost no mathematical operation on it means anything.

Order without distance (S24):

  • Hurricanes: Cat 5 is worse than Cat 4, which is worse than Cat 3. But Cat 2 is not half of Cat 4.
  • Mohs hardness: talc is the softest and diamond the hardest, with gypsum and others in between. The next mineral up is not "double" talc's hardness.
  • Class standing: scores of 92, 82 and 81 give ranks 1, 2 and 3, yet the gaps are 10 marks and 1 mark.
  • Military ranks: General > Colonel > Major > Lieutenant > Sergeant. The order is clear, but the distances between ranks need not be equal.
  • Income brackets (< USD 20,000 · USD 20,000–49,999 · ≥ USD 50,000): a billionaire and someone earning exactly USD 50,000 tick the same box.

Rankings are relative, not absolute (S24):

  • You can't be "doubly sure" or "half sure". Your top choice depends on the choice set: ranking from {A, B, C, D, E} can crown a different winner than ranking from {C, D, E, X, Y}.
  • The humorous slide asked the class to rank 1–4: being hit in the face with a dead rat; being buried up to your neck in cow manure; failing this course; eating nothing but chopped liver for a month. The point is how hard it is to rank options that are all bad, which is the same flaw the soft-drink ranking has in S25.

Why temperature has no ratios (S24):

  • New York at 40° and Buffalo at 20° does not mean one is "twice as hot". Nor is 10° half of 20°, or 20° half of 40°.
  • Body temperature: about 98°F is normal and 100–101°F is fever, while normal is about 37°C. The numbers don't line up across scales because the zeros differ.
  • Lecturer's slip: in class Kelvin was illustrated as "300 K is half of 150 K". It should read 150 K = ½ × 300 K.

Discrete or continuous? Class debates (S24):

  • Petrol pump: fix the litres (7 L) and the bill comes out like ₹700.xx; fix the rupees (₹100) and you get about 0.97 L. The class agreed that whichever one is fixed, the other is continuous. Prices quoted to two decimals are part of why the bill looks continuous.
  • Retail store: footfall is discrete. A student called sales revenue continuous, and the lecturer asked how often prices really take continuous values (loose groceries priced in paise). Her follow-up task: "go back and think about both examples again."
  • Units sold is ratio, but only for goods sold in whole units. Fuel is a flow commodity and therefore continuous.

Students' "interval" proposals and the verdicts (S24):

  • 12-hour clock (2–4 pm and 6–8 pm are both 2 hours): rejected. Spans are measured exactly (120 min each), and whether the time was "productive" is a separate question.

location · central
mean, median, mode: one centrally located value that represents the whole set
location · non-central

dispersion

MEAN · MEDIAN · MODE

mean
$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i}{n}$. Data need not be ordered. Pulled by outliers.
median
Order first; position $\frac{n+1}{2}$. With n = 7 it is the 4th value. With n = 6 the position is 3.5 → $(30+40)/2 = 35$.
(1) robust to extremes on either side · (2) a random observation is equally likely above or below it · (3) $\sum|x_i - \text{median}|$ is minimum
mode
Most frequent value; it must appear at least twice. Possible outcomes:
unimodal; bimodal (5 and 10 each ×3); multimodal (six values ×2 → six modes); or no mode when all values are distinct. S25 once misspoke "multimodal" for all-distinct data; the convention is "no mode".

QUANTILES (S25–S26)

k parts → k − 1 cuts
quartiles 3 · quintiles 4 · deciles 9 · percentiles 99. Always order the data first.
position rule (S26)
$Q_1: \frac{n}{4}$, $Q_2: \frac{2n}{4}$, $Q_3: \frac{3n}{4}$
integer → average that observation and the next · not integer → round up to the next position. Positions, not values. Excel may interpolate differently.
Q1 · Q2 · Q3
25th · 50th (median) · 75th percentiles. Q1: 25% below, 75% above.
percentile ≠ marks
40/100 can be the 99th percentile: top 1% of candidates.

FREQUENCY & GROUPED DATA (S26)

frequency table
$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum x_i f_i}{\sum f_i}$, with $\sum f_i = n$. Use it when values repeat often.
grouped mean
Same formula, with $x_i$ = class midpoint. Commute classes 0–10 … 40–50 → midpoints 5, 15, 25, 35, 45 → 21.8 min.
grouped median
$L + \left(\frac{N/2 - cf}{f}\right)\times h$
Median class = first class with cf ≥ N/2. Here cf = cumulative frequency of the class before it. The slide was missing the ×h.
continuity correction
Inclusive classes 51–60, 61–70 of a continuous variable become 50.5–60.5, 60.5–70.5.
grouped mode
$L + \left(\frac{f_1 - f_0}{2f_1 - f_0 - f_2}\right)\times h$
$f_0$ = class before, $f_1$ = modal class, $f_2$ = class after.
grouped percentile
$P_k = L + \left(\frac{kN/100 - cf}{f}\right)\times h$. This was on a slide but not read aloud; it is the standard textbook form.

Dispersion, shape, z-scores and displays

      
How spread out the data are (range, IQR, s, variance, CV), what shape they take (skewness, kurtosis, box plots), and how to show them (frequency graphs, two-variable displays).

    


    

      

        
SPREAD (S26)

        

          
range = max − min
Simple and easy to explain, but driven by outliers. Monthly profits: 32 − 12 = 20 lakh.

          
IQR = Q3 − Q1
Spread of the middle 50%. Robust to extremes, but ignores 50% of the data.

          
sample SD s
$$s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum (x_i-\bar x)^2}{n-1}}$$
            (1) Squaring removes the zero sum: $\sum(x_i-\bar x)=0$.

            (2) Least squares: $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2 \le \sum(x_i-a)^2$ for every $a$. (The median minimises $\sum|x_i-\text{median}|$ instead.)

            (3) Divide by $n-1$: one degree of freedom is lost to $\bar x$.

          
degrees of freedom
In $2x+3y=12$ you choose $y$, and then $x=(12-3y)/2$ is forced. One constraint costs one df. Using $\bar x$ to compute $s$ costs one.

        

      

      

        
σ vs s · VARIANCE · CV (S27)

        

          
population σ
$\sigma=\sqrt{\sum(x_i-\mu)^2/N}$. Uses μ and divides by N. Census only: every unit must be recorded.

          
sample s
Uses x̄ and divides by n − 1. Default: treat your data as a sample.

          
why n − 1
It makes $s^2$ unbiased for $\sigma^2$: $E[s^2]=\sigma^2$. Stressed in class: "not an unbiased estimate of s". A statistic estimates a parameter. Dividing by $n$ underestimates; S27 said "overestimate", which was a slip.

          
variance s², σ²
Additive for independent variables: $\text{Var}(X+Y)=\text{Var}X+\text{Var}Y$. SD is not, because $\sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt a+\sqrt b$. Variance is central to theory (moments, $\chi^2$). Drawback: squared units (m²). For the algebra see m5 and m9.

          
CV = s / x̄ × 100%
Unit-free. Use it when units (USD vs yen) or magnitudes differ. CV = 100% means $s=\bar x$, i.e. a lot of variability.

        

      

      

        
SHAPE AND z (S27, S29)

        

          
mean vs median
$\bar x <$ median → left skew (pulled by low values). $\bar x =$ median → symmetric. $\bar x >$ median → right skew. Task times: 15 > 10 h, with one 63-hour employee → right skew.

          
Pearson Sk = (x̄ − mode)/s
The sign gives the direction. Not applicable to bimodal data. Reference only: $3(\bar x-\text{median})/s$.

          
kurtosis (S29)
Leptokurtic: tall and narrow, piled near the centre. Platykurtic: flat and wide. The normal curve (mesokurtic) sits between them.

          
z = (x − x̄)/s
Mean 0, SD 1, no units. Below the mean gives a negative z. For a population: $(x-\mu)/\sigma$.

          
coverage
Normal: ±1.96 → 95% · ±3 → 99.7% · ±4 → 99.99% (≈ 99.994%). Any distribution (Chebyshev, m8): at least $1-1/2^2=75\%$ within ±2 SD. Rule of thumb: |z| > 5 is very unusual.

CLT at work: probabilities for x̄, the 95% interval, and sample size

      
Pritee's applied problems on her half-table: standardise a sample mean with σ/√n (never σ alone), then use the same half-width to size a sample. The CLT theory itself is in module 7.

    


    

      

        
SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION · CLT

        

          
sampling distribution of x̄
Draw many samples of size n, take each mean, plot the means. E.g. heights of 30 grade-5 students per sample give $\bar x_1, \bar x_2, \bar x_3, \dots$ A "sample" is a set of n units, not one unit.

          
CLT (n ≥ 30)
$\bar X \approx N(\mu,\ \sigma^2/n)$ whatever the population's shape, including skewed delivery times.

          
unbiased
$E[\bar X] = \mu_{\bar X} = \mu$: the mean of all the sample means is the population mean.

          
standard error
$SE = \sigma_{\bar X} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$. With large n and σ unknown, s can stand in for σ.

        

      

      

        
ONE UNIT OR THE MEAN OF n? (S29)

        

          
one unit: no √n
$z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ · motors (a)

          
mean of n units
$z = \dfrac{\bar x - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$ · chains, bulbs, motors (b), delivery

          
same bound, different z
At 98,000 miles: z = −0.2 for one motor, z = −2 for the mean of 100 motors.

          
population σ
When the whole population is known, divide by N, not N − 1.

        

      

      

        
HALF-TABLE (PRITEE): AREA FROM 0 TO z

        

          
lookup

RANDOM VARIABLES & BERNOULLI (S31)

        

          
random variable
A rule that maps outcomes to real numbers. On a die, "less than 3" and "even" are two different RVs. Discrete means at most countably many values.

          
PMF | PDF
Discrete → probability mass function, $p_X(a)=P(X=a)$. Continuous → density. Never say "density" for a discrete RV (S34).

          
E and Var
$E[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)$
$\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$

          
Bernoulli · Ber(p) = B(1, p)
One trial: X = 1 is a success, X = 0 a failure. $E[X]=p$.
$E[X^2]=0^2(1-p)+1^2\cdot p=p$ (said "p²" once in class; it is $p$).
So $\mathrm{Var}=p-p^2=p(1-p)$.

        

      

      

        
BINOMIAL B(n, p) (S31–S32)

        

          
four conditions: name the one that fails
① two disjoint outcomes ② fixed, finite n ③ independent trials ④ constant p. "Not binomial" alone is not an answer. Say which condition breaks.

          
PMF
$\binom{n}{x}p^x(1-p)^{n-x}$, for x = 0…n. The probabilities sum to 1 (binomial theorem).

          
E[X] = np: the trick
Drop the x = 0 term. Use $x\binom{n}{x}=n\binom{n-1}{x-1}$ and pull out $np$. Put a = x − 1, b = n − 1. What remains is a full B(b, p) sum, which equals 1.

          
Var = npq
Write $E[X^2]=E[X(X-1)]+E[X]$. The same trick, starting from x = 2, gives $E[X(X-1)]=n(n-1)p^2$. Then $n(n-1)p^2+np-n^2p^2=np(1-p)$.

          
mode
The largest integer $\le (n+1)p$. Why: $\frac{P(k)}{P(k-1)}=\frac{(n-k+1)p}{k(1-p)}\ge 1 \iff k\le(n+1)p$.

          
at most · at least · at least one
≤ k · ≥ k · $1-P(X=0)$

        

      

      

        
THE OTHER FOUR (S32–S34)

        

          
Poisson(λ)
Counts per unit of time or space, with independent events at a constant rate. It is the limit of B(n, p) as $n\to\infty$ and $p\to 0$ with $\lambda=np$ held fixed. PMF $\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}$, x = 0, 1, 2, … Mean = variance = λ ("a bit limiting"). Scale λ to the window.

          
Geometric(p)
The trial of the 1st success. PMF $(1-p)^{x-1}p$, with support starting at x = 1. Mean $1/p$, Var $(1-p)/p^2$, CDF $1-(1-p)^x$.

          
Negative binomial(r, p)
The trial n of the r-th success. PMF $\binom{n-1}{r-1}p^r(1-p)^{n-r}$, for n = r, r+1, … Mean $r/p$, Var $r(1-p)/p^2$. Setting r = 1 gives the geometric. "The last trial must be a success."

          
Hypergeometric(N, M, n)
The course's only without-replacement model. PMF $\frac{\binom{N}{i}\binom{M}{n-i}}{\binom{N+M}{n}}$. Mean $np$ with $p=\frac{N}{N+M}$; Var $np(1-p)\left[1-\frac{n-1}{N+M-1}\right]$.

          
elsewhere
MGFs and general formulas: m6. The bell shape the binomial tends to: m7.

PDF
$f(x) = \frac{1}{\beta - \alpha}$ on $(\alpha, \beta)$, 0 otherwise. Width $(\beta-\alpha)$ × height $\frac{1}{\beta-\alpha}$ gives area 1. For $U(0,1)$: $f = 1$.

          
CDF
$F(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a}$ on $[a, b]$; 0 before $a$, 1 after $b$. Corners at $a$ and $b$ make it not differentiable there; everywhere else $F'(x) = f(x)$.

          
mean
$E[X] = \frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}$, the midpoint.

          
variance
$\frac{(\beta-\alpha)^2}{12}$, via $E[X^2] = \frac{\beta^2+\alpha\beta+\alpha^2}{3}$, using $\beta^3-\alpha^3 = (\beta-\alpha)(\beta^2+\alpha\beta+\alpha^2)$.

          
✗ log substitution
A student's suggestion (S35), and wrong: the denominator is the constant $\beta-\alpha$, not $\beta - x$. Pull it out and integrate directly.

        

      

      

        
NORMAL N(μ, σ²)

        

          
history
de Moivre, 1733, as the limit of the binomial: $n$ large, with $p$ and $q$ not small (Poisson needs $p$ very small). The slide said "English mathematician"; he was French-born and worked in England. Extended by Laplace via the CLT (m7).

          
PDF
$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$, $-\infty < x < \infty$. The exponent has $2\sigma^2$, twice the variance; she corrected herself on this (S36).

          
standardise

Correlation: Pearson r, Spearman ρ, and where regression comes from

      
This module covers how to put a number on two variables moving together, what that r can and cannot claim, the rank version for judges' scores, and the step into regression.

    


    

      

        
BIVARIATE, NOT CAUSAL (S36)

        

          
correlation
Co-variation of two variables: a change in one is associated with a change in the other. It shows co-movement only, not causation.

          
positive (direct)
Height–weight of children up to ~18 · education–income per capita · income–consumption · supply–price. Both falling together is still positive.

          
negative (inverse)
Speed–travel time · price–quantity demanded (law of demand, $Q_d = f(P)$) · vehicle weight–fuel efficiency.

          
behind co-movement
Seasonal · trend · cyclical · random (irregular) components.

          
scatter diagram: 3 limits
(1) gives no number for strength; (2) subjective, because the scale or omitted initial values change the look; (3) gives no significance test.

        

      

      

        
PEARSON r · 1/n FORMS (S36–S37)

        

          
assumptions
(1) the relationship is linear; (2) many independent causes act on each variable, so each is roughly normal (height, weight, profit, sales, prices); (3) the forces acting on X and Y are not wholly independent: some common force links them, or else r = 0.

          
r
$r = \dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}$, with $\operatorname{Cov} = \frac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)$. Covariance algebra is in m9.

          
shortcuts
$\operatorname{Cov} = \frac1n\sum xy - \bar x\,\bar y$
$\sigma_X^2 = \frac1n\sum x^2 - \bar x^2$

          
n-form (textbook equivalent, not written in class)
$$r=\frac{n\sum xy-\sum x\sum y}{\sqrt{n\sum x^2-(\sum x)^2}\,\sqrt{n\sum y^2-(\sum y)^2}}$$

        

      

      

        
PROPERTIES OF r (S37)

        

          
−1 ≤ r ≤ 1 · THE most important
Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the deviations $a_i = x_i-\bar x$, $b_i = y_i-\bar y$: $(\sum a_ib_i)^2 \le \sum a_i^2\sum b_i^2$. Equality holds iff $a_i/b_i$ is constant, i.e. a perfect line. A reported $|r|>1$ means an error.

          
sign | size
Sign gives direction | size gives strength. Indicative bands for |r|: 0–0.25 none · 0.25–0.5 weak · 0.5–0.75 moderate · > 0.75 strong · ≈ 0.9–0.98 near-perfect. The cut-offs vary by author.

          
origin & scale
$u = \frac{x-a}{h}$, $v = \frac{y-b}{k}$ ⇒ $r_{UV} = r_{XY}$. The sign flips if exactly one of h, k is negative (added in the notes).

          
independent ⇒ r = 0
The converse fails: $y = x^2$ gives r = 0. r only sees lines.

          
r²
The coefficient of determination (explained variation). r is not on a ratio scale.

          
correlation matrix
The diagonal is all 1s. It holds simple pairwise r only; partial r is computed differently.

        

      

    


    

      

        
SPEARMAN RANK CORRELATION (S38)

        

          
ρ
$$\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}$$

          
derivation
Ranks are $1..n$, so both series have mean $\frac{n+1}{2}$ and variance $\frac{n^2-1}{12}$. Let $d_i = x_i - y_i$. Then $\sum d_i = 0$ (an arithmetic check), and $\frac1n\sum d_i^2 = 2\sigma^2(1-\rho)$. Substitute $\sigma^2$: the 12 and the 2 leave 6.

          
ties
Average the positions (75, 75 in 2nd and 3rd → 2.5 each), then skip them. Add $\sum\frac{m(m^2-1)}{12}$ to $\sum d^2$, over every tie group in both series.

          
vs Pearson
On untied ranks, Spearman = Pearson. Spearman is distribution-free, whereas Pearson assumes a normal parent population. Use it for ranked qualitative attributes. Don't rank continuous data, because that loses information. It gets cumbersome for n > 30.

        

      

      

        
MORE VARIABLES → REGRESSION (S38)

        

          
partial r₁₂.₃
Correlation of X₁ and X₂ with X₃ held constant. It is linear and assumes normality. Concept only; the formula is for reference and was not covered: $r_{12.3} = \dfrac{r_{12}-r_{13}r_{23}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{13}^2)(1-r_{23}^2)}}$

          
multiple correlation
Announced ("definition only") but not covered.

          
regression
Sir Francis Galton, inherited height: "regression towards mediocrity" (law of universal regression). Karl Pearson later confirmed it.

          
deterministic | statistical
Lab sciences (chemistry): controlled, repeatable experiments, so A, B, C reliably give X, Y, Z | economics and data science: random variables, so relationships hold only statistically.

          
error sources
Omitted variables · interdependence among the variables · intrinsic random variability.

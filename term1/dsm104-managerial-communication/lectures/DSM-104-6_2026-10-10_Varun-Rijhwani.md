## 4. Module 3, Topic 1: Meetings

### 4.1 Opening Exercise: "What makes a meeting a waste of time?"

Students shared these problems from their own experience:

| Problem | Explanation (from discussion) |
|---|---|
| **No agenda or topic** | People don't know what the meeting is for. |
| **Too much time spent giving context** | Up to "90% of the time" goes on background that could have been shared asynchronously by text or email. |
| **Unpreparedness** | People arrive and only then discuss and form opinions. Points should be collected and circulated by email beforehand. |
| **Unnecessary attendees** | Whole teams are invited when many have no role in the item. |
| **Repeating points to show participation** | For example, a manager repeats the same remark to look involved, which wastes everyone's time. |
| **Digressing** | The discussion drifts away from the main agenda. |

### 4.2 Student Solutions

- **Scrum calls (Rahul).** These are agile stand-ups, strictly time-bound at about 15 minutes.
  - The scrum team consists of a **Product Owner, a Scrum Master, and Developers**. The development team may include developers, functional consultants, and others, each with their own agenda.
  - The purpose is narrow: discuss the issues, the pros and cons, and the solutions.
  - A common failure is going too deep into technicalities. Details belong in a separate meeting.
  - The Scrum Master facilitates, notes points, and moves the meeting forward.
- **The host's responsibility (Nihal).** The host or Scrum Master decides the topic and the decision to be made before the meeting. Attendees therefore come prepared.
  - **Lesson:** regardless of meeting type, the host is responsible for directing the meeting and communicating in advance what is required of attendees.
- **Encourage equal participation.** Prevent one person from dominating the conversation.
- **Timebox the agenda (Mohammad).** Divide the session time across subtopics so speakers know when they are running over. Microsoft Teams now offers agenda timeboxing that notifies a speaker who exceeds the allotted time.
- **The 24-hour pre-meeting message (Viky Raj).** When hosting a decision meeting, send a message 24 hours before that covers three things:
  1. **What** decision needs to be taken.
  2. **Why** it needs to be taken.
  3. **Any other context** surrounding the decision.

  The instructor praised this as a good way to keep discussions focused and avoid wasting others' time.

### 4.3 Framework: Effective Participation in Meetings (slide)

1. **Prepare.** Know the agenda and what information or questions to bring. For example, a 15-minute scrum call needs very specific inputs. Pre-meeting emails support this.
2. **Contribute.** Make a relevant point and explain your reasoning. See the Point, Reason, Next Step structure in 4.4.
3. **Listen.** This is described as a paramount skill in group settings.
   - **Build, don't repeat.** Repetition happens when people aren't listening, and it drags meetings on for hours.
   - **Rephrasing is different from repeating.** Rephrasing checks that you understand and are on the same page. Repeating just parrots what was said and wastes time.
4. **Be mindful of tone.** Raise concerns respectfully. There is a professional way to disagree.
5. **Follow through.** At the end, do a quick review of decisions and responsibilities: **who is doing what, and by when**.

### 4.4 Structure for Contributing: Point, Reason, Next Step

| Step | Example from the slide |
|---|---|
| **Point**: state your suggestion first | "I suggest a smaller pilot." |
| **Reason**: explain why | "We can identify problems before committing to the full budget." |
| **Next step**: seek buy-in or action | "Could we agree on the pilot scope today?" |

**Lesson:** following this step-by-step structure keeps meetings running smoothly and removes blockages as the discussion moves forward.

## 5. Six Thinking Hats (Edward de Bono)

### 5.1 Video Summary
- The book *Six Thinking Hats* was published in **1985** by **Edward de Bono**. The video described him as a Maltese physician.
- **Premise:** the brain thinks in several distinct ways, which can be deliberately challenged. Looking at a problem through all six hats means using every approach. The resulting decisions combine ambition, skill, sensitivity, creativity, and good contingency planning.
- The technique can be used in meetings or alone. Each hat is a distinct style of thinking.

### 5.2 The Six Hats

| Hat | Thinking mode | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **White** | Data / information | Study the available facts and figures and see what you can learn from them. |
| **Red** | Intuition / emotion / gut feeling | Give knee-jerk reactions. Consider how people will react to the decision emotionally. |
| **Black** | Caution / risks ("devil's advocate") | Identify weak points, risks, and logistical problems in the plan. |
| **Yellow** | Optimism / benefits | Focus on the benefits and value. This is useful when everything looks gloomy. |
| **Green** | Creativity | Generate creative ideas, alternatives, and solutions. |
| **Blue** | Process control | Manage the thinking process and decide which hat suits each stage. For example, if ideas are running dry, switch to green. |

### 5.3 Key Clarifications from the Instructor

- **The hats are thinking modes, not personality types.** Everyone can switch between hats within the same meeting.
- **Black-hat thinking means reasoned caution, not simply being negative.** It involves questioning assumptions, the source of the idea, and where the idea is heading.
- **No fixed sequence.** You can wear any hat at any time and return to a hat repeatedly. For example: white, then red, then white again. Your emotional reaction may change after seeing the data, and that is fine.
  - Wear a hat when it is missing from the discussion. If everyone is positive and no one is questioning cost or logistics, put on the black hat.
- **You don't need to announce that you are using the framework.** It is simply a way of drawing out different points of view.
- **Consequence of not using it:** an incomplete, non-comprehensive decision. Skipping a hat means a perspective was never considered.
  - Skipping the red hat means feelings were not considered. You need not act on feelings, but you should have considered them.
  - Skipping the black hat means things may "go south" during execution.
- **Benefit:** a "360-degree view" and a well-informed, wholesome decision.
- **Use judgment about when to apply it.** It is most useful for brainstorming, generating ideas, finding creative solutions, or getting unstuck. It is not needed for routine meetings where nothing needs to be contributed. More generally, choosing the right framework for the situation is itself a skill.
- **It is not only the host's job.**
  - Responsibility ultimately lies with the host or the person in the power position.
  - However, any participant can notice a missing perspective and "wear" that hat. For example, if everyone dwells on negatives, bring the yellow hat.
  - "You don't need a position to lead." Knowledge of the framework is a source of power.

### 5.4 Student Views on Applying the Hats
- **Possible order:** start with blue to set the process, then white (information), then black and yellow (negatives and positives), and end with green (solutions).
- **Another view:** analyse the problem with the other five hats during preparation, then use blue during the contributing stage to decide what fits the agenda.
- **Blue as the guiding framework (Ashraul).** The sequence of the other hats follows the objective or agenda.
- **The hats reduce conflict (Nihal).** Everyone looks in the same direction at the same time, so opposing viewpoints clash less, debates are not repeated, and meetings get shorter.
- **A data-first sequence (Punchmi).** Analyse the data (white), find the shortcomings and bring in empathy, for example a person was sick (red), stay optimistic (yellow), generate creative alternatives (green), and apply critical judgment (black).
- **The instructor's own handling of the test discussion, analysed by Rahul:**
  1. White: gathering what students said.
  2. Red: reading their emotions.
  3. Black and yellow: weighing the loss of surprise against other factors.
  4. Green: the creative solution of a test the next Saturday for everyone.
  5. Blue: keeping the process under control so all students participate.

  The instructor called this a great example.

### 5.5 In-Class Exercise: Should the Company Adopt an AI Meeting Assistant?

**Decision on the table:** a company wants to introduce an AI assistant that produces meeting summaries and action points. Should it adopt the tool for **all project meetings**?

- **Given concerns:** accuracy, confidential information, cost, and over-reliance. Employees might read only the summaries and skip the full discussion or agenda.

**Blue hat (where to start?)** One student first said red, then corrected to **white**. The class started with white.

**White hat (data and information):**
- Does the tool align with data-security requirements? Many companies currently restrict AI tools.
- How error-prone is the tool? This can only be judged from its outputs, which can then be compared.
- What is the exact price of the tool?
- How much is currently spent on documentation? This allows a cost comparison.
- How much time will it save on summarising, and will it provide evidence of what was discussed?
- Is it needed for all project meetings or only the important ones? The instructor placed this question under white hat.
- **Jasraj:** we don't have the data yet, so run a **pilot with one team**, collect data, and analyse before rolling out to all. The instructor noted this applies the "smaller pilot" idea from the Point, Reason, Next Step example.
- **Instructor's summary:** white hat establishes the need for the decision and whether the advantages outweigh the cost.

**Red hat (gut reactions):**
- Privacy worries. The instructor asked whether this was a true first reaction or second-order thinking.
- "I'll be very happy. It saves time and gives crisp information." The instructor treated this as the genuine knee-jerk reaction: time savings and freedom to focus on other tasks.
- Discomfort at being recorded all day. Off-topic talk would be captured; for example, today's discussion about the exam would have been recorded. Summaries might be accessed by anyone or misused, and the data could be used to train the model.

**Yellow hat (benefits):**
- People spend a lot of meeting time taking notes and may miss points in fast-paced meetings.
- The assistant produces the minutes of meeting (MOM) automatically, so nothing said is skipped.

**Black hat (risks and limitations, from Aayushi):**
- Which meetings would it cover, day-to-day or only important ones? It would be helpful for recording what vendors or clients say.
- How expensive is it?
- How much confidential data would be exposed to the tool?
- How would implementation work? Would it really cover all meetings?

**Real-world addition (Rahul):**
- In project implementation, especially under the **waterfall method** where the full product is built and then presented, clients sometimes claim "we never asked for this," particularly when payment is at stake.
- Without a record, there is no proof. AI-recorded minutes can resolve such disputes.
- So recording has both pros and cons.

**Lesson:** switch hats as the situation demands. When you notice a missing perspective, such as nobody discussing risks, put on that hat. This leads to a well-informed decision.

## 6. Groupthink and the Abilene Paradox

### 6.1 Agreement Is Not the Same as Consent
- **Question posed:** if everyone agrees and no one objects, does everyone actually want that decision?
- **Rahul's answer: not always.** Meetings often run on majority or consensus, and dissenters follow along. Valid disagreements can at least be recorded in the minutes of meeting.
- **Definition of groupthink:** you hold a different view but stay silent because others seem to agree. You assume that if everyone agrees, they must be right, so you go along.

### 6.2 Video: The Abilene Paradox
The video is dated but is the original on this concept. It is narrated by the son-in-law, Jerry, who later studied group dynamics in organisations.

- **Setting:** a 104-degree July Sunday in Coleman, Texas. The family is playing dominoes.
- **What happens:**
  - The father-in-law suggests driving to Abilene for supper at a cafeteria.
  - Abilene is far away, and the Buick's air conditioning is not fixed.
  - Each person agrees: "sounds good to me," "only if Mom wants to go," "of course I want to go."
- **The outcome:** a miserable round trip of about 4 hours and 106 miles in a "furnace" of a car, with a bad meal.
- **Afterwards:** in the silence, each person reveals they never wanted to go.
  - One went to satisfy the others.
  - One went "to be sociable."
  - The father who proposed it says he was "just making conversation."
- **Narrator's conclusion:** four reasonable people took a trip none of them wanted. Organisations take similar "trips to Abilene" all the time.

### 6.3 Lessons Drawn in Class
- **Cost of the paradox:** unvoiced disagreement leads to bad decisions that waste money, resources, infrastructure, and manpower. Afterwards, no one takes ownership; everyone says it wasn't their idea.
- **Why they went: power and position.** The idea came from the father-in-law, the head of the family. The others deferred and cancelled out their own opinions.
- **Workplace parallel:** when a senior makes a point, people assume it must be right and stop asking questions or raising logistical concerns.
- **Key takeaway:** present your own thoughts regardless of who proposed an idea. Do not be swayed by seniority or power.
- **Nuance (Rahul's appraisal example):**
  - In performance-appraisal meetings, a manager whose own appraisal depends on a senior who is present will usually stay quiet if their views differ. Practically, speaking up is hard.
  - The instructor acknowledged this. In promotion-type decisions you may genuinely have limited say.
  - In **brainstorming, policy-change, or cross-departmental problem-solving** meetings, where the stakes differ, speaking up and using frameworks like the Six Thinking Hats is appropriate. It depends on the situation.

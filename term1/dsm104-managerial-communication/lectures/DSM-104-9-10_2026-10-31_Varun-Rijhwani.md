### 3.1 Group 3: "The 70-Hour Work Week: Sustainable Growth Strategy or Leadership Failure?"

**Structure:** hook → pro side → nuance → micro evidence → macro evidence → balanced conclusion and recommendations.

**Opening hook (decision-maker framing)**
- You are a CEO with declining growth.
- You email "Starting Monday, we work 70 hours a week," and the deadline is met.
- A month later, do you repeat it?
- Lesson: one success does not justify repetition. Leaders shape **expectations and culture**, not just results.

**Pro side: the "sprint" argument**
- **Founder vs. employee double standard.** As founders we would work 80–90 hours. As employees we demand 40 hours and call ambition "exploitation."
- **Historical comparisons**
  - Japan and Germany in the 1950s worked very long hours (*likely: about 2,400 hours/year*) to rebuild.
  - China's **996 rule** (9 am–9 pm, 6 days/week) is credited for Alibaba and ByteDance.
- **"Coordination tax" rebuttal to "just hire more people."** Communication pathways grow as

```math
\text{pathways} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}
```

  - $`n=5`$ gives $`10`$.
  - $`n=15`$ gives $`105`$.
  - So tripling the team does not triple output; it adds bureaucratic friction.
- **Experience compounding over 5 years** (implicitly about 50 working weeks per year):
  - $`40 \times 50 \times 5 = 10{,}000`$ hours
  - $`70 \times 50 \times 5 = 17{,}500 \approx 18{,}000`$ hours
  - Claim: you compress a decade of growth into half the time. "Not a life sentence, a season of sprinting."

**Nuance: personal agency**
- High intensity is powerful only when **voluntary**. Examples: a parent working overtime, exam preparation, an athlete.
- It becomes toxic when choice is removed, or when uncompensated overwork becomes the baseline.
- Greatness is built in **defined seasons**: short, with a clear goal and deadline, like a product launch.

**Micro level: individual cost**
- The "more hours → more effort → more productivity" chain is questioned.
- 2026 Blind survey, 1,600 Indian professionals: **83% reported burnout**.
- 2026 UKG survey of Indian frontline workers with burnout: **75% lower productivity, 61% more mistakes**.
- **Gujarat case:** during COVID, factories were allowed 72 hours/week (12 h/day). Trade unions challenged it, and the Supreme Court struck it down in 2020.
  - Lesson: economic pressure does not automatically justify a heavier burden on workers.
- **Caveat stated by the group:** the surveys show association, not causation. The concern is extreme hours becoming a *regular expectation* rather than an occasional necessity.

**Macro level: organizational impact**
- Mandating 70 hours is "a lazy system bandage," not a strategy.
- Stanford labour research: output drops sharply beyond a threshold of hours.
- **Goodhart's law** (transcribed as "good arts law"): when hours become the measure of commitment, "choice becomes an illusion." Presence becomes a vanity target, producing "performative late-night emails."
- **Ideal worker norm:** the fiction that the employee has no life outside work.
  - It creates a "silent demographic tax" on caregivers and women.
  - Flexibility-stigma research: demanding "total life surrender" selects for **privilege, not talent**.

**Conclusion and recommendations**
- The answer is "not simply yes or no." Extra hours are fine when **purposeful and temporary**.
- Three recommendations:
  1. **Efficient systems:** better planning and resource allocation; remove unnecessary activity.
  2. **Measure outcomes**, not time spent.
  3. **Sustainable performance:** ambition with realistic expectations.
- Closing line: *"We are not against hard work. We are against normalizing overwork."*

**Instructor feedback and Q&A**
- He wanted examples of companies achieving results *without* 70-hour weeks.
  - The presenter later clarified she had cited 996 companies.
  - The instructor's point: **put the case study visibly on the slide** and walk through it as a company case.
- **Question:** What is the future of the **four-day work week** in India?
  - Answer: India is a developing country with a young population (median age about 28). A **mixture** of approaches is better. China (Jack Ma's defence of 996) was cited as a recent example.
- **Instructor's context:**
  - Gen Z is redefining workload norms.
  - Narayana Murthy's "youth should work 70 hours" remark caused strong public backlash.
  - Post-war Japan and Germany were a different era.

### 3.2 Group 5: "Hybrid & Remote Work: Productivity Gain or Cultural Loss?"

**Interaction device:** the audience was asked to answer in chat whether they prefer hybrid or work-from-office.

**Evolution of work**
- Work from office (shared rhythm, one place) → remote (COVID, digital) → **hybrid** (a purposeful mix).

**The "hybrid operating system"**
- The focus is *how* work happens, not *where*.
- It has three elements: **alignment, coordination, and collaboration**, producing shared direction, rhythm, and practice.

**Culture and productivity are one interdependent system**
- Culture is not just volunteering and team-building.
- Culture sets how people work, and productivity reveals and reinforces those conditions.

**Fixing isolation through deliberate collaboration**
- Replace accidental office conversations with **intentional connections**: scheduled meetings, check-ins, virtual brainstorming.
- **Measure the quality of collaboration**, not the number of meetings:
  - cross-team response times
  - whether meetings produce clear actions and owners

**Digital tools framework: Connect → Coordinate → Decide → Deliver**

| Stage | Tool / practice |
|---|---|
| Connect | Teams / Zoom |
| Coordinate | Jira (tasks owned and tracked) |
| Decide | Decision logs, clear deadlines |
| Deliver | Visible milestones, fewer hand-off delays |

- KPIs: decision cycle time, tool adoption, on-time milestones.
- Measure **outcomes and cycle times**, not login hours or message volume.

**Evidence**
- A study of **1,612 employees** (*likely the Trip.com RCT, Bloom et al., Nature 2024*) found hybrid workers had **about one-third lower resignation rates with no decline in performance**.

**Case: Spotify "Work From Anywhere" (2021)**
- Employees choose home, office, or anywhere, depending on role.
- The **distributed-first** meeting design gives remote participants an equal voice, so they are not "second-class participants."
- Principle: **"right place for the right task."**
  - Focused individual work happens at home.
  - Brainstorming, planning, and problem-solving happen in person.
- HR leadership reported **no negative impact on productivity**.
- Lesson: flexibility alone is not enough. It needs inclusion design.

**Commute economics**
- Average urban one-way commute is about **27 min**, at about **₹1,044/month**.
- Remote work 2 days/week saves $`27 \times 2 \times 2 = 108 \approx 2`$ hours/week.
- The saved time goes to rest and family; fuel and travel costs fall.

**Well-being and challenges**
- Well-being covers physical health, mental health, and the ability to focus.
- Fully remote work risks losing:
  - informal learning and mentoring
  - personal connection
  - wellbeing, through too many online meetings and poor communication that add stress
- Flexibility needs **connection, support, and belonging**.

**Intentional hybrid design**
- **Remote:** focused individual tasks.
- **Office:** collaboration, coaching, creativity, relationship-building.

**Recommendations**
1. **Judge the work, not the location:** quality, deadlines, goals.
2. **Make office days useful:** discussion, mentoring, brainstorming, not just attendance.
3. **Keep everyone in the loop:** remote staff get equal access to updates and decisions.
4. **Trial and review:** pilot for a set period, collect feedback, measure.

Closing message: "Plan, measure, listen, keep improving." Focus less on *where* people work and more on *how* they work together.

**Instructor feedback and Q&A**
- He liked the personal **avatars**.
- **Question:** What challenges does the C-suite face implementing, for example, 3 days in office and 2 at home?
  - Group's answer:
    - suits tech more than manufacturing or continuous operations
    - only about 50–53% hybrid adoption in India
    - collaboration is harder
    - cost considerations
- **Key lesson (exam/workplace relevant):** the presentation was one-sided in favour of hybrid work. When proposing an idea to senior management (hybrid work, a four-day week, anything), give a **360-degree view**: benefits to employees, benefits to the company, **and challenges**. Also include **company examples**.

### 3.3 Group 1: "AI & Automation: Reskill or Restructure?"

**Hook:** "Imagine going to work tomorrow and finding an AI does your job in seconds… the future is already here."

**Three ways AI changes work**
1. **Automate:** repetitive tasks go to machines.
2. **Augment:** employees use AI for efficiency and high-value work.
3. **Reorganize:** roles and responsibilities must be rethought.

**Contrast case: Klarna vs. IBM (same technology, different outcomes)**

| | Klarna | IBM |
|---|---|---|
| What it did | Replaced customer support with AI, claiming it did the work of about 700 agents | Automated some HR tasks |
| What happened | Went too far and started **rehiring humans** | **Redeployed** people to other departments; total headcount **rose** |

**Data: World Economic Forum projection to 2030**
- **92 million** jobs displaced and **170 million** created, a net gain of $`170 - 92 = 78`$ million.
- **39% of core skills** will change (engineering, analysis, diagnosis).
- Thesis: **"AI replaces tasks, not jobs."** Decide task by task.

**Task-sorting framework: a 2×2 on two axes**
- How reliably can AI do the task?
- How important is human judgment?

| AI reliability | Human judgment | Action | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| High | High | **Augment / reskill** the worker | Coding, analysis, diagnosis |
| High | Low | **Automate** | Invoice processing, data entry, HR queries |
| Lower | High | **Keep human** | Client management, escalations |
| Unclear | Unclear | **Wait; don't pivot yet** | — |

**Two leadership shortcuts, both traps**
- **Shortcut A: cut first.**
  - Visible savings, and markets applaud.
  - But undocumented know-how walks out, trust collapses, and when the AI falls short you rehire. This is the "bill" Klarna paid.
- **Shortcut B: train first.**
  - Preserves knowledge and trust, but it is slow and productivity dips.
  - If there is no new role at the end, the training is wasted.
  - **TCS** trained about **5.5 lakh** employees and still cut about **12,000** roles.
- **Lesson:** the order matters. **Redesign roles first, then reskill people into them.**

**India angle: restructuring "at the hiring gate"**
- There are no mass layoffs. Firms simply **hire less** at entry level.
- In FY26 about **7,000 fewer jobs** across the top 5 IT firms, the first net decline in about 20 years. The TCS CEO said they would not downsize but would hire less.
- Who absorbs this: entry-level applicants, which describes most of the class.
- Long-term cost: cutting hiring at the bottom today means no experts at the top in 10 years. **Protect the first rung.**

**The four moves, in order**
1. **Map tasks:** break each role into tasks and sort them with the 2×2.
2. **Redesign roles:** rebundle the remaining tasks into new roles *before* cutting anyone.
3. **Reskill into a named destination role**, not generic "AI literacy."
4. **Protect rung one:** keep apprentice and entry roles.

Closing quote (Andrej Karpathy): *"Build Iron Man suits, not Iron Man robots."* Klarna cut first; IBM redesigned first.

**Q&A**
- **Question:** Which industries besides software are being hit, and how are they responding?
  - Group's answer:
    - manufacturing, PCB design, and woodworking are affected, though less so
    - these industries are slow to adapt ("if it ain't broke, don't fix it")
    - the right move is a judgment call: a carpenter shouldn't buy AI CNC machines, but a mass door or table manufacturer should

### 3.4 Group 4: Gen Z vs. Organizational Expectations (role-play board meeting)

**Format:** the group **role-played a board briefing**. Each member took a persona: Chief HR Officer, Gen Z associate, senior manager, data/analytics lead, external consultant, and welfare officer. This was a strong use of audience framing.

**CHRO opening**
- Hook: a stat on the multiplier by which **workplace culture outweighs salary in predicting voluntary turnover**. The figure is not clearly audible (*likely the MIT Sloan finding of about $`10.4\times`$*).
- Claim: no high performer left for pay; they left due to a breakdown in operational infrastructure.
- Thesis: **"Modernize the means, preserve the mission."**
- The friction is neither a Gen Z work-ethic crisis nor a leadership failure. It is an **infrastructural mismatch**: industrial-era management systems applied to a digital-native workforce.
  - "20th-century factory-clock metrics on 21st-century digital minds" destroy trust, accelerate burnout, and alienate top contributors.
- Data:
  - Gen Z is **>27%** of the global workforce.
  - **94%** prioritize continuous skill-building over tenure.
  - **74%** use AI daily.
  - Measuring presence over contribution means paying an "attrition tax."
- Three pillars of the briefing:
  1. frontline drivers
  2. operational imperatives
  3. mutual adaptation framework

**Gen Z associate: personal narrative**
- **Bangalore, 2023:** a senior told new joiners that to climb the ladder they must arrive first and leave after their seniors.
- **2026:** the same expectations persist. Logging out on time invites comments, and seniors check in during leave, which blurs boundaries.
- **Mail-merge story:** she personalized emails to individual users instead of one generic CC email. Her senior told her to follow "whatever the hierarchy decided."
  - Lesson: the emphasis shifted from teamwork to hierarchy, even though Gen Z is simultaneously asked to innovate with AI.
- Message: Gen Z is not rebelling. They understand deadlines and will put in extra effort, but want boundaries respected. **"An exception should not become an expectation."**

**Senior manager: leadership perspective**
- The boardroom refrain: "Why can't this new generation work the way we did?"
- Leadership's mandate is operational rigor, accountability, and scalable growth.
- Cascading goals, each with a practice that clashes with Gen Z expectations:

| Goal | Practice used to reach it |
|---|---|
| $100M annual revenue | Structured top-down communication |
| +40% release velocity | Fixed schedules |
| 99% system availability | Annual performance reviews |
| Zero major technical errors | (individual-level target) |

- **Four fault lines:**
  1. time vs. output
  2. annual vs. instant
  3. profit vs. purpose
  4. loyalty vs. boundaries

**Analytics lead: three tensions, each grounded in theory**
1. **Presence vs. performance**
   - The organization needs supervision and client coverage; employees want autonomy and outcome-based evaluation.
   - Gallup data on hybrid preference (*figure unclear*).
   - **Self-determination theory:** autonomy works when responsibilities are clear.
2. **Hierarchy vs. feedback**
   - Annual appraisals do not give timely coaching. Deloitte 2025: **86%** of Gen Z emphasize this (*wording unclear*).
   - Managers expect initiative while employees expect proactive guidance.
   - Theories: **leader-member exchange (LMX)** and **procedural justice**, which emphasize trust and fair support.
3. **Process vs. digital agility**
   - Approvals vs. efficiency. Deloitte 2026: **74%** of Gen Z use AI at work.
   - **Role clarity** and the **psychological contract** explain why permissions and expectations must be made explicit.

- **Consequences**
  - **40%** frequently stressed (2025); **58%** report digital fatigue (2026).
  - **Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model:** excessive demands undermine wellbeing.
  - **44%** have left a role lacking purpose; only **54%** believe their team could keep performing if a key expert left.
  - **Person–organization fit** theory explains this.
- **Caveat stated:** these are descriptive findings, not causal estimates, and do not prove that age causes conflict.

**External consultant: recommendations**
- Diagnosis: "Not a generation problem, a **measurement** problem. When managers can't see outcomes, they watch presence; when employees can't see reasons, they push back."
- Motto: **"Flex the how, fix the what"** (transcribed as "fix the work").
- Three changes, each asking something of both sides:
  1. **Outcome-driven KPIs**
     - Measure sprint commitment, defect rate, and client satisfaction instead of seat time.
     - Gen Z gets autonomy but no free pass, because outcomes are harder to hide behind. (Cited the same hybrid study of about 1,600 employees: one-third lower resignations, no change in promotions or performance.)
  2. **Agile feedback**
     - A 15-minute check-in every sprint instead of one surprise annual appraisal.
     - Infosys and TCS have moved to continuous feedback.
     - Gallup: about **80%** of employees who got meaningful feedback in the past week are fully engaged.
     - Caveat: if you want weekly feedback, be ready to hear "this isn't good enough" weekly.
  3. **Reverse mentoring**
     - Pair a junior engineer with a delivery head. The junior teaches AI tools; the senior teaches what no AI can, such as calming an angry client the night before a release.
     - A company running this since 2008 was cited.
- **Fixed, non-negotiable:** security, deadlines, client commitments.
- **Pilot:** one team for one quarter. Track attrition, delivery, and client satisfaction, then scale.
- *"Autonomy isn't a right you claim; it's a trust you earn by delivering."*

**Welfare officer: closing**
- The issue is the gap between employee expectations and how organizations traditionally operate.
- What each side needs:
  - **Employees:** autonomy, flexibility, meaningful work, recognition, work–life balance.
  - **Organization:** reliable delivery, SLAs, quality, compliance, clear goals.
- **Professional commitments** (deadlines, collaboration, quality, KPIs/SLAs) and **personal wellbeing** (physical health, recharge time, personal commitments, flexibility with team agreements) go hand in hand.
- "Not a choice between people and performance; create conditions where both are sustained."

**Q&A**
- **Question:** Beyond KPIs and SLAs, what can companies do for the wellbeing of *all* generations (millennials, boomers, etc.)?
- Answers:
  - reverse mentoring for cross-generational understanding
  - cut unnecessary meetings
  - set realistic goals
  - quarterly reviews
  - **informal activities such as sports**: on the cricket field, hierarchy and age fade
- The instructor endorsed the last answer ("Absolutely. Great.").

### 3.5 Group 2: "The Right to Disconnect"

**Agenda**
1. What is the right?
2. Why do off-hours matter?
3. Why is disconnecting hard?
4. How can law protect business agility?
5. Should it be a legal right?

**Hook scenario**
- A 9-to-6 day; you log off at 6 PM, and at **9:47 PM** an "urgent" message arrives. Must you respond tonight?
- **Parkinson's law** (paraphrased): when you allow people more time, work expands to fill it and people become less structured.
- **Definition:** the right to disconnect is the employee's right to be offline outside working hours.

**Why it matters**
- Eurofound: **>80%** of employees receive work calls and messages after hours.
- Why they respond: a sense of responsibility, meeting expectations, and fear of consequences.
- Impacts:
  1. **mental health:** no real break, rising stress
  2. **physical health:** lost sleep and meals
  3. **personal life:** family, relationships, hobbies
  4. **performance:** rested employees return with better focus and energy
- Goal: help people perform well *during* work, not keep them connected for as many hours as possible.

**Root causes (organizational)**
1. **Unclear ownership:** no boundaries on availability, so people feel responsible for every message.
2. **Time-based control:** measuring hours and response speed rather than delivery. "Being busy ≠ being productive."
3. **Micromanagement:** constant monitoring and demands for instant updates.
4. **Capacity gaps:** unrealistic workloads, poor deadline planning, understaffing.

**Evidence (Eurofound)**
- **75%** responded because it was *expected*; **61%** feared negative consequences.
- Key insight: *technology enables connection; management expectations determine whether we feel compelled to stay connected.* A rule alone isn't enough. Management practices must change, while genuine emergencies are still handled.

**Solution 1: the CREST legislative framework**
- **C – Charter:** each firm publishes rules on extra hours and the related compensation.
- **R – Right to refuse:** employees may refuse off-duty work with no negative consequences.
- **E – Emergencies:** after-hours work is allowed only for genuinely urgent or important cases.
- **S – Scale:** rules scale with firm size. Start with large firms, since strict rules could hurt small ones.
- **T – Teeth to enforce:** an external authority plus an internal cell that monitors and protects these rights.

Context cited:
- a private member's bill in Parliament (*Supriya Sule; stated as 2015, the bill is generally dated 2018*)
- a Kerala assembly bill
- the Indian labour codes
- foreign examples, including Australia

**Solution 2: the RAG urgency rule** (transcribed as "9am rule"; used in Australia)
- 🔴 **Red (urgent):** must be done now or overnight. It must be labelled as such when assigned.
- 🟠 **Amber (important, not urgent):** can wait until **9 AM the next day**.
- 🟢 **Green (routine):** no urgency; handled in the normal course of work.

**Solution 3: "Switch OFF," for what firms can do from tomorrow**
- **O:** leaders model the behaviour.
  - Example: leaders who arrive early and stay late set a "presence" norm that teams copy, even though presence says nothing about outcomes.
- **F – Firewall:** e.g., **Volkswagen** stops email delivery to employees from 30 minutes after their shift ends.
- **F – Fine:** firms compensate off-hours work at $`2\times`$ to $`3\times`$.
- Visual on the slide: a toggle. When work switches OFF, "life switches ON."

**Conclusion**
- Should it be legally required? **Yes, "with a little common sense."**
- The "Hey, just a quick question" at 10 PM is rarely quick.
- But a system crash at 10 PM needs action. "Sorry, our right to disconnect starts at 6 PM" isn't acceptable either.
- How the three solutions fit together:
  - **CREST** gives legal protection.
  - **RAG** separates real emergencies from messages that can wait.
  - **OFF** reminds managers to measure results, not WhatsApp response time.
- Callback to the 9:47 PM message: routine work waits until tomorrow, and genuine emergencies follow a plan.
- Closing lines:
  - *"Protect the right, plan for emergencies, respect the boundaries."*
  - True agility is a workplace that functions even when employees disconnect.
  - "Our phones may be smart, but that doesn't mean we should be [always on]."

**Q&A**
- **Question:** India's culture rewards obedience (a legacy of colonial rule). Can employees really afford not to respond after hours?
- Group's answer: awareness is key, plus cultural change through policies and training. Some Indian organizations already do this.
- Instructor's synthesis:
  - Policies exist; what must change is **mindset**.
  - That requires awareness, empathy for the incoming generation, and redesigned workflows.
  - It also matters for working professionals pursuing further education.

### 3.6 Group 6: "Talent vs. Experience: Who Gets the Job?"

**Roadmap:** review the empirical evidence, test Coolidge's verdict, and end with a practical hiring framework.

**Anchor quote: Calvin Coolidge** (stated as the 38th US president; he was actually the **30th**)
> "Nothing in the world can take the place of persistence. Talent will not… Genius will not… Education will not… Persistence and determination alone are omnipotent."

- Central question: does this hold when building a team, for example when screening 200 CVs on a Monday?

**Two candidates**
- **Candidate A, the Veteran**
  - 10 years in role, proven track record, industry judgment, pattern recognition ("knows his way around the coffee machine").
  - Risk: entrenched habits and a flattening learning curve.
- **Candidate B, the Challenger**
  - High cognitive ability, rapid adaptation, thrives with new tech stacks.
  - Risk: less day-one domain knowledge; unproven under real pressure.
- Joke: both CVs say "results-driven team player."

**Experience as a hiring signal**
- "5–7 years required" is the **most common** screening method.
- But researchers have studied it far less than general mental ability (GMA). It is used because it is cheap, easy to count, and easy to defend to an executive committee.
- **Meta-analysis** (*Van Iddekinge et al.; stated as 2018*): **74 studies, about 24,000 people**.
  - Corrected correlation between prior experience and job performance: $`r = 0.07`$.
  - Variance explained: $`r^2 = 0.07^2 = 0.0049`$, i.e. **less than 1%**.
  - Experience also does not predict commitment, satisfaction, or retention.
- Taglines:
  - "Years of experience is the participation trophy of hiring."
  - "10 years of experience can mean 1 year repeated 10 times."

**Where experience *does* help: three boundary conditions**
1. **Day-one ramp speed:** a small onboarding advantage that flattens as peers learn the workflow.
2. **Lower cognitive demand:** the link is strongest in repetitive work, where muscle memory matters (sample of about 16,000 workers).
3. **Complexity ceiling:** in complex, novel, or fast-changing work, returns diminish and problem-solving agility takes over.

- Takeaway: *"Tenure gives you a head start in the sprint, but cognitive capability wins the marathon."*

**But talent alone was also "marked down"**
- **Schmidt & Hunter (1998):** GMA validity of about $`0.51`$, the gold standard for about 20 years.
- **Sackett et al. (2022)** corrected over-adjustments in the older methods:
  - GMA fell to about $`0.31`$; some recent samples show around $`0.03`$ *(as stated)*.
  - The new top predictor is the **structured interview at about $`0.42`$**.
- "Even the research had to be re-interviewed." Neither pure experience nor pure talent is safe.

**Persistence / grit (Coolidge's favourite)**
- Popularized by Angela Duckworth.
- **Credé, Tynan & Harms (2017)** meta-analysis (sample counts garbled in the transcript; the published study used about 88 samples and about 66,000 people):
  - Grit has only a **modest** correlation with workplace performance and retention.
  - Grit overlaps heavily with **conscientiousness**: $`\rho \approx 0.84`$. So grit is "largely conscientiousness repackaged," or as the slide put it, **"Grit is conscientiousness in a hoodie."**
  - The **perseverance of effort** facet predicts reasonably well; the **consistency of interest** (passion) facet adds almost nothing.
- Verdict: persistence matters but is **not omnipotent**. Without ability, strategy, and fit, it becomes "stubbornness."

**The answer: stack predictors (multivariate validity)**
- GMA plus a **work sample** or **structured interview** gives a combined validity of about $`0.60`$–$`0.65`$. Adding **integrity tests** helps further.
- This holds for both entry-level and experienced hiring.
- "Stacking predictors beats picking camps": test how people **think** *and* how they **perform**.

**Case: IBM skills-first hiring**
- Problem: the four-year degree was being used as a proxy for skill.
- IBM removed degree requirements from **>50% of US job postings** where the degree wasn't essential, and tested hands-on skills and cognitive ability directly.
- About **20% of US hires** joined without a four-year degree, with performance standards maintained.
- Lesson: credentials are lazy proxies; test the capability itself.

**Case: Google Project Oxygen (leadership level)**
- Great managers weren't simply the veterans. What set them apart was **behaviour**: coaching, empowering teams, clear communication, good judgment.
- When promoting, ask what someone *actually does*, not how long they've been there.
- Tenure builds domain credibility; **observable leadership behaviour shows readiness**.

**Hiring decision framework (role-dependent)**

| Role type | Weight | How to test |
|---|---|---|
| Executive and safety-critical roles | Experience | Track-record audit or scenario simulation |
| Programming, creative, entry-level roles | Talent and learning agility | Work sample or structured interview |

**Worked example: hiring a data scientist**
1. **Write the JD as skills** (strong SQL, solid ML grasp), not "4–7 years as a data scientist." *Years are a proxy; skill is the requirement.*
2. Give every candidate a **real SQL task or small modelling problem**, and ask **everyone the same questions**.
3. **Score each component separately.** Suppose an 8-year candidate struggles with the modelling task while a 2-year candidate solves it well and picks up the new tool quickly. A blended "gut feel" lets experience hide that gap; separate scores reveal it.
4. Have **2–3 raters score the same task with the same rubric**. If one gives 9/10 and another 5/10, **discuss the gap before deciding**. This counters individual bias.

**Conclusion**
- "Coolidge was half right": persistence and capability beat credentials alone, but neither is omnipotent without role fit.
- Experience is a **signal, not proof** of capability.
- Call to action: next time you write a JD, replace "5–7 years" with the skill you actually need.
- Closing question to the audience: "If two candidates deliver today's result, what evidence shows who is ready for tomorrow?"

**Q&A**
- **Question:** For a high-potential, low-experience candidate, what evidence should the organization rely on?
- Answer:
  - It depends on the work. Experience matters where muscle memory and field familiarity matter; innate agility matters in fast-changing tech roles.
  - The presenter cited extra research: founders and executives producing top 0.1% returns averaged about **49.57 years** of age, and executives over 50 with a solid track record were about $`1.8\times`$ more likely to transform a company.
  - "It's never black or white; it's always in the grey."

## 5. Exam, Assignment and Evaluation Pointers

**Graded components and rules**
- The Google Form upload of the deck **counts for evaluation**.
- Each speaker gets 2–2.5 min; the group gets 14 min (5 members) or 16.5 min (6 members). **Overrun content is not evaluated.**
- **Group dynamics: 10 marks.** Poor time distribution among members costs these marks.
- Individual feedback was noted for each presenter, and grades with feedback pointers will cover the presentation and the individual assignment.

**What the instructor valued, from his feedback across groups**
- **A 360-degree view:** cover benefits to employees and to the company, *and* the challenges and counter-arguments. This is especially important when pitching to senior management.
- **Concrete company case studies shown visibly on slides**, e.g. firms that succeed without 70-hour weeks, or firms running four-day weeks.
- **Use of course structures:** the preview-view-review structure was explicitly praised.
- Audience engagement devices worked well: hooks, scenarios, chat polls, avatars, and role-play personas.

**Presentation techniques demonstrated across groups (reusable patterns)**
- **Opening hooks:** a "put yourself in the CEO's seat" scenario, a "9:47 PM message" story, a provocative quote (Coolidge), or a striking statistic.
- **Signposting:** an agenda slide and explicit handovers to the next speaker ("Over to you, X, who will explain…").
- **Callback endings:** return to the opening scenario in the conclusion.
- **Acronym frameworks** for memorability: CREST, RAG, OFF, and the four AI moves.
- **Balanced theses:** "We're not against X; we're against normalizing X."
- **Caveats about evidence:** state that findings are correlational, not causal. This builds ethos.

# DSM-104 Managerial Communication, Session 8 (24 Oct 2026, Varun Rijhwani)
## Topic: Persuasive Communication (Ethos, Pathos, Logos) and Final Presentation Briefing

---

## 1. Overview

This was the **final teaching session** of the course. It opened with a student-led recap of the previous session on **presentation structure** (Preview → View → Review, the PREP framework, and the call to action). The main topic was the **content** of a presentation: how to make that structure persuasive. The class defined **persuasion** and separated it from **manipulation**. An in-class exercise compared three versions of a ₹5 crore cybersecurity pitch, which introduced **rhetoric** and its three appeals: **ethos** (credibility), **pathos** (emotional appeal / why the audience should care), and **logos** (logic and evidence). The instructor covered:
- the "two Rs" of evidence (recency and relevance),
- how to balance the appeals for different audiences,
- the trap associated with over-relying on each appeal.

The class then analysed a clip from *Made in India: A Titan Story*, in which Xerxes Desai pitches the watch business to JRD Tata, to identify each appeal and the order in which it was used. The session closed with logistics and evaluation rules for next weekend's **final group presentation**.

## 2. Topics and Concepts Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap of the previous session: Presentation Structure
Summarised by a student (Rahul), with additions from the instructor.

**A. Preview (opening section)**
- **Attention grabber.** The first few minutes are where you win over the audience.
- **Preview statement:** what you are going to speak about.
- **Overview statement** (the instructor said this was missed in the recap): list the points the presentation will cover.
- **Positioning statement:** give the context (why you are saying this) and state the position you are taking on the topic.
  - Keep it concise. Do not drag or digress, because presentations are time-bound.
- **Things to avoid:**
  - Cracking jokes.
  - Overloading slides with content or detail. When slides are crowded, the audience reads them and stops listening to the speaker.

**B. View (main body / "meat" of the presentation)**
- Develop the **key takeaway messages**: what the audience should take away and what is in it for them.
- Frame each key message using the **PREP framework**:
  - **P**osition: state your point.
  - **R**easons: why it holds.
  - **E**xamples: evidence or illustrations.
  - **P**osition: restate your point.

**C. Review (closing section)**
- **Summarise** the main points the audience should leave with.
- The close should be **focused and converging**. It should leave an impression and should not open up a new presentation.
- End with a **Call to Action (CTA)**.
  - The CTA depends on what the presentation asks the audience to do and on how involved the audience is.
  - *Previous-class example (opening a manufacturing unit in China):* the CTA asked the audience to fill in a form with certain information. Based on their entries, the presenter would provide an estimate of the business opportunity.
  - *For the final presentation:* the CTA is to seek **approval or a "green signal" from senior management** for your pitch.

> Transition to today: the structure is the skeleton. Today's focus is on making it **meaningful and impactful** using the principles of persuasive communication.

### 2.2 What is Persuasion?
Class-generated definitions:
- Convincing the audience.
- Making them believe your point of view is also right.
- Influencing the audience to think or act in line with an objective you have defined.

**Instructor's definition:** persuasion means convincing and influencing, i.e. getting the audience to see a thing or phenomenon your way. It does **not** mean manipulating. There is a subtle line between the two, and crossing it turns persuasion into manipulation.

### 2.3 Persuasion vs. Manipulation

| Persuasion | Manipulation |
|---|---|
| Facts are laid open and the process is transparent | Facts are concealed or hidden, and the process is dishonest or deceptive |
| Supported by information and data | Information is withheld from the other person |
| Reasons and arguments are honest | Reasons and arguments are deceptive |
| The other person keeps **free will** and a choice of action | Free will is taken away, and the person is led or coerced into a particular action |
| Based on **sound argument** | Exploits the audience's **vulnerabilities**, such as insecurities, fear or greed |

**Key line:** persuasiveness rests on **sound argument**. Manipulation takes advantage of the audience's vulnerability and denies them a choice.

### 2.4 In-Class Exercise: "Which pitch would persuade you?"

**Scenario:** You are on the executive committee. The CISO wants **₹5 crore** for a major cybersecurity upgrade. You read three versions of the pitch and decide which makes you most inclined to approve it, with reasons.

The slides were shown on screen and not read aloud. The content below is reconstructed from the discussion.

- **Pitch A.** The speaker emphasises their own experience: *18 years managing cybersecurity across banking and technology organisations*. On the basis of what "my team and I" are observing in the current system, they recommend the upgrade. **This is ethos.**
- **Pitch B.** The speaker points to what happened to a **comparable company/competitor**, says the same could happen to us, and stresses that **customer information** is at stake. It answers *why should we care* and *what is at stake*. Some students felt it was exaggerated. **This is pathos.**
- **Pitch C.** The speaker gives figures: current exposure, the investment of **₹5 crore**, and an exposure reduction of **₹8 crore** after the investment. **This is logos.**
  - Implied net benefit, as a student reasoned: $8 - 5 = 3$ crore reduction in exposure beyond the cost.

**Class vote:** everyone chose **C**. Reasons given:
- It is clearly defined, concise and factual.
- Figures "speak for themselves."
- Committee members have a top-level view of the losses, so A and B felt generic.

**Lesson (the "catch"):** **no single pitch is best on its own.** Each one is incomplete:
- A has credibility but gives no reason to care.
- B gives a reason to care but no proof.
- C gives numbers without credibility or a reason to care.

Effective persuasion **combines all three**. These three types together are called **rhetoric**.

### 2.5 Rhetoric: Ethos, Pathos, Logos

- **Rhetoric** is the art of persuasion.
  - Exam note: rhetoric is **not** the same as a "rhetorical question."
- It is carried out through three appeals:

**1. Ethos (credibility and trust)**
- What makes **you** the right person to speak on this topic: your experience, qualifications and background.
- It gives the audience a reason to listen to you in the first place, and so it earns their trust.
- Ethos can belong to the **organisation**, not only the individual. A company that has been in business a long time already has established credibility.

**2. Pathos (emotional appeal)**
- Answers **why the audience should care** and what is at stake.
- Emotional appeal does **not** mean making the audience sad or making them cry.
  - Negative or exploitative pathos becomes manipulation, as discussed in 2.3.
  - The goal is to make them **care** about the topic, e.g. why they should invest in the cybersecurity upgrade.

**3. Logos (logic and evidence)**
- Relies on argument, reasoning, reports, statistics and numbers: "this is the logic, hence this is the conclusion."
- **Cite your data** so that it is more credible.
- Logos is **not only numbers**. Market observation or research, such as spotting a gap in the market, also counts as logic and evidence. This point came up in the Titan clip.

**The Two Rs of evidence (for logos):**
1. **Recency.** Data should be recent. Citing a 1996 study looks dated and raises questions about the quality of your argument.
2. **Relevance.** The study or evidence must relate directly to your topic. Irrelevant evidence looks like digression or poor preparation. It weakens the evidence, and then the whole argument falls apart.

---

### 2.6 Balancing the Three Appeals (Audience-Dependent)

- An ideal persuasive pitch **balances all three**. It should not overload the audience with numbers, with emotion, or with "I, me, myself."
- **Why balance matters.** If you rely only on pathos, for example storytelling, the **skeptics or cynics** in the audience will object.
  - *Skeptics/cynics* are listeners who doubt the information or its credibility.
  - They will ask: "The story is convincing, but where are the numbers? Where are the returns?" You need **logos** to satisfy them.
- **Tailor the emphasis to the audience:**

| Audience | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| Finance- or data-oriented manager | Lean on **logos**, but still use some pathos |
| Storyteller or marketer manager | Lean on **pathos** (why the audience should care) |
| You are new or have little experience in the area | Make sure you establish **ethos** early so the audience has a reason to listen |

- **Student Q&A (Rahul): what if the audience is mixed (juniors and seniors, unknown backgrounds)?**
  - With a mass audience you cannot analyse each member.
  - **Answer:** balance all three and do not go overboard with any of them.
- **Order is flexible.** You do not have to follow ethos → pathos → logos.
  - You can open with a story (pathos), bring in ethos in the middle, and end with logos.
  - Ethos does not have to come first. It can emerge naturally as you describe what happened, which shows your experience.
- **Introspective exercise:** ask yourself which appeal is your **default**, then deliberately include the ones you tend to ignore.
  - The aim of the course is to rise above your "default self" and make your communication comprehensive and wholesome.

### 2.7 Traps in Using Each Appeal

| Appeal | Trap | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | Talking about your title, position and tenure instead of earning trust in the room. The talk becomes "I, me, myself" with no substance. | Establish credibility briefly, then move on and let the work speak. |
| **Pathos** | **Leading with emotion before the audience is ready.** This comes across as manipulative. | Build **rapport** first, then bring in storytelling or emotional appeal. |
| **Logos** | **Information overload**, and assuming the numbers will "sell themselves" without explanation. | Deliver information in a staggered way. Numbers also need to tell a story, so explain the story behind them. |

- **General rule:** do not begin with too much of any one appeal. Take a **progressive** approach.
- **Student Q (Nal): example of the pathos trap?**
  - People confuse emotional appeal with stirring emotions, e.g. making the audience cry, feel bad or feel sad. That moves toward manipulation.
  - There is a thin line between making the audience care for good reasons and making them emotional with arguments that do not hold up. Pathos starts to fall apart once you cross it.

### 2.8 Case Clip: *Made in India: A Titan Story*

**Context**
- **Xerxes Desai**, founder of Titan, gets about **60 seconds** (in the clip he asks for "2 minutes") to pitch **JRD Tata** on pivoting into the **watch business**.
- It is the first conversation about the idea, and everything is at stake.

**What to watch for**
- The three appeals and the specific lines that show each one.
- His physical presence and gestures.
- His **unself-conscious confidence**. He is speaking to a legend but is not deterred from arguing for what he believes is right.

**Gist of the scene** (the audio was patchy in the transcript)
- Nokia is pivoting to the cell phone business. Desai wants to pivot away from Tata Press.
- JRD asks, "Just because Nokia is pivoting…?"
- Desai: "Press is yesterday…"
- JRD: watchmaking is "a very difficult, very delicate business."
- Desai asks for 2 minutes and makes the pitch:
  - Lakmé is "a brand that changed the way women look at themselves," but there is nothing comparable for men.
  - The watch as a symbol of status and achievement, for film stars, doctors and lawyers.
  - "We are the best candidates to do this."
- JRD: "Your Hindi has definitely improved… think about it."
- In the series, JRD does think about it and the business gets going. The instructor recommended watching it.

**Class analysis**
- **Is there ethos?** Some students said no, because they already know and trust each other: the line about his Hindi improving shows a long relationship.
  - **Instructor's view:** ethos is present, at the **end**: "We are the best candidates to do this."
  - That credibility comes from **Tata's established brand trust and quality reputation**. A new company would not have this when entering a new domain. This is organisational ethos.
- **Logos:**
  - Identifying a **gap in the market**: Tata made Lakmé for women, but there is nothing for men. The instructor confirmed this is logos, a reasoned observation from history and the market, even without numbers.
  - The limited options: pivot, or IPO, which is not viable right now. So pivoting is the logical choice.
  - "No one is making premium watches in India" was also cited as logos.
- **Pathos:**
  - Making JRD care about the space: the watch is not just for those whose "time is good" but for those who want to **change their time**.
  - Status and achievement imagery.
- **Student Q (Jasra): why is the Lakmé example logos and not pathos?**
  - **Answer:** it is a combination. The gap (Lakmé for women, nothing for men) is a logical, data- or history-based observation.
  - He then gives that angle an **emotional framing** to make JRD care.
  - This is **logos used to build into pathos**. The reverse also works, depending on the audience.
- **Sequence used: Logos → Pathos → Ethos.**
  - Desai could choose this order because he knew his audience well, i.e. he had done his **audience analysis**.
  - The instructor linked this to the audience-analysis model from the first class, transcribed as "paybok." This is likely **PAIBOC**: Purpose, Audience, Information, Benefits, Objections, Context.

**Lessons from the clip**
1. A strong pitch uses all three appeals.
2. **Sequence is audience-driven.** If you know the audience, choose the order to suit them. If you cannot analyse the audience (a mass audience), balance all three.
3. Confidence and physical presence add to persuasiveness.

## 3. Final Group Presentation: Guidelines and Evaluation (Important)

**Schedule**
- **Next Saturday:** two back-to-back sessions of group presentations. This is the end of the course.

**Time and rehearsal**
- A **timer** will be used. **Anything spoken beyond the allotted time will not be evaluated.**
- Rehearse your own part, and also **rehearse together as a group online** to check the total time and decide what to tweak.

**Individual requirements**
- **Each member must speak for at least 2 minutes** to qualify for evaluation.
- **Absence or not presenting gets an automatic F.**

**Transitions (will be checked)**
- Do not hand over abruptly, and do not go silent and let the next person just start.
- Close your part and **signpost what comes next and who is presenting it.** For example:
  - "That was the marketing and advertising brief from my side. To talk about the financials, I have Niha, who will explain where the money is going."
- Speakers do **not** need to introduce themselves individually at each handover.
- **Team introductions (optional):**
  - Given the time constraints, the first speaker *may* briefly introduce the team.
  - Alternatively, use a closing **"Meet our team" thank-you slide** with photos, designations and brief qualifications.

**Primacy and recency effects (will be checked)**
- **Primacy effect:** how you open. It should grab attention.
- **Recency effect:** how you close.
- These are the two parts the audience remembers most. The **View** section is the "meat" of the presentation.

**Structure and content**
- Follow the **Preview → View → Review** structure.
- Use **PREP** for key messages.
- End with a **CTA**. For this assignment, that means seeking **senior management's approval or green signal**.
- Apply a **balance of ethos, pathos and logos**, avoiding the traps listed in 2.7.

**Materials**
- The instructor will email the **slides on presentation structure** (from the previous class) after the session.
- Contact the instructor with any questions.

### Quick-Revision Summary
- **Persuasion** is convincing through sound, transparent argument that preserves free will. **Manipulation** conceals facts, exploits vulnerabilities and removes choice.
- **Rhetoric** (the art of persuasion, not a "rhetorical question") works through three appeals:
  - **Ethos:** credibility and trust (personal or organisational).
  - **Pathos:** why the audience should care (not making them cry).
  - **Logos:** logic and evidence (not only numbers). Apply the **two Rs: Recency and Relevance.**
- **Balance** the three. Lean toward one based on the audience. Use an equal balance for a mass or unknown audience. **The order is flexible.**
- **Traps:**
  - Ethos: relying on title and tenure.
  - Pathos: emotion before rapport.
  - Logos: data overload, or numbers with no story.
- **Cybersecurity pitch exercise:** A = ethos, B = pathos, C = logos. None works alone.
- **Titan clip:** Logos (market gap) → Pathos ("change their time") → Ethos ("we are the best candidates").

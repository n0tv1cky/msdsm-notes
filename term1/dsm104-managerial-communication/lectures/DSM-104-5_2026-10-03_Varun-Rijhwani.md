This session closed the written-communication module. It began with a recap of Session 3 (essentials of managerial writing: the 7 C's checklist and Level 5 Leadership's "mirror and window" mindset) and Session 4 (routine, positive, or neutral messages: subject lines, the direct approach, and the inverted pyramid). The main topic was **negative or bad-news messages**. A bad-news message has two jobs: delivering an unchangeable decision clearly, and preserving the relationship. To do both, writers use **tact**, a **buffer**, and usually an **indirect structure** (Buffer → Explanation → Bad News → Alternatives/Next Steps → Positive Close). The class compared two versions of a training-request rejection to see why the indirect version works better. ## 2. Topics Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap of Session 3: Essentials of Managerial Writing

**The 7 C's of effective writing**
- Treat them as a **checklist** to run your writing against, not as definitions to memorise.
- The C's named in class were clear, concrete, coherent, complete, correct, and *consideration*. Consideration came up again later as a key quality for bad-news messages.
- The standard 7 C's list, for reference, is clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, and courteous. Different textbooks swap in "consideration."

**Level 5 Leadership as a writing mindset**
- Core idea: *when you are not present in person, your writing demonstrates your leadership.*
- The concept was introduced through emails announcing layoff decisions written by two CEOs.
- It has two halves:

| Situation | Metaphor | What the leader does | Poor alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Things go **wrong** | **Look in the mirror** | Takes responsibility and owns accountability | Blaming the environment or situation, i.e., passing the blame (shows poor leadership) |
| Things go **well** | **Look out the window** | Credits the people, teams, and contributors who made it possible, using inclusive language | Taking credit yourself |

- Example used: **Tim Cook's email on Apple's 50th anniversary**, which credited people and used inclusive language. This is the "window" behaviour.

### 2.2 Recap of Session 4: Routine, Positive, or Neutral Messages

**Subject line guidelines**
- Short and specific.
- Positive or neutral.
- Related to the reader receiving it.
- Professional in tone.
- Clarification (in response to a student, Arya): short, specific subject lines are preferred for **all** emails, not just positive or neutral ones.

**Direct approach**
- Preferred for routine, positive, or neutral messages.
- It assumes the reader is **extremely busy**. You state the main point first, so the reader gets the gist even if they skip the rest.
- The underlying principle is respecting the reader's time when everyone receives a barrage of emails.

**Inverted pyramid**
1. Main point or main action (top)
2. Next most important details
3. Less important details (bottom)

Previous exercises included reorganising messages into an inverted pyramid, writing a full email from a provided structure, and turning a raw scenario into an inverted-pyramid email.

### 2.3 Introduction to Negative or Bad-News Messages

Class brainstorm on what to be careful about when delivering bad news:
- **Sensitivity**
- **Consideration** of the reader's perspective (one of the C's)
- **Empathy**
- An **indirect approach**: give background and explanation first, then the main point, then actions. The instructor confirmed this is right in **most, not all,** cases.
- **Tact** (discussed in detail below)

**Student example (Rahul): performance appraisal conversations**
- When an appraisal hasn't gone well, a good manager doesn't open with "your appraisal went badly, no increment this year."
- Instead, the manager first builds confidence ("you've done good work"), then explains the competitive context (people are rated relative to each other), then delivers the outcome.
- The instructor named this opening a **buffer**.

### 2.4 Tact

Student attempts at defining tact:
- The news shouldn't come as a shock, but should still convey the message correctly.
- Not hurting the reader's sentiments.
- The reader shouldn't be offended, even by negative news.
- Knowing your audience and how they're likely to respond, and framing the message accordingly.

**Rahul's PIP (Performance Improvement Plan) example, which the instructor said summarised tact well:**
- In parts of the IT industry, a fixed share of people (about 1%, per the student) *must* be placed on a PIP, regardless of how they actually performed.
- The organisation often doesn't want to lose these people.
- The first reaction to a PIP is resistance. Employees may challenge it, and HR gets involved.
- Tactful delivery expresses confidence and makes clear "we're not isolating you." It frames the PIP (for example, a 3-month rigorous training period) as an opportunity to grow, not as "you're down and out."

**Key idea:** with bad news, especially by email, "you can't just rip off the band-aid." You need a **structure**, and the indirect structure starts with a **buffer statement**.

**Focus of the lesson:** the decision has already been made and cannot change. The question is how to *deliver* it.

### 2.5 Worked Example: Message A vs. Message B (Rejecting a Training Request)

**Scenario:** An employee requested approval to attend a **week-long executive leadership program**. The request is being declined because of the training budget.

- **Message A (direct):** The bad news (request not approved) is in the **first sentence**. Reason given: *"the available training budget has already been allocated to employees whose roles require the program this year."*
- **Message B (indirect):** It opens with appreciation, e.g., *"Thank you for your interest in the week-long executive leadership program."* It then gives the reason, *"the available training budget has been prioritized for employees whose current roles require the program this year."* Only then does it state the refusal, and it ends on a positive, forward-looking note: not this cycle, but possibly in future.

**The class unanimously preferred B. The reasons discussed:**
- **Acknowledgement and consideration (Shaham):** B shows the application was actually reviewed with due diligence and wasn't rejected ad hoc.
- **The employee showed initiative:** the program goes beyond their usual workload and is an effort to upskill. You want them to keep applying in future and not feel disheartened.
- **Position of the bad news (Minakshi):** A puts it in the first sentence. B places it after the pleasantries and explanation, once the reader is comfortable.
- **Discouragement:** opening with "not approved" makes the reader think "maybe I wasn't qualified." They may not even finish reading. B gives hope for next year.
- **Psychological blockage (Ashra):** starting with negative information lowers acceptance and raises resistance, which can have long-term effects. B ends on a positive note.
- **Same reason, different reception:** both messages give essentially the same reason. What changes how it lands is the **wording** ("allocated" vs. "prioritized") and the **placement**.

**Counter-argument the instructor raised:** "Isn't direct better for a busy reader?" Not here, because the relationship takes priority. See 2.8 for when direct *is* better.

### 2.6 The Two Jobs of a Bad-News Message

1. **Communicate the decision** that has already been taken, with clarity, accuracy, and understanding.
2. **Manage and preserve the relationship**, especially with employees and customers. This usually requires **tactful language**.

**The central challenge:** *How do you communicate an unfavourable decision clearly without unnecessarily damaging the relationship?*

**Contrast with routine messages:**

| Message type | Priority |
|---|---|
| Routine, positive, or neutral | The **task**: passing on information and prioritising details |
| Negative or bad news | The **relationship**: the decision can't change, so the focus is how to present it |

### 2.7 Types of Bad-News Situations (non-exhaustive list)

Bad news is "bigger than saying no." Workplaces involve people with emotions, and their wages are their livelihood. Examples:
- Rejecting a request (e.g., a leave application)
- Declining a proposal
- Denying a promotion
- Announcing a policy change, which can be bad news in some cases
- Refusing a refund
- Cancelling a project
- Communicating poor performance or feedback
- Selecting another vendor

**Important principle:** *A negative decision does not automatically require a negative tone.* The hardest part, and the essence of tact, is conveying a negative decision in **professional and positive language**.

### 2.8 Framework: Direct vs. Indirect Approach for Bad News

**Direct approach (bad-news version):**
1. Bad news, stated up front in the first line
2. Reason or explanation
3. Next steps or alternatives
4. Close

**Indirect approach (used most often, when relationships are prioritised):**
1. **Buffer:** establishes context. It should be related to the news or situation at hand.
2. **Reason or explanation**
3. **Bad-news statement**
4. **Alternatives or next steps** (if any)
5. **Positive close**

The instructor recommended screenshotting this slide as a reference for drafting any bad-news message.

> **Neither approach is automatically better. Context determines the strategy.**

**When direct is appropriate: the time-sensitivity example**
- A student asked for an example, because direct seemed rude.
- The instructor modified the same training scenario. The employee must decide **today** whether to pay the registration fee personally before registration closes, and has had to follow up with the manager because no reply came. They also need to book travel and handle logistics.
- In this case the employee wants the answer up front, so **direct (Message A style) is preferred**.
- The approach changes because of the **time factor**. The workplace is complex, priorities shift, and managers sometimes must use the direct approach.
- The instructor asked the class to think of **other situations** where a direct approach suits bad news. This was left as an open question; the session only gave time sensitivity. Possible answers to consider include a reader who prefers or has asked for bluntness, or a minor or routine piece of bad news.

### 2.9 In-Class Activity: Can AI Write a Bad-News Message with Tact?

**Setup:** A scenario plus two email responses, one written by a human and one by AI. Students had to read the scenario carefully, decide which structure (direct or indirect) and details it called for, then identify the author of each email with reasons. The document was shared in the chat by Kesha, the class assistant.

**Scenario (reconstructed):** A company is introducing or expanding **employee monitoring** of IT systems, which affects employees' personal information. This is a sensitive policy announcement.

**Student arguments:**
- **Email 1 is human (Rhan):** it gives precise, specific information. Email 2 is basic and generic. He added that the AI result might depend on the prompt. The instructor countered that even with a lot of context in the prompt, AI may not produce the required tact and sensitivity.
- **Email 1 is AI (Raj):** it adds details not given in the scenario (new clients onboarded, a successful first phase, data deleted within 24 hours), and AI tends to add information on its own. Playing devil's advocate, the instructor asked whether those details are too specific for AI to invent.
- **Email 1 is AI (another student):** its action step (signing the policy) is clearly defined, as if generated from a list of prompted points. Email 2 is generic and leaves the action unstated.
- **Email 1 is human (another student):** its phrasing feels natural, e.g., "technology has an opportune way of racing ahead of you." Email 2 is extremely smooth and structured.
- **Email 2 is human (Rahul):** it contains the line "we value the trust you place in our IT systems," which seemed to require knowing the employees. The instructor corrected this: **AI is fully capable of generating such lines and can hallucinate or fabricate content.**
- **Clues for Email 1 being human:** a typo or error in Email 1 (AI is unlikely to make it), and the **em dash** in "high-profile," a telltale AI sign that people now often remove.
- A student linked Email 1 back to **"mirror"** accountability: an offer to contact the writer personally for clarification.

**Answer: Email 1 was written by a human (the instructor). Email 2 was written by AI.**

**Analysis of Email 1 (human), which follows the indirect structure:**
- **Buffer, related to the situation:** *"I'm very pleased to inform you that data breaches and phishing attacks have decreased after the implementation of the monitoring program last year."* This reassures readers that monitoring has already worked and frames the new step as a continuation.
- **Explanation:** high-profile clients were recently added to the portfolio, and the company is taking every step to prevent incidents. Only someone inside the organisation would know this.
- **Bad news:** the expanded monitoring, stated *after* the explanation.
- **Grievance mechanism:** a proper channel for raising concerns.
- **Concrete call to action (CTA):** **a declaration to be signed by all employees.** This confirms the policy reached every employee and records their agreement, which is necessary because personal information is being monitored.
- **Positive close**, in professional and positive language.

**Analysis of Email 2 (AI):**
- Dry, hollow, "wordy," and superficial. It "looks fine on paper."
- It merely announces the policy, with no sensitivity, tact, or empathy.
- Run-of-the-mill reasoning: it just cites company policy with no specific reason.
- **No real CTA.** "Should you have any questions or require further clarification…" is a **courtesy close**, which is conditional, not a concrete call to action.
- It gives no way to make sure every employee is aware of the policy or has agreed to it.

**Lessons:**
- A **call to action is a must** in such messages, and AI drafts tend to miss these context-specific details. Human intervention is needed.
- **Machine heuristic bias:** the tendency to treat AI output as perfect. Resist it.
- **The first draft should always be written by you.** It doesn't have to be perfect, but writing it yourself preserves the context and the necessary information.
- For routine messages, AI's shortcomings matter less. For sensitive messages involving people's personal lives, they matter a lot.

### 2.10 Practice Exercise: Outlining a Program-Discontinuation Email

**Task:** Read a detailed case (shared as a PDF). Sift out the relevant information and outline an email to all employees using the **indirect approach**. Students did not need to write the full email in class, only to decide what goes in each section: **Buffer → Explanation → Bad News → Exit (close)**. They can finish the full email at their own pace.

**Case gist (reconstructed):** The company has been funding employees' education, with around 20 employees benefiting. A client contract was lost, causing revenue loss, so the program is being discontinued. Some participants, possibly those currently enrolled in undergraduate study, may continue to receive support. One affected person in the case appears to be named Nikhil Arora; the name is unclear in the transcript.

**Student outline (praised by the instructor):**
- **Buffer:** how the program has been progressing and what it has been doing.
- **Explanation:** the lost contract and revenue issues have caused losses.
- **Bad news:** the program is being discontinued, with details such as who will continue to receive support.
- **Exit:** the company is looking at other ways to support employees, and this is temporary. *"When we are back on track and have sufficient profits again, the program will come back."* The instructor called this brilliant because it addresses low morale and offers hope.

**Q&A clarifications:**
- **Can you open with the explanation, e.g., "the company is facing losses"?**
  - No. Neither approach starts with the explanation. Direct starts with the bad news. Indirect starts with the buffer.
  - Opening with the explanation feels abrupt. The buffer establishes context first: what is this email about?
  - A lost client causing losses is a routine business matter, and a direct approach could suit an email about that alone. Cutting funding for employees' education is sensitive: people had hopes, were enrolled, and had planned their futures. Relationship preservation dominates, so use the indirect approach.
- **Can the buffer list the program's past benefits?**
  - Not in this case. When you are taking the benefits away, listing them in the buffer "adds salt to the injury."
  - The buffer should be relevant and neutral or positive, but should not highlight what the reader is about to lose.

## Routine, Positive & Neutral Business Messages

---

## 1. Session Overview

This session moves from the general writing-skills principles covered earlier (the 7 C's, Level 5 leadership and the "mirror/window" test) into the first of the message-type frameworks: **routine, positive and neutral business messages**, typically internal emails. The instructor covers what makes an effective **subject line** (short/specific, positive or neutral, reader-related, professional, optionally containing a request or action), contrasts the **indirect approach** (context → explanation → main point) with the **direct approach** (main point first), and introduces the **inverted pyramid** structure for organising routine messages. Four applied exercises are worked through in class: critiquing a badly-ordered meeting-reschedule email, re-sequencing a four-part insurance-meeting email into inverted-pyramid order, composing a full email from a given pyramid outline (department-head meeting), and composing an email from scratch out of a raw scenario (new online leave-management system). The session closes with a discussion of how AI (ChatGPT/Claude) defaults to verbose, writer-centred prose unless you prompt it with a framework — reinforcing that knowing the frameworks is what lets you use AI well — plus housekeeping on the in-class test, the elevator-pitch assignment deadline, and the upcoming group presentations. Next session: **negative / bad-news messages**.

## 2. Topics & Concepts, in Order Taught

### A. Recap of the previous session (writing-skills essentials)

- **The 7 C's of communication** (as recalled by the class): **Clear, Concrete, Coherent, Courteous, Concise, Correct, Complete**.
- **Two layoff-announcement emails compared** (both CEOs citing AI as a driver of layoffs):
  - The comparison was *not* "which writer is better" in general, but **who took responsibility vs. who blamed external factors**.
  - Email 1: attributed layoffs largely to external forces (AI) — failed to own the decision.
  - Email 2: used **personal pronouns** and explicitly took accountability — perceived as more human and more connected to employees.
  - Lesson: blaming external factors damages **credibility** and how you are **perceived as a leader**.
- **Level 5 Leadership — the mirror/window principle** (instructor's correction to a student's inverted version):
  - **When things go wrong → look in the MIRROR** (take accountability; own it; don't play the blame game).
  - **When things go right → look OUT THE WINDOW** (credit employees, teams, external contributors; don't "blow your own trumpet").
  - Boastful self-crediting ("I led the team, I made this happen") is not appreciated at the workplace.
  - **Your writing reflects which way you are looking.**
- **Case example: Tim Cook's memo on Apple's 50th anniversary** — thanked all employees for their hard work; a textbook case of "looking out the window" when things go well.

### B. Today's message type: routine / positive / neutral messages

- Focus: **internal communication** where the information is **routine, positive, or neutral** — information being passed from one level to another.
- Key premise: the reader **does not need to be prepared** for anything unpleasant, and the reader **has no time**.

### C. The subject line

**Why it matters:** the subject line decides *whether the email gets opened at all*, and answers the question **"What is this email about?"**

Class-generated effects of sending an email with **no subject line**:
- Signals the email is **not urgent / not important**.
- High chance of landing in the **spam folder**.
- Looks **unprofessional** / looks like it came from a random person, not an organisation.
- Conversely, a good subject line gives the reader a reason to **reply** ("this is important for me, I should respond").

**Five rules for subject lines:**

| Rule | Bad | Good |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Short and specific — use phrases, not full sentences** | "To find out employee career options" | "Employee career options" |
| 2. **Positive or neutral, never threatening** | "Warnings about abuse of company phones" | "Our company phone policy" |
| 3. **Related to the audience / reader-centric** | "Career options" (for whom?) | "Employee career options" |
| 4. **Professional tone, not casual** | "Smile, we see you" | "Security equipment installation" |
| 5. **May carry a request or an action** | — | "Urgent: problem needs your input"; "Review agenda before 3 p.m. meeting" |

- Tone also depends on **context and workplace culture**.
- Rationale: in a world of multitasking and back-to-back meetings, **conciseness is appreciated** — and the subject line is part of the email.

### D. Worked Example 1 — The badly-organised meeting-reschedule email

**The flawed email** (3 paragraphs): a vague subject line; paragraph 1 = background/origin of the problem; paragraph 2 = explanation; paragraph 3 = the actual announcement (the Friday 4 p.m. meeting is moving to Wednesday 3 p.m.); then an action item.

**Class critique:**
- Subject line gives no information → should be something like *"Team meeting moved to Wednesday 3 p.m."*
- **Verbose and redundant** — same point made repeatedly; could be one paragraph instead of three.
- The **third paragraph should be first** ("start with the conclusion, then give the reasoning").
- It is written **from the writer's perspective**, not the reader's — it traces the origin of the problem before saying what the reader must do.
- Somewhat unprofessional sign-off.

**Diagnosis:** the writer used the **indirect approach** — background → explanation → main point → action. Wrong framework for a routine message.

**The fix — the DIRECT APPROACH:**
1. **State the main point in the very first line(s).**
2. Then give the **details / explanation**.
3. Then **close with a call to action** (here: "update your calendar before Monday") or a positive note.

> **Key line from the lecture:** *"Directness is not bluntness — it is respect for the reader's time."*

**The test for a routine message:** *reading only the first paragraph (first 2–3 lines) should tell me what the email is about.* If I ignore the rest, I should still be fine.

**When to use the indirect approach instead:** sensitive issues / bad news — there you build context first, then explain, then deliver the news. (Framework changes with message type — covered next session.)

### E. The Inverted Pyramid (framework for routine / positive / neutral / goodwill messages)

$$\text{Most important information (the announcement / main point)}$$
$$\downarrow$$
$$\text{Explanation and key details}$$
$$\downarrow$$
$$\text{Less important information} \;/\; \text{call to action} \;/\; \text{background context}$$

- Top = the announcement (e.g., "the meeting has shifted from Friday to Wednesday").
- Middle = key details (where, when, what you must do).
- Bottom = less important information, contextual background ("where this originated"), and the closing CTA.
- Background/origin information is **deliberately placed last** — it may even be **omitted** entirely, depending on the audience, time-sensitivity and situation. Keep it only if readers genuinely need to know *why* ("Why suddenly a health insurance plan?").

### F. Worked Example 2 — Re-sequencing exercise (vision & dental insurance meetings)

The email contained four chunks of information, numbered as displayed:

1. "Please meet me in the first-floor conference room" (plus sign-up sheet instruction).
2. "A number of employees have asked about obtaining vision and dental insurance. Our company is happy to make this information available to you."
3. "I have scheduled short presentations…"
4. "These meetings are open to all employees."

**Student answers offered:** 3-1-4-2, 3-2-4-1, 3-1-2-4, 2-3-4-1, 2-3-1-4, 3-4-1-2.

**Correct order: 3 → 1 → 4 → 2.**

**Reasoning given by the instructor:**
- **3 first** — the announcement (presentations have been scheduled) is the main point; that is what the email *is*.
- **1 second** — meeting details: where to go, what to do (sign up). These are the key details supporting the announcement.
- **4 third** — "These meetings are open to all employees" refers to *which* meetings — so it must **follow** the meeting details; it is less important information.
- **2 last** — "a number of employees asked…" is **background/origin information**, which in a direct approach belongs at the bottom (and could be dropped).
- Note the instructor's framing: *"The idea is not to have a sequence of information but to prioritise which information is the most important."*

**Student question addressed:** "Isn't the call to action normally at the *end* of an email?" — Answer: in this message type the **hierarchy of importance overrides the convention**. "Please meet me in the conference room" is a key detail, not background; demoting it to the end would misclassify it. You may **reiterate** the sign-up instruction at the end if you wish, but the venue/action detail stays high in the pyramid.

### G. Worked Example 3 — Composing an email from a given pyramid (department-head meeting)

**Given structure (5–6 minutes, written in chat):**
- **Most important:** announce a Fair department-head meeting, **9:00 a.m. Monday**, to discuss competing with the new **Maxi-Mart** that has opened in the area.
- **Less important:** areas to consider during the meeting — **customer service, stock/talk availability, community outreach, other areas**.
- **Close:** a **motivational call to action**.

**Feedback given on student submissions (i.e., the evaluation points):**
- **Drop the company name "Fair" from the subject line and the opening line** — this is *internal* communication, so naming your own company is **redundant**. "Department head meeting" is enough.
- **Include the venue** — conference room / Zoom. Omitting it makes the message **incomplete** (violates the "complete" C).
- **Do not elaborate the agenda bullets** (e.g., "customer service — identifying ways to improve customer experience and strengthen customer relationships"). You are only *informing* people which areas will be considered so they can come prepared; details belong in the meeting. Keep it concise.
- **Subject lines must not be long** — one student's was flagged as too long; abbreviate where sensible.
- **The closing CTA must be motivational, not instructional.** "Please attend our upcoming meeting…" reads as a direction/instruction. Better: make it welcoming and collective — *"Your ideas and input will be valuable as we consider how the company can strengthen its position."*
- **Model opening praised in class:** *"We will be holding a meeting on Monday to discuss how [we] can compete with the new Maxi-Mart."* — short, crisp, neutral, non-threatening, no instruction.
- Praised submissions included phrasing like "We encourage you to come up with…" — encouragement over command.
- **Class participation was recorded** for everyone who posted a response in the chat.

### H. Worked Example 4 — Composing an email from a raw scenario (online leave-management system)

**Scenario elements (students had to decide the hierarchy themselves, 5 minutes):**
- From **1 November**, all leave requests go through a **new online leave-management system**.
- Employees must **activate/register their account by 30 October**.
- A **Zoom demo/training session** will be held; the **link will be shared in a follow-up email**.
- Benefits: employees can **check leave balance** and **track approval status**.
- Background: **HR expects the system to reduce delays**.

**Strong structures presented by students and endorsed:**
- Subject line carrying the **action + deadline** (e.g., "Action required: activate leave account by Oct 30 — new system from Nov 1"), with **months abbreviated (Oct, Nov)** to keep the subject short.
- Para 1: the announcement — from 1 November all leave requests will be through the online system.
- Para 2: the call to action (**bolded** so it isn't missed) — activate your account by 30 October.
- Para 3: supporting/transition help — Zoom demo details; link to follow in a separate email.
- Para 4: benefits (leave balance, approval tracking) and **background** (HR expects reduced delays), plus an invitation to reach out with questions.
- Close on a **positive, encouraging note**.

**Instructor's overall appraisal:** most of the class correctly applied the inverted pyramid and wrote correct subject lines. Particularly appreciated: putting the **30 October deadline into the subject line**, which creates **urgency** and improves the chance the email is opened promptly (time-sensitivity).

### I. Using AI for routine messages

- Question posed: if you paste this scenario into ChatGPT/Claude **without** specifying a structure, what do you get?
- Class answer: it "writes a whole story", explaining every point in unnecessary detail.
- Diagnosis: default AI output behaves like the **flawed first email** — it starts from the **writer's perspective** and **takes too long to reach the main point**; it is **not concise**.
- Conclusion drawn:
  - **Do not rely blindly on AI**; don't ship its first draft.
  - AI is fine for a **first draft** — *if* you know which framework to specify. Prompt it explicitly: *"compose this email using an inverted pyramid structure."*
  - **Only when you know the frameworks can you leverage AI properly** — otherwise you settle for whatever it gives you, a "run-of-the-mill message."
  - **Accountability:** your name goes under the message, so what you do with AI reflects on you.

## 3. Frameworks & Rubrics — Quick Reference

- **7 C's:** Clear, Concrete, Coherent, Courteous, Concise, Correct, Complete.
- **Level 5 Leadership:** mirror when things go wrong; window when things go right.
- **Audience analysis / "payback"(PAIBOC-type) model** referenced: routine messages must be built around the **audience, not the writer**.
- **Direct approach (routine/positive/neutral):** Main point → details/explanation → less important info / background → CTA or positive close.
- **Indirect approach (negative/sensitive):** Context/buffer → explanation → main point/bad news.
- **Inverted pyramid:** most important → key details → least important / background / CTA.
- **Subject-line checklist:** short & specific (phrase) • positive/neutral, non-threatening • reader-related • professional tone • may state request/action; deadlines can be included to create urgency; avoid naming your own company in internal mail; abbreviate to keep it short.
- **The first-paragraph test:** if the first 2–3 lines don't tell the reader what the email is about, the structure is wrong.

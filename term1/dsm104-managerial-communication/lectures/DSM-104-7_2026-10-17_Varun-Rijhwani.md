# DSM-104 Managerial Communication: Session 7 Study Notes
**Session file:** DSM-104-7_2026-10-17_Varun-Rijhwani
**Topic:** Structuring a Planned Business Presentation (Preview, View, Review) and Slide Design Basics

---

## 1. Overview

This session covered how to **structure** a planned business presentation, such as one delivered to senior management. The instructor stated at the outset that this session is about structure and that the next session (Session 8) will cover **content**, including ethos, pathos and logos. A memorable presentation rests on three things: well-developed key takeaway messages, supporting evidence for every claim, and a clear structure. The core framework is **Preview → View → Review**, which comes with approximate time allocations and named components:

- **Preview:** attention getter, positioning statement, overview.
- **View:** two to four key messages, each developed with the **PREP method**.
- **Review:** recap, reinforce, call to action.

A running example about helping small businesses move manufacturing to China illustrated each component. The session then turned to slide design. A clip from the "How to avoid death by PowerPoint" talk demonstrated the **principle of contrast**, and the instructor added the "six-line" slide rule and advice on using cue cards. It closed with the group presentation guidelines (speaking time, transitions, Q&A etiquette) and student questions.

---

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Three pillars of a strong, memorable presentation
1. **Well-developed key takeaway messages.** The audience should never be confused about the point. Aim for one to three clear messages and design the whole presentation around them.
2. **Supporting information.** Every claim must be backed by sources that are relevant and recent where the topic requires it. This links to next session's ethos, pathos and logos.
3. **Clear structure.** This is the focus of today's session.

**Principle: content first, delivery second.** "Content is king." Develop the content first, then decide who delivers what and how.

### 2.2 Identifying key takeaway messages: three guiding questions
1. What do you want the audience to **remember**?
2. **Why does it matter** to the audience?
3. What should **everything else support**?

Busy audiences, such as senior management who have given you 10 to 15 minutes, need the key takeaways **stated early and re-emphasised throughout**.

> **Scope note:** This framework is for **planned** presentations, which is also what the assignment requires. Spontaneous or impromptu speaking uses different techniques that are outside this lecture's scope.

### 2.3 The core framework: Preview, View, Review

| Section | Purpose | Components | Approximate share of time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preview** | Set expectations and flow, create interest and enthusiasm, show relevance | Attention getter, positioning statement, overview | $10\text{%}$ to $15\text{%}$ |
| **View** | The "meat" or crux: develop and support the key messages | Two to four key messages, each built with PREP | $85\text{%}$ to $90\text{%}$ (as stated) |
| **Review** | Recap, reinforce and drive action | Recap, reinforce, call to action | Remainder |

- The proportions are **approximate**, but the **principle is not**: every planned presentation must have all three sections.
- *Note for your own notes:* the stated figures add to roughly $100\text{%}$ before the Review is counted ($15\text{%} + 85\text{%} = 100\text{%}$). In practice, the Review needs a short slice of time, so treat the View as "the large majority" of the talk.
- **Illustrative calculation (not given in class):** for a 15-minute talk, a Preview of $10\text{%}$ to $15\text{%}$ is $0.10 \times 15 = 1.5$ to $0.15 \times 15 = 2.25$ minutes.

### 2.4 Primacy and recency effects
- Audiences best remember **how a presentation opens (primacy)** and **how it closes (recency)**.
- Both the opening and the closing must therefore be powerful.
- **Practical implication for group work:** know your group members' strengths and weaknesses in advance. Choose your strongest speakers to **open** and **close**.

### 2.5 Preview section in detail

In the first two minutes, the audience decides:
- whether to keep listening;
- whether the talk will benefit them;
- whether it is valuable enough to take away or act on.

A strong preview has three parts.

#### (a) Attention getter

**Traps to avoid:**
1. **Opening with a joke.** Humour is a double-edged sword, especially given today's "cancel culture" and sensitivity. Use it only if you know the audience well (see audience analysis from Session 1) and the joke is relevant to your topic.
2. **Data dumping.** Many statistics at the start cause information overload and the audience switches off. Use at most **one** striking statistic.

**Effective openers discussed in class (several were student contributions):**

| Opener | Example or note |
|---|---|
| **Shocking statistic** (just one) | "35% of people are switching to vegan products right now." |
| **Rhetorical question** | A question that needs no answer. The instructor distinguished *rhetoric* from a *rhetorical question*. Student example: "Would you rather have a million dollars or…?" |
| **Image or contrasting pictures** | Student Rahul described an economics professor who opened a lecture on the Great Depression with no words. He showed a photo of a fun-loving 1930s American family, then switched to people queuing at a food stall. **Lesson:** a visual contrast conveys the intensity of the topic and hooks attention. |
| **Celebrity reference** | Linking a topic such as diabetes, health or sportsmanship to a celebrity (e.g., a cricketer or actress) works because celebrities have mass appeal. |
| **Origin story** | Tell the story of the product or of yourself, then drive it toward the objective. People love stories, but keep the story **relevant and brief**, not a "saga". |
| **Imagined or hypothetical scenario** | "Imagine…" draws the audience into the situation. |

Choose the opener that best fits your topic.

#### (b) Positioning statement
- **Definition:** a statement that positions or "plants" your topic in the audience's minds. It tells them what the presentation will do and what to look out for.
- **Example (China case):**
  > "Today I'm going to discuss how small businesses like yours can easily move some or all of your manufacturing to China to lower production costs and meet other manufacturing goals. In fact, I'm going to explain how you could have a manufacturing presence in China in as little as 1 month."
- Possible attention getters for this topic include an anecdote about overseas manufacturing, a statistic about it, or a related rhetorical question.
- **Meta-example:** the instructor's own opening line ("today we'll talk about structure; next class, content") was itself a positioning statement.

#### (c) Overview statement
- Breaks the positioning statement into its **segments**, effectively previewing the key takeaways.
- Use **simple, conversational language** and keep it to one to three sentences.
- Use plenty of **"you" language** to create an instant, direct connection, so the talk does not sound generic.
- **Example (China case):**
  > "I'm going to focus on three primary benefits of sourcing and manufacturing in China. The first and most compelling reason is that **you** can reduce production cost by **40–60%**. The second reason is that **you** can maintain and even increase the quality of your products. The final reason is that **you** can cut time to market by another **1–3 months**."

### 2.6 View section in detail
- Develop **two to four key takeaway messages**, with **four at most**.
- Assume the audience may be **skeptical**, so each position needs support.
- **Types of evidence:**
  - cases
  - examples
  - stories
  - illustrations
  - analogies
  - statistics
  - facts
  - quotations
  - personal experience
- **Use support in moderation.** "The problem today is not information, it's information overload." Choose evidence that is strong and relevant rather than plentiful.

#### The PREP method (for developing each key message)

| Letter | Step | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | Position or Point | State your main conclusion or central stance. |
| **R** | Reason | Explain why you hold this position. |
| **E** | Example | Give evidence: a case, story, fact or statistic. |
| **P** | Position or Point (again) | Restate and reinforce your point. |

The structure can be summarised as $\text{PREP} = \text{Point} \rightarrow \text{Reason} \rightarrow \text{Example} \rightarrow \text{Point}$.

**Worked example (shared as a Word document in class, China case):**
1. **Position:** "It's challenging for most small businesses to set up manufacturing in China."
2. **Reason:** There are major barriers to entry:
   - finding and building relationships with Chinese suppliers;
   - having engineers on the ground;
   - learning to deal with a different business culture.
3. **Example:** "Let me give you a quick example of clients we've worked with to successfully set up manufacturing in the past year. Their initial efforts were costly and disappointing." Describe the specific challenges they faced and how much they spent, with **real statistics** so the example is credible and not fabricated.
4. **Position restated:** "Without access to relationships with Chinese manufacturers and suppliers, and an on-the-ground presence to monitor operations, small businesses can find it very challenging and costly to set up production in China."

**Follow-on:** after restating the problem, introduce your **USP** (unique selling proposition). You have the contacts and presence to solve exactly these problems. The evidence shows the difficulty, and your offer resolves it.

**Repeat PREP for each key message** in the View section.

### 2.7 Review section in detail
Do not end with just "thank you for your time". Finish with a **clear direction** for the audience, built from three parts:

1. **Recap:** summarise the key messages in a few sentences.
2. **Reinforce:** return the audience to the single most important takeaway, which the group decides on.
3. **Call to action (CTA):** ask for a decision, a commitment or a next step.

**Worked example (China case):**
> "Thank you for attending this seminar. I want to quickly mention how you can get a more specific understanding of the savings you can achieve by producing in China. In your packets, I have enclosed a free estimate form. If you can provide some basic information about one of your products, we can have cost estimates and shipping details back to you within a week. The estimate is free; there's no obligation."

**Class analysis of the example:**
- Sentence 1 expresses **gratitude**.
- Sentence 2 is the **recap** (understanding the savings).
- The free estimate form **combines reinforcement and the CTA**. It also reinforces the message *later*, with real numbers and data.

**Lessons on the call to action:**
- A CTA must be **highly specific and concrete**. A vague "feel free to reach out to us" is "building castles in the air".
- **Without a CTA, the presentation is incomplete.**
- The CTA can be anything. For example, when seeking approval from senior management, the **permission itself is the CTA**. How you articulate it matters most.

### 2.8 Avoiding "Death by PowerPoint": slide design
**Video:** a talk titled "How to avoid death by PowerPoint" (shown in class as a talk clip; link shared in chat). Only **point #4, Contrast**, was shown. Watch the rest yourself while designing your slides.

**Summary of the contrast clip:**
- **"Contrast controls your focus."**
- **Lists:** if a full list appears at once, the audience's eyes wander. Instead, reveal one item at a time and dim the others. The audience then follows the highlighted item "like a kitten chasing a laser pointer".
- **Large tables:** apply the same principle and highlight only the relevant cell or row. A cluttered table then becomes easy to follow.
- **White backgrounds:** most companies use white slide backgrounds, which makes the screen the brightest, highest-contrast and biggest object, so it competes with the speaker. The speaker recommends **dark backgrounds**, so the audience's eyes relax and focus on the presenter.

**Key lesson:** **the speaker is the presentation; the slides are only a visual aid.**

**Additional design rules from the instructor:**
- **Never memorise** your presentation word for word.
- **Avoid text-heavy slides.** The instructor called this the "t-shirt rule": **no more than six lines per slide**, with no clutter.
- Keep slides **clean and minimal**, and do not overload them with pictures or text.
- The instructor's own teaching slides are text-heavy because the purpose is teaching. Formal business presentations have a different aim, so their design must also differ.
- Slides should be **visually appealing, not a manuscript**. Experiment with how to present information visually.

### 2.9 Cue cards (from student Q&A)
- **Question (Rahul):** what if technical content needs more than six lines, and stage fright makes you forget the rest?
- **Answer:** use **cue cards**, which are small index cards with pointers for the next points to cover. Experienced speakers carry and refer to them, and this is completely natural.
- **Question (Subhat):** isn't the PPT itself a cue card?
- **Answer:** not really. It is a visual aid, so you cannot dump everything onto it. Cue cards hold the extra detail that does not belong on a slide.

### 2.10 Delivery and etiquette for the group presentation
- **Transitions:** every speaker except the last must hand over smoothly, never abruptly.
  - *Example:* "To understand the financial implications of this business decision, Keshav will walk us through the details."
- **Closing:** express gratitude, then **open the floor to questions**.
- **Q&A protocol:**
  - Before answering, announce: "I'll be happy to answer that question."
  - If several members want to answer, agree on who answers. Online, that person unmutes and answers; others do not blurt out responses.
  - Start by thanking the asker: "Thank you for that question. That's a very interesting observation." Then answer.
- **Questions you cannot answer (Rahul's question):**
  - Acknowledge honestly that the point was outside the scope of the presentation.
  - Offer to look into it and **take it offline**, for example in a one-to-one discussion afterwards.
- **Online delivery reminders** (carried over from earlier non-verbal communication sessions):
  - Use voice modulation.
  - Use gestures within the visible frame and facial expressions.
  - Look into the camera to keep eye contact.
  - Converse with the audience rather than reading from a script.

### 2.11 Other points from student Q&A
- **Feedback on the video self-pitch assignment:** quick feedback is not possible because evaluation takes time and some submissions were late. Approach the presentation independently and ask questions if needed.
- **Model script for the self-pitch:** none will be provided. The pitch is subjective and personal; pitching yourself to Sundar Pichai, for example, would need a completely different script. "Communication has no one-size-fits-all." The course teaches frameworks and mindsets, not templates.

## 3. Exam and Assignment Notes

**Group presentation guidelines** (a detailed document will be emailed by Keshav after class):
- **Minimum speaking time per member: 2 minutes.** Speaking for less than 2 minutes affects that member's evaluation.
- **Maximum per speaker: 2.5 minutes.**
- **Strict total time limit.** For a 15-minute group slot (used as an example), anything spoken **after the limit is not considered**.
- **Total time depends on group size,** so five- and six-member groups get more time. The guidelines will give the details.
- **Topic selection is first come, first served, and topics cannot be repeated.** There is a deadline, so choose early.
- **No postponements.** The schedule (presentations around **30 October**) is fixed. A student working offshore was told it cannot be changed.
- The presentation must be **planned** and follow **Preview → View → Review**, including a **call to action**.
- **Rehearse as a group,** especially the transitions.
- Follow the slide design principles: contrast, a maximum of six lines per slide, clean and minimal slides, and the speaker as the presentation.
- Watch the full **"Death by PowerPoint"** video while designing your slides.
- Follow Q&A etiquette: thank the asker, announce who will answer, and take unanswerable questions offline.
- **Next session (8)** covers presentation **content**, including **ethos, pathos and logos**.
- The instructor's closing advice: treat this as a safe space to experiment, because learning matters more than marks.

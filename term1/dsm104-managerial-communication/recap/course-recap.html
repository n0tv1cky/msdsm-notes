Audience first: the five prerequisites and PAIBOC

Before any email, speech or pitch, clear the five prerequisites, then walk PAIBOC in order, because communication is about the audience and not the speaker.

FIVE PREREQUISITES: CLARIFY BEFORE YOU SPEAK (S01)

  • Purpose: why am I communicating?
  • Audience awareness: who am I speaking to, or writing this email to? This is the most important of the five.
  • Clarity: a thought that isn't clear in your head can't be articulated, even in writing.
  • Timing: when you do it.
  • Credibility = Competence + Character + Consideration. It is the slowest of the five to build and the fastest to be spent.

PAIBOC: SEQUENTIAL, EACH STEP NEEDS THE ONE BEFORE

P · Purpose
One aim, crystal clear. Keep the end goal in mind and work backwards ("presenting to senior management for approval" means the deck writes itself toward that goal).
A · Audience
Demographic: age, education, occupation, income. Psychographic: needs, values, lifestyles, attitudes, fears. S02 adds knowledge level, interests, expectations and language preferences.
I · Information
The content is set entirely by step A, so personalise it to the receiver. Include market and competitor intelligence too.
B · Benefits
Turn features into benefits: "let the audience see what is in store for them". The lever to choose here is carrot vs stick.
O · Objections
Anticipate them in advance by stepping into the audience's shoes. The O is not "objectives".
C · Context
Communication never happens in isolation. Consider the audience's sentiments, timing, the environment (political scenario, organisational climate) and whether it is a crisis or normal communication.

CARROT VS STICK

  • Carrot: an incentive or reward for the desired behaviour (positive motivation).
  • Stick: a penalty for the wrong behaviour, e.g. the ₹500 fine.
  • Choose by situation and time sensitivity. Panchayat used only the stick and offered no carrot.

ETHOS · PATHOS · LOGOS (PREVIEW)

  • Pathos: the audience is moved by emotion, so use emotional storytelling (Bittoo on the father).
  • Logos: the audience is moved by reason, numbers and statistics, so use data and evidence.
  • Ethos: the speaker's credibility (competence, character, consideration).
  • Diagnose whether the audience is pathos- or logos-inclined and tweak the message to match. That diagnosis is itself audience analysis. Full treatment comes in the persuasion session.

SMALL RULES

  • The C's: consideration of the receiver, their culture and the language used. One company-wide email is read differently across backgrounds, education and regions.
  • Slides: no more than 6 lines per slide. Overloaded slides pull attention away from the speaker.
  • No one-size-fits-all: pitch the content at the audience's level.
  • Surprise test (S04): a Google Form in roughly the first 10 min of any of S05–S08, with MCQs and one-word or one-sentence answers. It covers everything taught so far and has no retest.

Non-verbal communication: the body and the voice

You start communicating before you open your mouth. This module covers the 7–38–55 split, kinesics, proxemics and paralanguage, all decoded from one boardroom scene.

7–38–55 (ORAL COMMUNICATION)

7% · words
the verbal content: what you actually say
38% · vocal / paralanguage
how you say it: pitch, volume, pace, pause, stress
55% · body language
posture, gestures, eye contact, facial expression
sync rule
7 + 38 + 55 = 100. Body and voice must be in sync with the words and never work against them. Strong content with poor delivery does not land.

KINESICS: POSTURE, GESTURES, FACE

posture
upright and straight signals confidence. Posture also covers where you stand: podium vs centre stage. Appearance goes with it.
hands limp / clasped behind back
the words are left carrying only the 7%
zone of influence
belly button → chin (instructor); the video said belly button → eyes. Think of an invisible box in front of you. Control two things: gestures stay in the zone, and they keep a reasonable amplitude.
leveler
palms down, hands cross and open downward / outward. Use it to decline, say no, or make a firm point.
placator
palms open and up, arms open. Use it to welcome ("Good morning, everyone").
facial expression
must be congruent with the content: fierce for the vision, soft for a loss

PARALANGUAGE = EVERYTHING ABOUT THE VOICE EXCEPT THE WORDS

pace
if you rush, nothing is absorbed. Go fast over what the audience already knows and slow down on what is new.
pitch
vary it; a monotone kills the message. Pitch rises for a challenge and drops for grief.
pause
pause after a strong, impactful or emotional point. The routine: slow the pace, make the point, then pause.
volume
match it to the size of the room
word stress
stress one word and the sentence means something else (try the widget below)

EYE CONTACT: PERIODIC + DISTRIBUTIVE

  • Split the room into left / centre / right.
  • Hold 3 s on one person, then on another in the same section. Shift gradually to the centre, then to the right.
  • Never more than 3 s on one person: longer reads as staring and intimidates.
  • The aim is to give every section equal importance. It has to be a conscious, effortful act. If you stare at notes, the floor or blank space, engagement drops.

CONGRUENCE PRINCIPLE

congruent
words, voice and body agree, so the message lands
conflict
receivers turn to the non-verbal cues to read your true attitude. Your words will not be trusted; your body will be.
"the body leaks the message"
it reveals intentions even when you are trying to say nothing
context
no single body-language act has one fixed meaning; it depends on the situation
course goal
turn subconscious body language into conscious, practised effort

ONLINE / ZOOM: GO THE EXTRA MILE

  • Keep the camera on throughout.
  • Look into the lens. On a call, that is your eye contact.
  • Framing: the audience must see you clearly.
  • Let your hands move naturally within the frame.
  • Upright posture, no slouching.
  • While others speak, hold a listening posture (hands in a set position) and nod.
  • Use facial expression and vocal variety on purpose.

Managerial writing: 7 C's, AI, and Level 5 leadership

A grammatically perfect email can still make you sound evasive. The 7 C's catch the drafting faults, and ownership on the page is what makes readers trust you.

WRITING REVEALS LEADERSHIP

correct ≠ good
A grammatically correct email can still sound defensive, vague or evasive.
what writing signals
Beyond informing or persuading, it signals judgement · emotional maturity · accountability · credibility.
internal = external
Internal communication matters as much as external.
why learn it when AI exists
Someone must review AI output, and writing yourself builds the rubric in your head for that review. Confidential material pasted into AI tools can feed model training, so write sensitive documents yourself.

7 C'S: A DRAFTING CHECKLIST, NOT A DEFINITION

Clear
Understood on the first reading. Attention spans are dropping, so nobody re-reads to decode you.
Concrete
Named actions, named owners, actual numbers, timelines.
Coherent
context → what happened → call to action
Courteous
Respectful and humane: a person, not a "tag of employees who will do the work". This applies even downwards.
Concise
No needless detail. Use short sentences, because a long one loses the reader by its end.
Correct
Grammar and facts right, and appropriate to the recipient.
Complete
Gives the reader enough to act. Gaps cause costly back-and-forth.

MINDFUL AI USE

  • Never outsource the whole task. Don't say "write an email for me". Draft it yourself or feed it your own points, then let AI polish. You hold the context.
  • Hallucination: fabricated facts destroy credibility. Feeding your own points reduces it.
  • Watermarks (Claude cited): heavy unacknowledged use is detectable and signals you didn't even proofread.
  • Machine-heuristic bias: assuming machine output must be correct. Few grammar errors ≠ accurate or appropriate.
  • Critical-thinking erosion.
  • AI slop: every email has the same structure and sentences, so none of them land. Distinctive writing differentiates you.
  • Always proofread. Low-risk uses are fine, e.g. a grammar check on a leave email.

COURTESY, CONTEXT, CULTURE

"Hope you're doing well"
This is Courtesy. Use it for clients, external stakeholders, or after a long gap. Drop it in routine internal mail.
semantics
Word choice can still shift meaning, hence appropriate to the recipient under Correct.
high- vs low-context
India is high-context: things are not said directly. Leaders switch between the two registers. The two layoff emails were written for different cultural audiences. This returns with negative messages.
PAIBOC's C = context
S03 pointed back to the S1 model: audience and context are always factored in.

AI has no mind of its own (S03):

  • The SAP client: in a client-facing SAP implementation, a client kept quoting ChatGPT "solutions" that were technically impossible. It derailed the work until it had to be escalated to seniors. AI scrapes the web: wrong input gives wrong output, and "AI has no mind of its own to verify it."
  • AI is fine for a plain letter to a friend. It fails where nuance, emotional register and technical expertise are needed.
  • The professor's push-back: for trivial tasks you can outsource, but do it often and you outsource thinking itself.

The bad 3-line email, diagnosed and not rewritten (S03): "several teams did not escalate concerns in time… some requirements were not met… please review what went wrong in your respective areas and ensure it does not happen again."

  • No owner, no action, no deadline, so nobody can act on it.
  • The sender takes no responsibility. It is accusatory, puts the burden of finding the fault on the reader, and goes to everyone instead of the responsible team.
  • It could have been constructive: "this happened, here's how we fix it".

Can an email pass all 7 C's and still fail? Yes (S03):

  • Word choice still sets the tone. Clarity and concision only preserve meaning.
  • Over-concision fails a client who needs the cause explained from the basics, because big words leave them lost.
  • The emotional connect may not come through.
  • Above all, missing ownership. Public CEO layoff memos (e.g. Microsoft's) explain the conditions in detail, but nobody takes the blame. And when things go well, do you blow your own trumpet?

Dorsey and the empathy question (S03):

  • "I'd rather feel awkward and human than efficient and cold."
  • Empathy or ownership, must you choose? No: "you can have the best of both worlds." Add a line of empathy and still take Dorsey-style responsibility. Good writing on sensitive issues does both.
  • Coming later: persuasion (ethos, logos, pathos) and how much emotion vs logic an email needs.

Routine, positive and neutral messages: direct approach and the inverted pyramid

When the news is routine, the reader needs no preparation and has no time, so the main point goes first and everything else is ranked below it by importance.

PREMISE

the reader
needs no preparation (nothing unpleasant is coming) and has no time: multitasking, back-to-back meetings
scope
mostly internal email; routine, positive or neutral information passed from one level to another
PAIBOC
build the message around the audience, not the writer
first-paragraph test
the first 2–3 lines must tell the reader what the email is about. If they fail, the structure is wrong.

DIRECT vs INDIRECT

direct (routine / positive / neutral)
main point → details / explanation → CTA or positive close
indirect (S5 preview: bad news)
context → explanation → main point. Right for sensitive news. In a routine message it turns into the writer's chronology, as in the reschedule email.
directness ≠ bluntness
directness is respect for the reader's time

INVERTED PYRAMID

  • Top: most important, the announcement ("the meeting has shifted from Friday to Wednesday")
  • Middle: key details: where, when, what you must do
  • Bottom: less important info, CTA, background ("where this originated")
  • Background goes last and may be omitted. Keep it only if readers need the why ("Why suddenly a health insurance plan?").
  • Prioritise, don't sequence.

SUBJECT LINE: WHY IT MATTERS, AND THE EXTRAS

  • Decides whether the email is opened. Answers "What is this email about?"
  • No subject line: looks unimportant or not urgent, likely ends up in spam, looks unprofessional (like a random person, not an organisation)
  • A good one gives the reader a reason to reply
  • A deadline in the subject creates urgency (the Oct 30 example)
  • Internal mail: don't name your own company ("Fair department head meeting" → "Department head meeting")
  • Abbreviate to stay short: Oct, Nov
  • Tone also depends on context and workplace culture

AI AND FRAMEWORKS

  • Unprompted, ChatGPT/Claude "writes a whole story": writer's perspective, slow to the point, not concise. It reproduces the flawed reschedule email.
  • It is fine for a first draft if you name the framework: "compose this email using an inverted pyramid structure"
  • Don't ship the first draft, or you settle for a "run-of-the-mill message"
  • "Only when you know the frameworks can you leverage AI properly."
  • Your name goes under the message, so the accountability is yours

Bad-news messages: tact, buffer, direct vs indirect

The decision is already made and will not change, so the skill is in how you deliver it. The reader should understand it clearly and still trust you afterwards.

THE TWO JOBS

  • 1 · Communicate the decision. It is already taken and can't change. Deliver it with clarity, accuracy and understanding.
  • 2 · Preserve the relationship with employees and customers. This usually needs tactful language.
  • The central challenge: state an unfavourable decision clearly without unnecessarily damaging the relationship.
  • You can't just rip off the band-aid. You need a structure.

TWO STRUCTURES (S05 SLIDE — SCREENSHOT IT)

indirect · 5 parts · most cases
Buffer → Reason / Explanation → Bad-news statement → Alternatives / Next steps (if any) → Positive close
direct · 4 parts
Bad news (first line) → Reason → Next steps / Alternatives → Close
S06 recap, short form
Indirect: Buffer → Explanation → Bad news → Exit. Direct: Bad news → Explanation → Exit.
  • Neither starts with the explanation.
  • "Neither approach is automatically better. Context determines the strategy."

TYPES OF BAD NEWS (NON-EXHAUSTIVE)

  • Rejecting a request (e.g. leave)
  • Declining a proposal
  • Denying a promotion
  • Policy change (in some cases)
  • Refusing a refund
  • Cancelling a project
  • Poor performance / feedback
  • Selecting another vendor
  • Bigger than saying no: people have emotions, and their wages are their livelihood.

TACT AND THE BUFFER

tact
Negative news in professional, positive language. "A negative decision does not automatically require a negative tone." This is the hardest part.
Class definitions: the news shouldn't come as a shock but must still land; the reader shouldn't be offended; know how your audience is likely to respond and frame the message for that.
good buffer
Related to the situation · neutral or positive · establishes context ("what is this email about?")
bad buffer
One that lists what the reader is about to lose. It "adds salt to the injury."

THE CLOSE AND THE FIRST DRAFT

concrete CTA
A specific action every reader must take. Example: a declaration signed by all employees, which confirms the message reached everyone and records their agreement.
courtesy close
"Should you have any questions or require further clarification…" This is conditional, so it is not a CTA.
write the first draft yourself
It doesn't need to be perfect. Writing it yourself keeps the context and the necessary information in the message.
machine heuristic bias
The tendency to treat AI output as perfect. AI drafts miss context-specific details such as the CTA. That matters little for routine messages and a lot when people's personal lives are involved.

Meetings: participating, Six Thinking Hats, and the Abilene paradox

A meeting is for the people in the room: don't waste their time, say your point so they can act on it, and voice the hat or the disagreement that nobody else is voicing.

WHAT WASTES A MEETING (CLASS LIST)

no agenda / topic
Nobody knows what the meeting is for.
context-giving
Can take up to "90% of the time" (a student's estimate). Most of it could go by text or email.
unprepared people
They arrive and only then form opinions. Points should be circulated beforehand.
unnecessary attendees
Whole teams are invited when most have no role in the item.
repeating to look involved
Example: a manager repeats the same remark just to show participation.
digressing
The discussion drifts off the agenda.

FIXES FROM THE CLASS DISCUSSION

scrum stand-up
Strictly ~15 min. Team: Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers. The Scrum Master facilitates, notes points and moves things on. Technical detail goes to a separate meeting.
host decides first
The host sets the topic and the decision to be made before the meeting, so attendees come prepared.
equal participation
Don't let one person dominate.
timeboxed agenda
Split the time across subtopics. Teams can notify a speaker who runs over.
24-hour pre-meeting message
Sent 24 hours ahead with 3 items: what decision, why it's needed, any other context.

FIVE ELEMENTS OF EFFECTIVE PARTICIPATION

1 · Prepare
Know the agenda and what information or questions to bring. A 15-min scrum needs very specific inputs.
2 · Contribute
Make a relevant point and give your reasoning (Point → Reason → Next step).
3 · Listen
"Paramount." Build, don't repeat. Rephrasing checks understanding; repeating just parrots and drags the meeting on.
4 · Tone
Raise concerns respectfully. There is a professional way to disagree.
5 · Follow through
Close with a quick review: who does what, by when.

POINT → REASON → NEXT STEP (SLIDE EXAMPLE)

Point: suggestion first
"I suggest a smaller pilot."
Reason: why
"We can identify problems before committing to the full budget."
Next step: ask for buy-in or action
"Could we agree on the pilot scope today?"
  • Following the structure keeps the meeting moving and clears blockages.

GROUPTHINK

groupthink
You hold a different view but stay silent because the others seem to agree. You assume everyone else must be right.
agreement ≠ consent
"Everyone agreed, nobody objected" doesn't mean everyone wanted it. Majority and consensus carry dissenters along.
minimum fix
At least get valid disagreements recorded in the minutes of meeting (MOM).
Abilene paradox
The extreme case: a whole group takes a trip that none of them wanted (story below).

Rules for wearing the hats (S6):

  • Modes, not personalities. Everyone switches hats within the same meeting.
  • No fixed sequence, and hats can repeat: white → red → white is fine, because your feelings may change once you see the data.
  • Wear the hat that is missing. If everyone is positive and nobody asks about cost or logistics, put on black. If everyone dwells on negatives, bring yellow.
  • You don't have to announce that you are using the framework.
  • Use it for brainstorming, creative solutions and getting unstuck, not for routine meetings. Picking the right framework is itself a skill.

Structuring a planned presentation: Preview → View → Review

Three sections, each with named parts, built around a few takeaways the audience can act on, with slides that keep the focus on the speaker.

THREE PILLARS OF A MEMORABLE TALK

  • Well-developed key takeaway messages. The audience is never confused about the point. Aim for 1–3 and design the whole talk around them.
  • Supporting evidence for every claim. Sources should be relevant, and recent where the topic needs it. This feeds S8: ethos, pathos, logos.
  • Clear structure: Preview → View → Review.
  • "Content is king." Content comes first and delivery second. Only then decide who says what, and how.

FINDING THE TAKEAWAYS: THREE QUESTIONS

  • What should they remember?
  • Why does it matter to them?
  • What should everything else support?
  • Busy audiences (senior management giving you 10–15 minutes) need the takeaways stated early and re-emphasised throughout.
  • Scope: planned talks only. Impromptu speaking uses other techniques.

PRIMACY AND RECENCY

  • Audiences remember the opening (primacy) and the close (recency) best, so both must be powerful.
  • In group work, know your members' strengths in advance. Put the strongest speakers first and last.
  • In the first two minutes the audience decides three things: keep listening? Will this benefit me? Is it worth taking away or acting on?

PREVIEW · ATTENTION GETTER

opening with a joke
A "double-edged sword" in an age of cancel culture and sensitivity. Use one only if you know the audience (S1 audience analysis) and it is relevant to the topic.
data dumping
Many statistics up front cause information overload, and the audience switches off. Use at most one striking statistic.
pick what fits the topic
Statistic, rhetorical question, image contrast, celebrity, origin story, "Imagine…". See the table below.

PREVIEW · POSITIONING + OVERVIEW

positioning statement
Plants the topic in the audience's minds: what the talk will do and what to look out for. China case: "…how small businesses like yours can easily move some or all of your manufacturing to China… a manufacturing presence in China in as little as 1 month."
overview
Breaks the positioning statement into its segments, which previews the takeaways. Keep it to 1–3 sentences in conversational language, with plenty of "you": you cut production cost by 40–60%, you maintain or increase quality, you cut time to market by another 1–3 months.
generic, no "you"
Without "you" language there is no direct connection, and the talk could be for anyone.

VIEW · MESSAGES, EVIDENCE, PREP

  • 2–4 key messages, max 4. Assume a skeptical audience: every position needs support.
  • Evidence types: cases, examples, stories, illustrations, analogies, statistics, facts, quotations, personal experience.
  • Use evidence in moderation: "The problem today is not information, it's information overload." Choose strong and relevant over plentiful.
  • PREP = Point → Reason → Example → Point. Repeat it for each message. After the restated point, bring in your USP.

REVIEW · RECAP, REINFORCE, CTA

recap
Summarise the key messages in a few sentences.
reinforce
Return to the single most important takeaway. The group decides which one.
call to action
Ask for a decision, a commitment or a next step, and make it highly specific and concrete. When you seek senior management's approval, the permission itself is the CTA, and how you articulate it matters most.
"thank you for your time"
Not an ending on its own.
"feel free to reach out to us"
"Building castles in the air." Without a CTA, the presentation is incomplete.

SLIDES · AVOID DEATH BY POWERPOINT

  • "Contrast controls your focus." Only point #4 of the talk was shown in class, so watch the rest yourself.
  • Lists: reveal one item at a time and dim the rest, so eyes follow "like a kitten chasing a laser pointer". Tables: highlight only the relevant row or cell.
  • A white background makes the screen the brightest, biggest object, so it competes with you. Prefer dark backgrounds.
  • "The speaker is the presentation; the slides are only a visual aid." Never memorise word for word.
  • T-shirt rule: ≤ 6 lines per slide. Keep slides clean and minimal, not a manuscript.
  • Put overflow detail on cue cards, small index cards with pointers. The deck is not a cue card.

GROUP DELIVERY ETIQUETTE

  • Signposted transitions, never abrupt: "To understand the financial implications of this business decision, [name] will walk us through the details." Rehearse them together.
  • Close: thank the audience, then open the floor to questions.
  • Q&A: say "I'll be happy to answer that question." Agree who answers; online, only that person unmutes. Start with "Thank you for that question. That's a very interesting observation."
  • Can't answer? Say honestly that it was outside the presentation's scope, offer to look into it, and take it offline (one-to-one afterwards).
  • Online: modulate your voice, keep gestures within the frame, look into the camera, and converse instead of reading.

  • No model script was given for the self-pitch: "Communication has no one-size-fits-all." Pitching yourself to Sundar Pichai would need a completely different script. The course teaches frameworks and mindsets, not templates.
  • "Treat this as a safe space to experiment": learning matters more than marks.

Where the audience shows up in the structure (the S1 refrain, applied in S7):

  • Takeaways start from why it matters to them, not what you want to say.
  • The overview speaks in "you". The close hands them one concrete next step, not "feel free to reach out".
  • Slides lead their eyes through contrast and keep them on the speaker.

Persuasion (ethos, pathos, logos) and what the final presentations rewarded

      
Rhetoric uses three appeals, balanced and ordered for the audience in front of you. The finals rewarded pitches with a 360-degree view and evidence put on the slide.

    


    

      

        
RHETORIC = THE ART OF PERSUASION

        

          
rhetoric
The art of persuasion, carried out through three appeals. Not the same as a "rhetorical question" (exam note).

          
ethos: why you?
Why you are the right person to speak: experience, qualifications, background. Gives the audience a reason to listen. Can be organisational: a long-established company already has credibility.

          
pathos: why care?
Why the audience should care and what is at stake. It is not about making them sad or making them cry. Exploitative pathos becomes manipulation.

          
logos: where's the proof?
Reasoning, reports, statistics. Not only numbers: spotting a market gap also counts. Cite your data.

        

      

      

        
THE TWO Rs OF EVIDENCE (LOGOS)

        

          
Recency
Data should be recent. Citing a 1996 study looks dated and raises questions about the quality of your argument.

          
Relevance
Evidence must relate directly to your topic. Irrelevant evidence looks like digression or poor preparation, so the evidence weakens and the whole argument falls apart.

          
skeptics / cynics
Doubt the information or its credibility. A pathos-only pitch draws "the story is convincing, but where are the numbers? Where are the returns?" Only logos answers them.

        

      

      

        
BALANCE BY AUDIENCE

        

          
finance / data manager
Lean on logos, but still use some pathos.

          
storyteller / marketer
Lean on pathos: why should they care?

          
you are new to the area
Establish ethos early, so they have a reason to listen.

          
mixed / mass audience
You can't analyse each member, so balance all three and overdo none.

          
order is flexible
You can open with a story, bring in ethos in the middle and end with logos. Ethos can emerge naturally as you describe what happened.

          
introspect
Which appeal is your default? Deliberately add the ones you ignore, and rise above your "default self".

        

      

    


    

      

        
FINAL PRESENTATION: RULES AND THE FEEDBACK PATTERN (S8 BRIEF, S9–10)

        
    
          
  • Preview → View → Review, with PREP for each key message. The instructor praised the groups for using this structure.
    • 
          
  • CTA = senior management's approval, the green signal.
    • 
          
  • Checked: primacy (how you open) and recency (how you close), the two parts the audience remembers. Transitions are also checked: close your part and signpost what comes next and who presents it. Don't go silent, and don't re-introduce yourself.
    • 
          
  • Time: 2 min minimum, 2.5 min maximum per speaker. A group of 5 got 14 min (5 × 2.5 = 12.5, +1.5); a group of 6 got 16.5 min (6 × 2.5 = 15, +1.5). A warning came at 2 min left, and overrun was not evaluated. Group dynamics were marked /10. Absence = automatic F.
    • 
          
  • Feedback pattern: give a 360-degree view, meaning benefits to employees, benefits to the company and the challenges. This matters most when pitching to senior management. Put concrete company case studies visibly on the slides.
    • 
          
  • "Everything is not about marks… it's more about learning."
    • 
        

      

      

        
REUSABLE TECHNIQUES THE GROUPS DEMONSTRATED (S9–10)

InformationSupported by information and dataInformation withheld from the other person

          
ReasonsHonest reasons and argumentsDeceptive reasons and arguments

S8 definition: persuasion means convincing and influencing, i.e. getting the audience to see something your way. There is a subtle line, and crossing it turns persuasion into manipulation.


    

      

        
APPEALTRAPFIX

        
          
EthosTalking about title, position and tenure instead of earning trust in the room; "I, me, myself" with no substanceEstablish credibility briefly, then move on and let the work speak

          
PathosLeading with emotion before the audience is ready; it comes across as manipulativeBuild rapport first, then bring in story or emotional appeal

          
LogosInformation overload; assuming the numbers will "sell themselves"Stagger the information and tell the story behind the numbers

        
      

    

    
General rule (S8): don't begin with too much of any one appeal; take a progressive approach. On the pathos trap: there is a thin line between making the audience care for good reasons and making them emotional with arguments that don't hold up.

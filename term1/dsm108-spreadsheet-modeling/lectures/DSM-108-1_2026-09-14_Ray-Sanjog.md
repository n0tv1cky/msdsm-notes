### 3.1 Exercise 1: Simple and Compound Interest

**Business problem:** Find the simple and compound interest on an investment.

**Formulas:**

Simple interest:
$$SI = P \times r \times t$$

Compound amount (compounded annually):
$$A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100}\right)^{t} = P(1+r)^{t}$$

Compound interest:
$$CI = A - P = P(1+r)^{t} - P$$

Here $P$ is the principal, $R$ is the rate as a number (e.g. 13), $r = R/100$ is the rate as a decimal (0.13), and $t$ is the number of years.

**Worksheet layout** (labels in the left column, values in the right) **[cell addresses inferred]**:

| Cell | Label (A) | Value (B) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Row 1 | Amount Invested | 20,000 | Formatted as currency using the ribbon, not typed by hand |
| Row 2 | Time (years) | 1 | See the ambiguity note below |
| Row 3 | Interest Rate | 13% | Typed as `13%`, or typed as 13 then formatted with the % button |
| Row 4 | Simple Interest | formula | |
| Row 5 | Compound Interest | formula | |

**Result discussed:** Simple interest = **₹2,600**, since $20000 \times 0.13 \times 1 = 2600$. The instructor noted that with $t = 1$, SI and CI give the same interest. If you change time to 2 years, they differ.

> ⚠️ **Ambiguity:** The transcript says "10 years… 10 year is how many? One year right?" and later "to the power 10." The SI result of 2,600 only works for $t = 1$. If $t = 10$ were used, then $A = 20000 \times 1.13^{10} \approx 67{,}891$ and $CI \approx 47{,}891$. A student also mentioned "27,800," which may come from different inputs. Check against your own file.

**Good practices stressed (these carry marks in exams):**

1. **Never type ₹ or $ by hand into a number cell.** Doing so turns the cell into a **text string**, and arithmetic on it will fail. Apply currency through the number-format options on the Home ribbon.
2. **Enter percentages as percentages.** Type `13%`, or type 13 and click the % format. Do not type 13 and then divide by 100 inside the formula. Formatted correctly, the formula needs no `/100`.
3. **Don't hard-code values in formulas.** Reference cells or names, not literal numbers such as `=20000*13/100`.
4. **Avoid spaces in variable or label names**, the same rule as in programming. When Excel creates names from labels, it replaces spaces with underscores.

#### Feature: Named Cells (the "first feature")

- **Why use it:** In a complex model, a formula like `=B1*B3*B2` is hard to understand when you, or someone you send it to, reviews it weeks later. Named cells make formulas self-documenting, like well-named variables in code. **The instructor gives marks for using named cells in exams.**
- **Method 1 (one cell):** Click the cell (e.g. the one with 20,000). Type a name into the **Name Box** to the left of the formula bar. Press **Enter**.
- **Method 2 (many at once):**
  1. Select both the label column and the value column.
  2. Go to **Formulas → Defined Names → Create from Selection**.
  3. Tick **Left column**, then click OK.
  4. Every value cell is now named after the label to its left. Check the names in **Formulas → Name Manager**.
- Names can be given to single cells, ranges, or whole tables.
- To audit formulas, use **Formulas → Show Formulas**.

**Formulas with names** (exact names depend on your labels; Excel converts spaces to `_`) **[inferred]**:

```excel
Simple Interest:
=Amount_Invested*Interest_Rate*Time

Compound Interest (caret operator):
=Amount_Invested*(1+Interest_Rate)^Time - Amount_Invested
```

#### Feature: the POWER function and the fx (Insert Function) dialog

The task was to rewrite the compound-interest formula with `POWER()` instead of the caret operator.

```excel
=Amount_Invested*POWER(1+Interest_Rate,Time) - Amount_Invested
```

- Syntax: `POWER(number, power)`, which computes $\text{number}^{\text{power}}$.
- **How to use the fx dialog:**
  1. Type `=POWER(`. The argument help only appears **after the opening bracket** is typed.
  2. Click the **fx** button next to the formula bar. A dialog opens and explains each argument.
  3. Fill in each field. The dialog shows a preview of the result.
- The instructor called **fx "your guide."** Use it whenever you meet an unfamiliar function, because exams may include functions you haven't seen before.

### 3.2 Exercise 2: Monthly SIP into a Mutual Fund

**Business problem:** On the first day of every month (1 Jan, 1 Feb, …, 1 Dec) you invest ₹20,000 in a mutual fund that returns 13% per year. What is the investment worth on 31 December of the same year?

**Answer:** **≈ ₹2,57,589.85** (Indian notation), which is ₹257,589.85.

> This matches the "2 57,589 85" heard in the transcript. Check: the monthly rate is $i = 0.13/12 \approx 0.0108333$. The annuity-due future value then comes to ≈ 257,590. If you get about **₹2,54,829**, you used end-of-month payments (`type = 0`), which is wrong here.

#### Method A: Excel's built-in `FV` function (Financial category)

All function categories are under **Formulas → Function Library**; `FV` is in **Financial**.

**When `FV` applies:** Use it only when the **payment is constant, the interest rate is constant, and payments are periodic**. If the rate or the SIP amount changes over time (e.g. the SIP grows 10% a year), `FV` cannot be used. You must build the logic manually, as in Method B.

**Syntax:** `FV(rate, nper, pmt, [pv], [type])`

| Argument | Required? | Meaning | Value here |
|---|---|---|---|
| `rate` | Required | Interest rate **per period** | `13%/12`. Payments are monthly, so divide the annual rate by 12. Use /4 for quarterly; leave as is for annual. |
| `nper` | Required | Number of payment periods | `12` |
| `pmt` | Required | Payment each period (the SIP amount) | `20000` |
| `pv` | Optional | Amount already in the fund at the start, e.g. ₹10 lakh | Omitted, so it defaults to 0 |
| `type` | Optional | **1 = payment at the beginning of each period**; 0 or omitted = payment at the end | **`1`**, since investments are made on the 1st |

In the fx dialog, the required arguments appear in **bold** and the optional ones in normal text.

**Why the result is negative:** `FV` follows the accounting cash-flow sign convention. Money you pay out is negative, so the future value comes back with the opposite sign. To show a positive result, put a minus sign in front, either on `pmt` or on the whole function.

```excel
=FV(13%/12, 12, -20000, 0, 1)
```

With named or linked inputs **[names and cells inferred]**:

```excel
=-FV(Interest_Rate/12, 12, SIP_Amount, , 1)
```

The `type` argument is the critical one. Leaving it out gives the end-of-month answer, which is wrong for this problem.

#### Method B: Manual calculation with the compound-interest formula

The instructor's point was: "Don't just trust Excel. Understand what it does behind the scenes."

**Key logic:** Each monthly deposit is an **independent investment** with its own tenure.

- January's ₹20,000 compounds for 12 months.
- February's ₹20,000 compounds for 11 months.
- …and December's ₹20,000 compounds for 1 month.

$$FV = \sum_{n=1}^{12} 20000\,(1+i)^{n}, \qquad i = \frac{0.13}{12}$$

This is the same as the annuity-due formula:

$$FV = PMT \cdot \frac{(1+i)^{N} - 1}{i}\,(1+i)$$

with $PMT = 20000$ and $N = 12$.

**Worksheet layout** **[columns and rows inferred]**:

| Column | Header | Content |
|---|---|---|
| A | Month | Jan, Feb, …, Dec. Type "Jan" and **drag the fill handle** to autofill the 12 months. |
| B | SIP Amount | 20,000 in every row |
| C | Monthly Rate | `=$B$3/12`, where B3 holds 13% (absolute reference) |
| D | Tenure (months) | 12, 11, 10, …, 1 |
| E | Value at 31 Dec | $P(1+r)^{n}$ for each row |
| F | % Contribution | Each row's value ÷ total |
| Total row | Net worth | `=SUM(...)` of column E, which equals the `FV` result |

**Formulas (row 6 as the first month, illustrative):**

```excel
C6:  =$B$3/12              ' absolute: always points at the annual-rate cell
E6:  =B6*(1+C6)^D6         ' or =B6*POWER(1+C6,D6)
E18: =SUM(E6:E17)          ' net worth, ≈ 2,57,589.85
F6:  =E6/$E$18             ' % contribution; total cell locked with $
```

> The transcript says the total cell was "I50"/"I15" and the rate cell was "B3." Your cell addresses will differ; only the `$` locking pattern matters. The phrase "divided by 0.183" in the transcript is an ASR garble of "divided by 12."

#### Feature: Absolute referencing (`$`, F4)

- **The problem:** If you drag `=B3/12` down one row, Excel adjusts it to `=B4/12`. The reference is **relative**, and B4 is empty.
- **The fix:** Click the reference in the formula and press **F4**. It becomes `$B$3`, an **absolute reference** that stays fixed when the formula is copied or dragged.
- **Where it was used:**
  - The rate cell in the monthly-rate column
  - The **total** cell in the % contribution formula. Every row must divide by the same total.
- Pressing F4 repeatedly cycles through `$B$3` → `B$3` → `$B3` → `B3`. This is standard Excel behaviour; the lecture only showed `$B$3`.

### 3.3 Exercise 3: Working with a Large Dataset (orders file)

**Tasks:** (a) How many orders are there? (b) What is the sum of Total Price?

#### Importing data the proper way

1. **First click the cell where the data should start**, typically A1 (the top-left corner). Data loads from the selected cell.
2. Go to **Data → Get & Transform Data → Get Data → From File → From Text/CSV**.
   - Other options include databases and existing connections. Excel can act as a connector to many data sources.
3. Browse to the file and click **Load**.
4. Double-clicking the CSV to open it also works, but the import route is the "proper" one.

#### Converting the imported Table back to a normal range

- Imported data arrives as an **Excel Table**, a database-like object. You can tell because a **Table Design** tab appears when you click inside it.
- This course does not use Tables, so: click anywhere in the data, then **Table Design → Tools → Convert to Range** and confirm.
- Afterwards the Table Design tab no longer appears.

#### Navigation shortcuts

| Shortcut | Action |
|---|---|
| Ctrl + ↓ / → | Jump to the last filled cell in that direction |
| Ctrl + Shift + ↓ | Select from the current cell down to the end of the data |
| Ctrl + Home | Go to cell A1 |
| Ctrl + End | Go to the last used cell |

Laptops differ. Some lack a Home key or need the Fn key, so find your own equivalents.

#### Good habits with large data

1. **Always add a filter first:** **Data → Filter**, or Ctrl + Shift + L.
2. **Freeze the header row:** **View → Freeze Panes → Freeze Top Row**, so headings stay visible while scrolling.
   - Other options are **Freeze First Column**, or **Freeze Panes** to freeze all rows above and columns left of the selected cell.
   - Freezing works from the top or left edge. You cannot freeze a block in the middle of the sheet.

#### Naming whole columns, then COUNT and SUM

1. Click the first data cell of the order ID column. Press **Ctrl + Shift + ↓**. Type `Orders` in the Name Box and press Enter.
2. Do the same for the Total Price column, e.g. naming it `Total_Price` **[the name was not clearly heard]**.
3. Write the formulas:

```excel
Number of orders:
=COUNT(Orders)          ' COUNT counts cells that contain NUMBERS

Total of prices:
=SUM(Total_Price)
```

- `COUNT` counts only numeric cells. If the order IDs are text, use `=COUNTA(Orders)`. COUNTA is not mentioned in the lecture, but it is the standard alternative.
- With named ranges you never have to reselect the data. You can also name a whole table and use the name in lookups (e.g. `VLOOKUP`), which comes later.

### 3.4 Exercise 4: Data Cleaning and Choosing the Right Chart (smartphone market share)

**Data:** Smartphone brands with their parent company and market share or volume. Examples mentioned: Samsung, Xiaomi, Vivo, Realme, Oppo, under parent groups such as **BBK Electronics** (heard as "BK"/"DK electron").

#### Feature: Text to Columns (data cleaning)

The raw data arrived as a single column.

1. Select the whole column.
2. Go to **Data → Data Tools → Text to Columns**.
3. Choose between the two modes:
   - **Fixed width:** you place break lines at fixed character positions. This failed here because it cut "Samsung" in the middle.
   - **Delimited:** the data is split by a separator character. **Use this.** Tick the matching delimiter (Tab, Comma, etc.; the exact one was not clearly audible) and check the preview.
4. Click **Finish**. The data is now in **3 columns**, likely Parent Company, Brand, and Share/Value **[inferred]**.

#### Chart task

Draw **one chart only** that instantly answers both questions:

1. **Which is the biggest parent company?** → **BBK Electronics.** Its brands Vivo, Realme, and Oppo combined are the largest.
2. **Which is the biggest individual company or brand?** → **Xiaomi.** The transcript mentions "40.7"; the units are unclear.

- Student attempts with ordinary bar or column charts failed. They showed either the parent level or the brand level, not both.
- **Correct chart:** a hierarchical chart, either a **Treemap** or a **Sunburst**.
  1. Select the three columns.
  2. Go to **Insert → Charts → Hierarchy (Insert Hierarchy Chart) → Treemap**, or choose Sunburst.
  3. In a Treemap, parent groups appear as large blocks containing the brand rectangles. Rectangle size shows share.
- The instructor's other course covers data visualization and storytelling (Tableau, Power BI). Choosing the *right* chart is a recurring theme.

Session 01

Opportunity cost, economic cost, economic rent

Why growth took off ~250 years ago, and the three-part vocabulary for how anyone decides whether an action is worth it.

The big picture

  • Growth was flat for millennia, then a "hockey stick" from ~1750 — the Malthusian trap (income gains eaten by population growth) finally broken.
  • Escape driven by continuous technological progress — the Industrial Revolution, starting in Britain.
  • Adam Smith (1776) predicted it; Malthus (1798) didn't believe it possible.

Formulas

Net benefit
Benefit − Cost.
Opportunity cost
net benefit of the next-best alternative.
Economic cost
direct cost + opportunity cost.
Economic rent
net benefit − opportunity cost (= Benefit − Economic cost).

Decision rule

  • Do A if Net benefit(A) > Opportunity cost(A) — equivalently, Benefit(A) > Economic cost(A). The two can never disagree.
  • Positive rent → do it; zero → indifferent; negative → don't.
  • Worked numbers (concert vs. babysitting): net benefit €30, opportunity cost €22, economic rent €8.
  • Both benefit and cost are subjective — rent is person-specific.

A model is a map, not a photograph — it throws away almost everything so the one mechanism you care about stands out.

  • To measure a subjective benefit or cost: keep raising the price until you'd refuse (that ceiling is your benefit); keep lowering the payment until you'd refuse the deal (that floor is your cost).
  • Why economic cost isn't accounting cost: a US steel firm had a power contract at $3/unit — cheap on the books. When a power crisis sent the market price to $30/unit, the opportunity cost of burning that power on steel was $30, not $3 — so the firm shut its plant and started reselling power instead. The ledger never would have told them to do that.

Session 02

Comparative advantage & gains from trade

Even a worse-at-everything producer should specialize — in whatever they're least worse at.

Definitions

Absolute advantage
can produce more of a good with the same inputs.
Comparative advantage
lower opportunity cost of that good, relative to the other good.

Greta & Carlos worked numbers

  • Opportunity cost of 1 ton wheat — Greta 25 apples, Carlos 50 apples.
  • Lower-cost producer specializes: Greta → wheat, Carlos → apples.
  • Self-sufficiency total: 800 apples, 44 tons wheat. Specialize + trade: 1,000 apples, 50 tons wheat — bigger pie, both eat more.

Rule to remember

  • Specialize where your opportunity cost is lowest — not where you're most productive in absolute terms.
  • Opportunity cost exists even with zero out-of-pocket cost.
  • Trade price must sit between both parties' opportunity costs for both to gain.

Even if Greta is better than Carlos at growing both crops, she still can't do both at once — the question is never "what am I good at," it's "what do I lose least by doing." She loses less by growing wheat than by growing apples, so wheat is where her time is best spent, full stop.

Session 03

Technology choice & the isocost line

Once engineering-dominated technologies are discarded, only relative input prices decide which one's cheapest.

Formulas

Production function
Y = f(inputs); constant returns to scale: f(λX) = λf(X).
Average product
AP = output ÷ input quantity.
Energy–labour ratio
= slope of the ray from origin = energy intensity.
Isocost line
C = wL + pR; intercepts C/w and C/p; slope −w/p.

Rules

  • Dominated technology: uses more of both inputs than another for the same output — discard it (the quadrant test), no prices needed.
  • What's left can only be ranked with input prices.
  • Proportional change in both input prices → isocost line unchanged. Only relative prices matter.
  • Only one price changing → isocost line rotates.

The Industrial Revolution story

  • British wages rose relative to coal price → isocost line steepened.
  • Switched cost-minimising choice from labour-intensive to energy-intensive technology.
  • Same output, lower cost → higher profit → innovation rent for the first adopter → Schumpeter's creative destruction.

Why "fixed proportions" — it's cooking. Extra labour with no extra ingredients doesn't make more food; you need both together, in the ratio the recipe calls for. That's why you can't scale one input alone: double the workers with the same machines and coal, and output barely moves.

Sessions 07–08

Game theory & social dilemmas

When two or more people's payoffs depend on each other's choices — sometimes self-interest is fine, sometimes it wrecks everyone.

Vocabulary

social dilemma
actions taken independently, in self-interest, produce a socially suboptimal outcome — relative to another outcome that was feasible.
game
players, feasible strategies, information, payoffs.
best response
the strategy giving the highest payoff given the other's choice.
Nash equilibrium
each player's strategy is a best response to the other's.
dominant strategy
best response to every strategy the other might play.

Solving a 2×2 matrix

  • Row player: tick the higher payoff down each column (holding the other's choice fixed).
  • Column player: tick the higher payoff across each row.
  • Cell with two ticks = Nash equilibrium. A game can have more than one — or none in pure strategies.

Two archetypes

  • Invisible hand (crop game): self-interest → the cell with the highest payoff for everyone.
  • Prisoners' Dilemma (pest control): dominant-strategy equilibrium gives lower payoffs than a feasible alternative — driven by external effects each player ignores.
  • Pareto improvement: at least one better off, nobody worse off. Pareto efficient = not dominated by any other allocation. A dominant-strategy equilibrium need not be Pareto efficient.
  • Fixes: altruism (indifference curves over payoffs turn downward-sloping), repeated interaction (threat of reverting to the bad equilibrium), taxes, institutions.
  • "A model is a caricature — that's the feature, not the bug." Nobody actually computes payoff matrices before choosing; you learn to play tennis by hand-eye coordination, not equations — but the programmer coding an Xbox tennis game still has to write those equations for the character to behave like a real player would. That's what a game-theory model is doing to your own decisions.
  • Social dilemma, made concrete: nobody wants to sit in traffic, yet everyone drives — each person counts their own fuel cost and comfort, not the jam they add to everyone else's commute. Same logic behind a group flat's permanently dirty kitchen: cleaning is real effort, everyone hopes someone else does it, so nobody does.

Sessions 09–10

The firm and its customers

Firms maximise profit subject to the demand curve — the same "willing vs. constrained" logic as the worker's problem, with price and quantity instead of consumption and free time.

Profit & the isoprofit curve

π = (P − C)·Q
TR = P·Q, TC = C·Q.
isoprofit curve
locus of (P, Q) at constant profit; downward-sloping; north-east = higher profit.
demand curve
the firm's constraint; downward-sloping; y-intercept = reservation price.

Scale & cost curves

  • Returns to scale: Q(λX,λY) =/>/< λQ → CRS / IRS / DRS.
  • AC = C(Q)/Q = slope of the ray from origin. MC = slope of the total-cost curve.
  • MC < AC → AC falling; MC > AC → AC rising; they cross at minimum AC = minimum efficient scale.
  • Class-height trick: if the 30th person joining a room is taller than the room's average, the average rises. Same logic: if the next unit's cost (MC) is above the average so far (AC), average cost rises — below it, average cost falls. That's the whole rule, no calculus needed.

Pricing formulas

MR = P + Q·(dP/dQ)
profit-max condition: MR = MC.
isoprofit slope
dP/dQ = −(P−MC)/Q.
elasticity ε
(dQ/Q)/(dP/P) — always negative.
Lerner condition
(P−MC)/P = −1/ε — less price-sensitive customers ⇒ higher margin.

Elasticity, classified

  • ε = −∞ — perfectly elastic (any price rise, customers leave entirely).
  • −∞ < ε < −1 — elastic.
  • ε = −1 — unitary elastic.
  • −1 < ε < 0 — inelastic.
  • ε = 0 — perfectly inelastic (price doesn't move quantity at all — e.g. a life-saving medicine).

Price discrimination

1st degree
a different price per person — e.g. pricing by browsing history, or US tuition by parental income.
2nd degree
discount conditional on quantity — "buy one get one," bulk pricing.
3rd degree
pricing by observable group — student discounts, weekday happy hours.

Why margin strategy isn't one-size-fits-all — same theory, opposite strategies, both rational, because elasticity differs, not competence:

  • Tesco: "pile it high, sell it cheap" — ~6.5% margin, profit from volume, flat demand curve (customers have little willingness to pay above cost).
  • Apple: prices at a premium — ~50–58% margin on iPhones, profit per unit, steep demand curve (loyal customers, high willingness to pay).

What macro studies, and measuring GDP

Macro studies aggregates and the short-run swings around the long-run trend, and GDP can be counted three ways (final uses, value added, factor incomes) that must always give the same total.

MACRO = AGGREGATES (S11)

recession
all markets hit a demand constraint together; auto, aviation and hotel workers are fired at the same time
sectoral shift ≠ macro
one good's demand falls while another's rises; jobs reappear elsewhere (landlines → mobiles)
potential output
what is feasible with existing capacity
actual output
what is actually produced; lower in a depression
output gap
potential − actual: the capacity exists but sits idle
the four questions
Why expansions? Why recessions? What causes inflation (a general rise in prices)? Should the government intervene, and if so how and when?

GDP: DEFINITION AND IDENTITY

GDP
the market value of all final goods and services produced by factors of production within a nation's borders in a given period
output ≡ income ≡ expenditure
your shirt is your expenditure, the seller's income and someone's output
factor → payment
land → rent · labour → wages · capital → interest · entrepreneurship → profit
three approaches
expenditure C + I + G + X − M
production Σ GVA + net production taxes
income Σ factor incomes + net indirect taxes
market prices vs factor cost
GDP at market prices = GDP at factor cost + indirect taxes − subsidies

VALUE ADDED AND IMPUTATION

GVA = GVO − IC
count final goods only; intermediate goods are embedded in the final good (wheat in bread). 750 + 1,000 = 1,750 double-counts.
GVA ≠ profit

counted (imputed)
self-produced goods at market price · owner-occupier's imputed rent · paid domestic help
not counted
cooking, cleaning or childcare done at home, because there is no fair price for it
inventory

perished output

EXPENDITURE SIDE · GROSS/NET · DOMESTIC/NATIONAL (S12)

  • C: durable / non-durable, food / non-food. Living in a house is C; building it is I.
  • I = fixed (factories, machines) + inventories, both planned and unplanned. The same car or laptop is C at home and I in a business.
  • G is valued at its cost of production, because its fees are not meaningful market prices.
  • +X, −M: exports earn domestic income with no domestic C, I or G. Imports are spent through C, I or G but earn no domestic income.
  • NDP = GDP − depreciation (consumption of fixed capital, including obsolescence). Net I = I − depreciation.
  • GNP goes by the residency of the factor owner (e.g. >183 days), not citizenship. Remittances are current transfers, not factor income.

SAVING = INVESTMENT, AND WHAT GDP LEAVES OUT

  • Disposable income: Y − T + TR
  • Sh = (Y − T + TR − C) = I + (G + TR − T) + (X − M). Household saving funds investment, the fiscal deficit and the trade surplus.
  • Twin deficits: if Sh and I stay put and the fiscal deficit rises, then X − M falls (the current-account deficit rises).
  • Excluded: financial transactions (shares, bonds); second-hand goods (only the broker's margin counts); transfers (pensions, unemployment pay, PM-Kisan, Ladli Behna); gifts; illegal activity.
  • Counted anyway: a matchmaker's fee. Samosa sellers and barbers are estimated from consumption surveys. MGNREGA is not a pure transfer, because work is done.

Why macro is about aggregates (S11):

  • Great Depression, two scenarios. Did the factories and farms of the 1920s vanish in the 1930s? Or did factories, farms and workers all still exist, idle because firms feared they could not sell? The answer is the second: capacity was intact and the expected demand was missing. India's typical development problem is the first kind: workers exist, but the capacity does not.
  • Sectoral shifts are not macro: Ola, Uber and Rapido vs autos; landlines → mobiles; diet changes. Resources move to where they are now worth more (opportunity cost, module 1). "Probably don't bail out the declining sector": consumers' preferences have moved.
  • Sometimes not intervening is the better choice.

Inside the circular flow (S11, S12):

  • Mukesh Ambani "belongs to the Antilia household" and supplies labour to Reliance: households own every factor.
  • A government university charges ₹1,000/yr and a school ₹500–1,000: economically insignificant prices. Whether to produce an extra unit is not decided by price, so the payment line is drawn thin (S11). Surgery costs ₹1 lakh privately vs ₹3,000–5,000 in a government hospital, so G is valued at cost (S12).
  • Apple economy: 100 apples × ₹1 = 100 = wages 70 + profit 30 = C 60 + G 10 + I 30. (Once in class it came out as "buy back 70, save 30". That version drops G.)
  • A firm doesn't care whether it sells to you or to an American; it is paid either way. That is why all final-demand flows are added together.

Value added, imputation and inventory (S11 → S12):

  • Refinery: crude at $100 a barrel becomes product at $105, so value added is $5. Likewise the baker's ₹5 is the price of the conversion, not of the bread.

Nominal vs real, price indices, and who loses from inflation

Value every year at base-year prices to strip out price changes, measure the price level with a fixed basket, and see who gains when inflation is uneven or comes as a surprise.

NOMINAL vs REAL GDP

nominal GDP (current prices)
Σ Pt·Qt: this year's quantities at this year's prices. Mixes price change and volume change.
real GDP (constant prices)
Σ Pb·Qt: this year's quantities at base-year prices. Volume only.
why prices at all
Prices are the weights. You can't add kg of wheat to kg of potato ("apples and oranges").
growth rule
gnominal ≈ greal + π. Real 7–7.5% + inflation 4–5% ≈ 11–12.5%. Newspaper growth ("grew 7.8%") is always real.
welfare
Use per capita GDP, not the total. Income is a flow (earned each year); wealth is a stock (accumulated past income and savings).

PRICE INDICES

CPI, weighted form
CPIt = Σ si,b · Pi,t/Pi,b. Each price relative is weighted by its base-year spending share s (0 < s < 1, Σ s = 1). For example, per ₹1: 25p health, 25p education, 20p food, 10p rent.
= Laspeyres ratio
Σ PtQb / Σ PbQb (×100). Denominator: what I spent on the base-year basket at base prices. Numerator: what the same basket costs at today's prices.
inflation
πt = (CPIt − CPIt−1) / CPIt−1 × 100. It compares the same basket in consecutive years. The RBI target is 4% ± 2% (2% to 6%), with 4% as the anchor.
CPI vs WPI
CPI: retail, goods and services (education, haircuts, rent). WPI: wholesale, goods only. WPI is the early-warning signal and the course's stand-in for a producer price index.
the basket
Built from the NSO Consumption Expenditure Survey (about 1.5 lakh households). An independent committee picks the items, with no target count. The basket differs across countries and across states.

INTEREST RATES · BASE YEAR

Fisher, exact
1 + r = (1 + i) / (1 + π), where i is the nominal rate and r is the real rate.
Fisher, approximate
r ≈ i − πᵉ. Lenders set i with expected inflation in mind.
surprise inflation
If π > πᵉ, wealth moves from lenders to borrowers, who repay in "cheaper rupees". Sellers gain when their prices outrun what they buy.
base-year rules
Pick a normal year, as recent as possible. India: 2004–05 → 2011–12 → 2022–23 (revised Feb 2026).
single vs double deflation
2011–12 series: single for manufacturing, double for agriculture. 2022–23 series: double for both (the IMF prescribes it). The question is debated; the lecturer leans to single for manufacturing because double deflation has conceptual problems. Double deflation measures volume from both the output and the input side.

Why bother with real (S12, S13):

  • ₹50,000 → ₹1.5 lakh looks like a tripling, but prices rose about 50–70%. Real gain: 3/1.5 = 2.0× down to 3/1.7 ≈ 1.76×. The lecture rounded this to "about 1.5–2×" (S12).
  • "The base year is very crucial." Valuing every year at the same prices is the only way to tell whether people later on actually have more goods (S13).
  • Per capita GDP, 1950–51: ₹285 at 1950 prices, or about ₹13,000 at 2011–12 prices. Only the constant-price figure is meaningful. By 2021–22 the average Indian is roughly 8–9× better off in real terms (S13).

Picking and revising the base year (S13):

  • Avoid abnormal years: 2020 (COVID), 2016–17 (demonetisation), 2017–18 (GST). A student added that 2025 (war, disruption) isn't ideal either.
  • Pen drive: 512 MB cost ₹2,000–3,000 in 2006–08, and far bigger drives cost about the same today. Value today's drives at old prices and people look implausibly rich, so an old base year overstates growth.
  • Grandparents vs us: land and wooden furniture against ACs and fridges. Lifestyles that far apart can't be compared, so keep the base recent.
  • Single vs double deflation is "Coca-Cola vs Thums Up": an academic matter of taste.

Price indices in practice (S13):

  • WPI leads CPI: printed menus and MRPs change only with the next batch, which was bought at the higher wholesale price.
  • Baskets differ by state: food weighs more in Bihar than in Kerala, rent weighs heavily in Mumbai, and betel leaf/supari is in Assam's basket but may not be in Rajasthan's. Weights follow actual consumption, so adding items can't easily rig the index.

Who loses from inflation (S13):

  • Your spending is someone's income. If every price rises 10%, incomes rise 10% too, and the medical-product maker is indifferent. You gain only if your own price or income outruns what you buy.
  • "A subtle point but a very important point": food-led inflation plus DA. The rich spend about 15% of income on food, the poor about 50%. DA tracks the all-consumer CPI, so the indexed rich are compensated for inflation that mostly hits others, and they spend it on iPads. Maids and drivers aren't indexed, so inflation is regressive.
  • Lend ₹100, get ₹110: at 6% inflation the lender buys more chocolates than today. At 12% the lender buys fewer, and the toffee-factory borrower who sells at ₹112 gains.

Sticky prices, the Keynesian cross and the multiplier

A recession cuts output rather than prices, the 45° line finds the equilibrium, ₹1 of spending becomes more than ₹1 of output, and taxes and transfers deliberately make that multiplier smaller.

SHORT RUN: OUTPUT MOVES, PRICES STICK (S13)

potential output Y*
Output from labour and capital at full employment, used in the normal way. It is the average sustainable level, not three 8-hour shifts.
frictional unemployment
About 4–5% even at full employment, because workers take time to move between sectors. The real problem is when firms are firing and no one is hiring.
sticky prices
Customer habituation: once a price is cut, customers get used to it, so it is hard to raise again.
nominal wages
Rigid downward. Cutting prices needs cutting wages, so firms cut output (and jobs) first.
flat SRAS
With unemployment, firms recall workers at prevailing wages, so output rises with no rise in cost and the curve is horizontal. At full employment, workers must be poached at higher wages, so it slopes up.
Keynes
Cycles are driven by shifts in AD; Y* does not move. Policy: boost AD.

DEMAND SIDE AND THE CROSS (S13–S14)

consumption
C = C̄ + cY, with MPC c = ΔC/ΔY and 0 < c < 1. The largest AD component: about 60% of GDP in India and 70% in the US.
what drives C
Income (the main one), future prospects, pensions and social security, the interest rate, and age (life cycle). The short-run model uses income only.
saving
S = Y − C
inventory rule
Y > AD → unplanned accumulation → firms cut output · Y < AD → depletion → firms raise output · Y = AD → equilibrium, S = I.
45° line
tan 45° = 1, so height = base: at every point, AD = Y.
AD line
AD = Ā + cY. Its slope c < 1 makes it flatter than 45°, so it crosses the 45° line once.
Ā (lump-sum taxes)
Ā = C̄ − c·TA + c·TR + Ī + Ḡ: every term that does not depend on Y. Disposable income is YD = Y − TA + TR.

MULTIPLIERS (S14–S15)

simple
α = 1/(1 − c). By equation: Y = Ā + cY → Y(1 − c) = Ā. By GP: 1 + c + c² + … = a/(1 − r). At c = 0.8, α = 5.
proportional tax TA = tY
AD = Ā + c(1 − t)Y, where Ā loses the −c·TA term. αG = 1/(1 − c(1 − t)), which is smaller than 1/(1 − c) because c(1 − t) < c.
transfer / lump-sum tax
ΔY = c·αG·ΔTR and ΔY = −c·αG·ΔT. Both are smaller than for ΔG, because households save (1 − c) before anything is bought: the first round is skipped.
raise t
Two effects: disposable income falls, so AD falls, and the multiplier shrinks. In a recession, cutting t raises the multiplier.

THE BUDGET AND THE CYCLE (S14–S15)

budget surplus
BS = tY − G − TR: intercept −(G + TR), slope t. Deficits widen automatically in bad times, so the deficit is not independent of the cycle.
automatic stabilizers
Income tax, unemployment benefit, MGNREGA. In a boom, disposable income rises less than Y; in a bust, it falls less. No case-by-case approval is needed.
countercyclical
In a downturn, borrow and spend. In a boom, tighten the belt and let the private sector spend.
procyclical
Spend more when revenue is high; fire staff and cut spending when it falls. This makes the swings bigger and is the most undesirable stance.

WHAT EQUILIBRIUM IS NOT

  • Equilibrium ≠ full employment. E₁ can sit at Y₁ < Y*. Equilibrium means "whatever is produced is sold", not "whatever can be produced is produced".
  • No idle resources, no multiplier. At full employment, extra G only reallocates (cement moves from houses to roads) and raises prices.
  • Short run vs long run. This module is the short run: demand decides how much of existing capacity is used. Capacity (Y*) is the long-run, supply-side question; growth is not covered.
  • Direct spending vs transfers (2008). ΔG has the bigger multiplier. Transfers let households choose what they actually need.

Why recessions cut output, not prices (S13):

  • Potential is like your absorption just before exams. It peaks, but you can't sustain it 365 days a year. Y* is the average sustainable level.
  • Frictional, not idle: STD/PCO booths close while delivery jobs open; the auto industry shrinks as people move to shared mobility; a locality switches from Chinese to Indian food, so chefs retrain. None of this means the economy is below full employment.
  • Ben Bernanke, Essays on the Great Depression. In the 1930s, factories and workers both existed, yet firms didn't hire because they feared they couldn't sell. That gave birth to formal macroeconomics.
  • Habituation: cut a ₹5,000 product to ₹4,000 and customers get used to it, so raising it back when demand returns is hard.
  • The barber: a haircut sells for ₹100 and the barber earns ₹3,000–5,000 a month. Selling at ₹80 needs a wage cut that workers refuse; they distrust the "recession" claim, and it is a matter of prestige. "Easier to fire than to cut wages." Wages do fall eventually, but it takes months or years.
  • COVID juice and ice cream: one-week-shelf-life stock was written off across many firms at once. That is a macro shock. A shift from wheat to millets, where one product loses and another gains, is micro.

The multiplier story (S13–S15):

  • Highway → cement and steel → recalled workers at prevailing wages. Output expands along the flat SRAS with no rise in cost (S13, again in S14–S15).
  • ₹1,000 crore factory: recalled workers spend on restaurant meals, shirts and holidays. Restaurants and textile firms, which had also laid off staff, rehire them, and those workers spend in turn, round after round (S14).
  • At full employment the highway only diverts cement away from houses, and house-building workers become road workers. That is reallocation plus inflation, with no multiplier (S14).
  • Planned vs unplanned inventory: car makers build stock in June–August for Diwali, Dussehra and Ganesh Puja. That is planned. Stock that piles up because expected sales never came is unplanned (S14).
  • Said twice in S14: "Equilibrium only means whatever is produced is sold, not that whatever can be produced is produced."

Why Prof. Sethia prefers a smaller multiplier (S14):

  • It works in reverse too. A firm relocates abroad or a project is judged unviable; laid-off workers cut spending, which causes more layoffs, round after round. A smaller multiplier damps this.
  • ₹100 example: 70 vs 28. The tax-and-transfer system is "macro insurance": the government takes from you in good times and gives to you in bad times.
  • Discretionary policy is slow: data, project design, parliamentary approval and "build the highway in my region". Stabilizers kick in automatically, because a newly unemployed person qualifies for the benefit or MGNREGA work and keeps spending.
  • After the 1930s, personal income tax and transfer systems made business cycles much milder; no comparable downturn has followed.
  • Two chat polls (counted for attendance). First: which is bigger at t = 20%? Answer: 1/(1 − c), because c(1 − t) < c. Second: is a bigger multiplier better? Most of the class said yes; one student said "depends", since it would cause inflation at full capacity. The professor said smaller.

Fiscal policy over the cycle (S15):

  • 2008: Obama argued for direct spending, with the government as "buyer of last resort": it has the bigger multiplier because tax cuts are partly saved. The counter-case: political distortions ("why not a project in my constituency?") produce useless roads, and spending can prop up dying regions and old technologies with no lasting benefit.
  • Procyclical policy copies household logic. "Earn less, spend less" is right for a family and wrong for a government.
  • COVID: revenue fell while spending ballooned (existing salaries for teachers and doctors, plus new health measures), so borrowing soared and rating agencies worried. The DEA Secretary explained to them why India's rating should not be cut for this borrowing; pandemics are not the time to worry about deficits.
  • Proportional taxes damp booms, but in a recession that damping is unhelpful, so governments may cut t to raise the multiplier.

Money, banks, bonds and the RBI's tools

The RBI prints only the base. Banks multiply it into deposits, bond prices move against interest rates, and every lever (CRR, OMO, repo, SLR) is finally aimed at goods, jobs and output.

WHAT MONEY IS (S15)

money
Whatever people use as a medium of exchange. It is backed by the belief that others will accept it; in India, that belief is trust in the government. It is the most liquid asset: a house, a share or a bond must first be turned into money.
four functions
Medium of exchange (ends the double coincidence of wants) · store of value (imperfect: ₹100 buys less next year) · unit of account · standard of deferred payment.
commodity money
Gold, silver. Has intrinsic value but a real-resource cost, and small denominations are hard.
fiat money
No intrinsic value, created "out of thin air". It holds value through trust and government discipline. Over-printing causes inflation.
the refrain
"More money does not create more real goods." Output depends on factories, steel and cement.

MEASURES AND THE MULTIPLIER (S15–S16)

M0 = H = monetary base
H = CU + RE: currency with the public plus bank reserves. Also called high-powered, base or narrow money. The RBI creates only cash.
M3 = M = broad money
M = CU + D: currency plus demand and time deposits. Commercial banks (SBI, HDFC, Canara, PNB) create the deposits.
money multiplier
mm = M/H = (1 + cu)/(cu + re) and M = mm × H, where cu = CU/D and re = RE/D.
comparative statics
re ↓ → mm ↑: at 20%, ₹1 of reserves backs 5 of deposits; at 10% it backs 10.
cu ↑ → mm ↓ (because re < 1): ₹1 in a wallet backs ₹1, while ₹1 in a vault backs 1/re.
what moves the ratios
cu: distance to ATMs, UPI acceptance (shoe-leather costs), festival cash (Diwali, Christmas). re: the RBI's CRR plus banks' excess reserves.

BANKS AND BONDS (S16)

only banks create money
Their liabilities (deposits) are themselves a means of payment. Bond and equity markets only transfer savings.
bare-minimum reserves
Reserves earn nothing and loans earn interest, so banks hold exactly the CRR.
perpetual bond
P = C / i. ₹100 a year at 10% is worth ₹1,000. Price and rate move inversely.
four statements, one event
RBI sells bonds = money supply ↓ = interest rates ↑ = bond prices ↓. When the RBI buys, all four flip.
why the RBI moves money
Inflation → contract. Recession → expand and cut rates so firms build factories and roads.

THE RBI'S TOOLS (S16–S17)

  • CRR changes the multiplier. OMO / repo changes the base. Banks prefer repo because CRR money earns nothing.
  • Repo: the RBI lends to banks (the ceiling). Reverse repo: the RBI accepts banks' deposits (the floor). 1 bp = 0.01%.
  • reverse repo ≤ WACR ≤ repo. Tight liquidity → WACR near the ceiling; surplus → near the floor. Spikes outside the band come from non-banks such as mutual funds.
  • Reverse repo must be < repo. Otherwise a bank could borrow from the RBI and park the money back at the RBI for a riskless profit.
  • SLR: a share of deposits held as government securities, on top of the CRR. It creates a captive market (financial repression). It is about allocation, not money supply.
  • Locked: 4% + 18% = 22%, so 78% is loanable (S16 figures).

BALANCE SHEET AND RUNS (S16)

  • Loss order: equity → debt holders → depositors. The owners chose the borrowers, so they lose first.
  • Leverage: a small equity base supports large loans made with depositors' money.
  • Skin in the game: equity holders monitor lending; depositors don't.
  • Bank run = a self-fulfilling prophecy. The money sits in 10-year loans that can't be recalled.
  • Liquidity ≠ solvency: a bank can be short of cash today while its loans still cover its deposits.
  • Lender of last resort: the RBI lends to solvent banks and can publicly vouch for their books.
  • Deposit insurance ₹5 lakh (strictly per depositor per bank). It exists to stop rumour-driven withdrawals.

TRANSMISSION TO THE REAL ECONOMY (S17)

the chain
RBI buys bonds → banks have surplus liquidity → lending rates fall → investment ↑ (plus housing and auto loans) → Ā ↑ → AD line shifts up → Y moves toward Y*.
investment demand
Slopes down in i, because interest is a cost. Cheaper credit makes unviable projects viable.
equilibrium ≠ potential
Firms produce what they can sell. The gap Y* − Y₁ is idle capacity.
the master
"The financial sector is not the master; it is the servant. The real economy is the master." (S16)

US, JAN 2000 → NOW: THE MULTIPLIER FELL (S16 chart)

  • Base grew from about $600 bn to about $5 tn: 5000/600 ≈ 8.3×. (The notes say 8–9×; the chart description says 9–10×.) It jumped twice, after the 2008 GFC and during 2020 COVID.
  • Money supply M grew from $4.6 tn to about $22 tn: 22/4.6 ≈ 4.8× ("about 5×").
  • mm fell from about 8 to 4.2. The endpoints give 4.6/0.6 ≈ 7.7 and 22/5 = 4.4.
  • Why: the Fed pays interest on reserves, and with borrowers defaulting, banks were risk-averse. "A central bank cannot force banks to lend."

INDIA: THE MULTIPLIER ROSE, AND DEMONETISATION (Nov 2016)

  • The base grew more slowly and M grew faster, so mm rose over the period.
  • Demonetisation: currency was deposited, so currency in circulation fell.
  • Banks couldn't find borrowers fast enough, so they parked the excess at the reverse repo window. M0 fell; base money was effectively "destroyed".
  • M = C + D was unchanged, because currency had only turned into deposits. With the denominator down, mm jumped.

What makes something money (S15):

  • A demonetised note is no longer legal tender. If people still accepted it, it would still work as money.
  • Barter: to get a haircut, the professor would need a barber who wants an economics lecture.
  • Gold standard debased: 1 g of gold = ₹100 is re-declared as ₹200, and sellers immediately ask ₹200 for what cost ₹100. It works like shrinkflation: the ₹10 noodle packet keeps its price but shrinks.
  • A ₹2,000 note's scrap value is negligible. "I promise to pay the bearer" only means the RBI gives you another note, not gold.
  • The ₹1 note is signed by the Finance Secretary, because authority sits with the government in Delhi. All other notes are signed by the RBI Governor, because printing was delegated to Mumbai.
  • Crypto (a student's point): it has no backing asset, so it is like fiat money without legal-tender status.

Commodity cost and over-printing (S15, S16):

  • Mughal silver: India had few silver mines, so it exported textiles and spices for silver. Control of the trade route through Mewar meant control of the currency: "if you don't control the trade route you don't control your currency."
  • Modern version: if Infosys or TCS export earnings had to be locked up in silver coins, they couldn't pay for oil imports. Fiat money frees those real resources.
  • Zimbabwe: people hold $1 bn in notes and the government prints $1 tn. Old holdings now buy about 1/1000 of what they did. Printing is stealing from note-holders.
  • Trust: people hold dollars, not Zimbabwean, Sri Lankan or Pakistani notes, because they trust the US not to over-print. The US left the gold link so it could print more. "Why is the dollar powerful? Basically, trust." (S16)

Language traps the professor policed (S15):

  • Say "money available for transactions", never that society gains "purchasing power".
  • The 200 in the vault can't buy toothpaste, so reserves are not part of the money supply.
  • Don't use more UPI "to get more money". More money does not create more real goods. With the same output, more money just means higher prices; helicopter money ends in inflation.

Banks, bonds and the government (S16):

  • Tata Motors raises money two ways. Through your SIP, you get a paper claim you can't spend on toothpaste. Through a bank deposit, Tata buys steel and you can still spend your deposit: both parties transact.

OPEN ECONOMY · FOUR DISTINCT TERMS

≈ 6% each pre-1991 → ≈ 12% in the first decade after → 20–22% now

Record of all transactions with the rest of the world.

trade balance
Part of the CA: X − M of goods and services only.
capital account
One leg now, counter-leg later: borrowing, lending, asset purchases (FCNR deposits, FDI, FAANG stocks, GoI bonds).

Policy in an open economy: fixed vs flexible, interest parity, the impossible trinity

When capital moves freely, the exchange-rate regime decides which policy lever reaches output, and every interest-rate gap is expected depreciation (or risk) in disguise.

SAVING, INVESTMENT, NX (S19 RECAP)

NX = S − I
Small open economy: r = r* (pinned to the world rate). Investment is read off I(r) at r*.
fiscal deficit ↑
S ↓ → i pressure ↑ → capital inflow → ₹ appreciates (50 → lower) → I > S → trade deficit, financed by the inflow.
fiscal discipline
S ↑ → i pressure ↓ → capital outflow → ₹ depreciates (50 → 60) → S > I → trade surplus. The country lends abroad.
recession remedies
Recession = unused capacity = deficient AD. Raise C, I, G or NX: cut rates, let the currency depreciate, raise G, build confidence. Money is not the only tool.

POLICY WITH FREE CAPITAL MOBILITY (S19)

fixed · monetary
Ineffective. Arbitrage forces i_d = i_f. The RBI's own dollar sales undo its bond purchase.
fixed · fiscal
Effective, because it is accommodated: the RBI buys the inflowing $ with ₹, so M rises automatically.
flexible · monetary
Effective via depreciation → NX ↑ → AD = C + I + G + NX ↑.
flexible · fiscal
Not covered in S19. The S18 loanable-funds case gives appreciation + CAD.
bonds ↔ yields
Buy bonds → price ↑, yield ↓. Sell bonds → price ↓, yield ↑.

WHY INTEREST RATES DIFFER (S19)

UIP, exact
1 + i = (1 + i*) · E^e / E
UIP, approx
i ≈ i* + (E^e − E)/E, i.e. i* + expected depreciation. With 50 → 55 and 2%: 12.2% exact, ≈ 12% approx.
source 1: inflation differential
P_D rises faster than P_F. To hold ε = E·P_F/P_D, E must rise. Investors expect that depreciation and demand a higher i.
source 2: risk premium
Default risk, political instability, an uncertain business environment, tax uncertainty.

IMPOSSIBLE TRINITY & THE EURO

  • You can have at most two of: fixed rate · independent monetary policy · free capital mobility.
  • Euro = a fixed rate with one ECB rate. Franc, mark and lira are gone. Italy or France in recession can neither cut its own rate nor devalue.
  • Devaluation gets around sticky nominal wages. Workers resist nominal pay cuts. Devaluation leaves local pay unchanged but cuts it in $ terms, so exports rise. Euro members gave this tool up.
  • China allows some movement, but it is a managed float, not a free float.

FIXED VS FLEXIBLE · BoP SIGNS

  • Fixed +: certainty; no conversion (Germany → France); no exchange risk on lending. It needs monetary discipline: with higher inflation you must eventually devalue.
  • Flexible +: independent monetary policy; adjustment happens through the exchange rate.
  • In the long run, rates follow inflation differentials. In the short run, capital flows also move them.
  • BoP = current account + capital account. RBI buys $ = BoP surplus. Pure float → BoP = 0. A reserve increase is recorded as a negative entry.

Flat, then steeper, because firms bid for the same workers. The ASR said "cheaper".

        

      

      

        
PHILLIPS CURVE AND EXPECTATIONS

        

          
Phillips curve
A.W. Phillips, UK data 1861–1913. Unemployment on the x-axis, nominal wage growth on the y-axis (later versions use inflation). Downward-sloping and convex: steep when the labour market is tight, flat when it is slack. A short-run trade-off.

          
wage hike = productivity + πᵉ
The wage-round rule. It keeps inflation going even at full employment.

          
height of the curve = πᵉ
A surprise moves you along the curve. A change in expectations shifts it.

          
NAIRU
The unemployment rate at which inflation neither accelerates nor decelerates (3%, 3%, 3%…).

          
inflation inertia (Solow)
We expect inflation simply because we have had it.

          
inflation ≠ price level
Inflation is the rate of change of P. Zero inflation means stable prices, not falling ones.

          
steady 3%
Pₜ = 1.03ᵗ · P₀, so P₂ = 1.0609 P₀. This needs money growth of about 3% (accommodation).

WHY WAGES AND PRICES STICK: 8 REASONS (S20)

        
    
          
  • Labour contracts: wages are fixed for 1, 3 or 5 years. In a slump firms can't cut pay, so they cut jobs.
    • 
          
  • Other nominal contracts: 10-year housing loans, deposits, 30–50-year bonds at fixed rates.
    • 
          
  • Misperception: a firm's own price is a clear signal; the general price level is a noisy one. Firms over-expand, and workers mistake a nominal raise for a real one.
    • 
          
  • Menu costs: reprinting costs money and signals something to customers. Prices change only if the cost rise persists.
    • 
          
  • Coordination failure: the first firm to raise prices loses customers.
    • 
          
  • Insider/outsider: unions bargain with employers; the unemployed are not at the table.
    • 
          
  • Hiring/firing costs: firms keep capacity unused rather than fire workers now and retrain new ones later.
    • 
          
  • Efficiency wages: above-market pay motivates workers, and the pool of unemployed raises the cost of shirking.
    • 
        

      

      

        
INFLATION: WHO PAYS, AND THE TARGETS

        
    
          
  • Anticipated (expect 3%, get 3%): real wages stay constant. Revising contracts and prices every year is still a cost.
    • 
          
  • Unanticipated, wages (3% raise, 10% inflation): 3 − 10 → ≈ −7% real. Fixed-contract workers lose; firms gain.
    • 
          
  • Unanticipated, loans: i = r + πᵉ = 3 + 3 = 6%. If inflation is 10%, the realised rate is ≈ −4%. Lenders lose to borrowers.
    • 
          
  • High inflation = uncertainty: at 25%, an error of ±5 is costly; at 3%, an error of 2–4% matters little.
    • 
          
  • Deflation is worse: sticky wages would have to fall; spending gets postponed; interest rates hit the zero lower bound.
    • 
          
  • Targets: RBI 4% ± 2% (a 2–6% band); US/Europe 2%. A healthy rate is about 2–3%.

Supply and unemployment in the professor's examples (S19):

      
    
        
  • Berozgari and mehngai (unemployment and inflation) are the two perpetual electoral issues. They trade off in the short run but not in the long run, and that is this whole module.
    • 
        
  • Pulses, coarse cereals or milk: when demand for one rises, that sector draws workers and capital from the others. When hotels, aviation, construction and housing all rise together, every firm bids for the same workers.
    • 
        
  • An RBI rate cut moves bond markets immediately. Cement demand follows with a lag, because construction takes time. Hiring and wages move last.
    • 
        
  • Samosas over french fries: shops close and open as tastes change (frictional). Europe's generous benefits let people search longer.
    • 
        
  • Shared mobility moves car-factory workers into driving; millets move farmers between crops (structural).

Why wages won't fall (S20):

      
    
        
  • "Bluffing" employer: workers suspect the recession talk is a ploy to raise profits. They refuse the cut, are laid off or quit, and discover the recession while job-hunting. Only then do they accept lower wages, and there is large-scale unemployment in the meantime.
    • 
        
  • Textile producer: it sees its own price rising and thinks demand for its cloth went up. It offers ₹10,000–11,000 to a worker who earlier would not work for ₹10,000. Later everyone sees that all prices rose: ₹10,000 buys less, so the next ask is ₹11,000 (≈10%) on average.
    • 
        
  • Restaurant menu: seasonal spikes in onion and tomato prices don't get the menu reprinted. Prices change only if the higher costs persist.
    • 
        
  • Insiders (unions) bargain; outsiders (the unemployed) would work for less but aren't at the table.
    • 
        
  • Efficiency wages: a fired shirker takes longer to find work, so the unemployed pool disciplines workers and cuts the HR spend on monitoring.
    • 
        
  • Coordination failure: no firm wants to move first. This is the same trap as the social dilemmas in m5.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Phillips curve and expectations (S20):

      
    
        
  • Politicians read the Phillips curve as a menu: pick low unemployment and accept high inflation, or the reverse.
    • 
        
  • "Expectations translate into reality." The worker asks for productivity + 4%. The employer saw its own prices rise 4% and agrees. Costs and prices then rise 4%, and next year the same 4% is asked for again. This is a self-fulfilling wage round at full employment.
    • 
        
  • In a boom, workers reject 4% and demand 6%. If the central bank accommodates, inflation rises to match.
    • 
        
  • "You can create demand by printing notes, but you cannot create supply." More money does not create more real goods (S15).
    • 
      

    


    

      
Disinflation and credibility (S20):

      
    
        
  • Volcker raised short rates to ≈14–15%. At 16–17% mortgage rates nobody buys houses, so housing jobs go, wage growth collapses and inflation falls.
    • 
        
  • When the Fed says "the labour market is tight", it means rates will rise. It will create unemployment without saying so.
    • 
        
  • Disinflation is a "cruel" trade-off that wastes a lot of output and jobs.
    • 
        
  • Seigniorage: a ₹2,000 note costs ₹5–10 to print, so the central bank makes ≈₹1,990 per note. That is why it might bluff.
    • 
        
  • Traffic rules: "drive on this side only" works if people believe it and comply. If the police drive on the wrong side, or announced fines are never levied, credibility collapses. Deliver what you announce and the losses are much lower.
    • 
      

    


    

      
Inflation, deflation and deficits (S20):

      
    
        
  • At 25% inflation, an error of ±5 (20% or 30%) is very costly. A band of "15% ± 5% would make life interesting."
    • 
        
  • Deflation: "build it tomorrow, and tomorrow never comes". Firms and consumers both postpone.
    • 
        
  • Annual MSP increases feed food inflation. Moderate inflation is part of India's "social bargain", which is why the target is about 4% rather than 2%.
    • 
        
  • A deficit borrowed at full employment pulls cement and steel away from private factories, so future generations inherit fewer factories.
    • 
        
  • Closing line: macro policy means stabilising output at potential, using the tools when the economy deviates on either side. At full employment, hold the status quo.

## 1. Overview

The session first recaps the components of aggregate demand: consumption, saving and investment, including inventory investment. It then builds the **Keynesian cross** diagram. The diagram uses a $45^\circ$ line to show how the economy reaches the equilibrium where output equals planned aggregate demand. The adjustment works through unplanned inventory accumulation or depletion.

Government spending, lump-sum taxes and transfers are then added to derive the **expenditure multiplier** $\frac{1}{1-c}$ in two ways: algebraically, and as the sum of an infinite geometric progression. The worked example is a ₹1,000 crore project with MPC $= 2/3$, which gives a total output change of ₹3,000 crore. The instructor stresses that the multiplier works only when the economy has **unemployed resources**. At full employment it only diverts resources and causes inflation.

The model is then extended to **proportional income taxes**, giving the smaller multiplier $\frac{1}{1-c(1-t)}$. The instructor argues that a smaller multiplier is often preferable because it makes the economy more stable. Taxes and transfers act as **automatic stabilizers**, illustrated with a ₹100 income example. The session ends by stressing that equilibrium is not the same as full employment, and that fiscal policy can raise aggregate demand to potential output $Y^*$.

## 2. Topics and Concepts Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap: consumption and its determinants
- Consumption is the **largest component** of aggregate demand.
- It depends mainly on **disposable income**: higher income induces more consumption.
- Other determinants:
  - **Future income prospects**.
  - **Social security / pensions.** If pensions exist, people save less on their own for retirement.
  - **Interest rate**.
  - **Age / life cycle.** People in mid-life tend to save more than the young or the old.
- In the short run, the analysis focuses only on income.
- **Consumption function** (linear):
$$C = \bar{C} + cY$$
  - $\bar{C}$ is **autonomous consumption**, which is independent of income.
  - $c$ is the **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)**: the extra consumption from one additional unit of income, with $0 < c < 1$.
- **Saving:**
$$S = Y - C$$
  Saving is income earned but not consumed.

### 2.2 Investment: fixed vs. inventory, planned vs. unplanned
- Saving is used for investment, which comes in two types.
  1. **Fixed investment**: factories, buildings and similar assets.
  2. **Inventory investment**.
- Inventory investment can be planned or unplanned.
  - **Planned:** car makers produce extra in June–August because they expect high festive-season sales (Diwali, Dussehra, Ganesh Puja). They build inventories and sell them when demand arrives.
  - **Unplanned:** firms produce in expectation of sales that do not materialise, so unsold stock piles up.
- The rule linking output and planned demand:

| Situation | Inventories | Firms' response |
|---|---|---|
| $Y > AD$ (output exceeds planned demand) | Unplanned inventory **accumulation** | **Cut** production |
| $Y < AD$ (demand exceeds output) | Unplanned inventory **depletion** (drawing down existing stock) | **Raise** production |
| $Y = AD$ | Unplanned inventory change $= 0$ | Equilibrium |

### 2.3 The Keynesian cross diagram
**Axes**
- Horizontal axis: income/output $Y$.
- Vertical axis: aggregate demand $AD$.

**The $45^\circ$ line (grey)**
- At any point on the line, the horizontal distance from the origin equals the vertical height.
- This follows from $\tan 45^\circ = 1$: in a right triangle with a $45^\circ$ angle, height equals base.
- So every point on the $45^\circ$ line satisfies **demand $=$ output**.
- The line lets you project output from the horizontal axis onto the vertical axis.

**The AD line (black)**
- The consumption line $C = \bar{C} + cY$ (magenta) is upward-sloping with slope $c < 1$.
- Planned investment $\bar{I}$ is **autonomous**: fixed in advance by firms and independent of income.
- The AD line, $AD = C + \bar{I}$, is therefore the consumption line **shifted up in parallel** by $\bar{I}$.
- Because its slope is less than 1, the AD line is flatter than the $45^\circ$ line and crosses it once.

**Lower panel**
- **Red line:** planned investment (horizontal, constant).
- **Green line:** saving, $S = Y - C$.

**Reading the graph to the left of the intersection**
- The black AD line lies **above** the grey $45^\circ$ line, so demand exceeds output.
- Inventories deplete, so firms **increase** production and the economy moves right.
- In the lower panel, saving is less than investment (red above green).

**Reading the graph to the right of the intersection**
- The AD line lies **below** the $45^\circ$ line, so output exceeds demand.
- Unplanned inventories accumulate, so firms **cut** production and the economy moves left.
- In the lower panel, saving is greater than investment (green above red).

**Equilibrium**
- Equilibrium is where the black and grey lines intersect: $Y = AD$, which is equivalent to $S = I$.
- At this point unplanned inventory change is zero.

### 2.4 Adding government (lump-sum taxes and transfers)
- **Disposable income:**
$$Y_D = Y - \overline{TA} + \overline{TR}$$
  - Taxes are paid to the government.
  - Transfers are received from it: e.g., PM-Kisan, pensions, Ladli-type schemes, unemployment benefits.
- **Consumption now depends on disposable income:**
$$C = \bar{C} + cY_D = \bar{C} + c(Y - \overline{TA} + \overline{TR})$$
- **Government spending $\bar{G}$ is autonomous.**
  - Salaries of doctors, teachers and soldiers are predetermined.
  - They do not depend on that year's income, since staff are not fired immediately.
- **Aggregate demand:**
$$AD = C + \bar{I} + \bar{G} = \underbrace{\bar{C} - c\overline{TA} + c\overline{TR} + \bar{I} + \bar{G}}_{\bar{A}} + cY$$
  So $AD = \bar{A} + cY$, where $\bar{A}$ collects every term independent of $Y$.
- The full expenditure identity was also referenced: $AD = C + I + G + X - M$.

### 2.5 Equilibrium income and the multiplier (equation method)
1. Set output equal to demand: $Y = \bar{A} + cY$.
2. Move $cY$ across: $Y - cY = \bar{A}$, so $Y(1-c) = \bar{A}$.
3. Solve:
$$Y^{*} = \frac{1}{1-c}\,\bar{A} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \Delta Y = \frac{1}{1-c}\,\Delta\bar{A}$$
- **Multiplier:**
$$\alpha = \frac{1}{1-c}$$
  It shows how much output changes for a one-unit change in autonomous demand, e.g., ₹1 of extra government spending.
- **Sign and size.** Since $0 < c < 1$, we have $0 < 1 - c < 1$, so $\frac{1}{1-c} > 1$.
  - Class example: if $c = 0.8$, then $1 - c = 0.2$ and the multiplier is $\frac{1}{0.2} = 5$.

### 2.6 How the multiplier works: intuition and GP method
**Story**
- During a recession, the government or a firm builds a **₹1,000 crore factory**. This is an increase in $I$ or $G$.
- Workers previously laid off are called back to supply cement and steel and to build the factory. They earn the ₹1,000 crore.
- They spend part of it on restaurant meals, shirts and holidays.
- Restaurants and textile firms, which had also laid off workers, rehire them. These workers earn income and spend part of it too, and the process continues.
- Final output rises by much more than the initial ₹1,000 crore.

**Worked example (MPC $c = 2/3$)**

| Round | Calculation | Spending (₹ crore) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1000$ | 1,000 |
| 2 | $1000 \times \frac{2}{3}$ | ≈ 666 |
| 3 | $1000 \times \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^2$ | ≈ 444 |
| 4 | $1000 \times \left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^3$ | ≈ 296 |
| … | … | … |

- Total change:
$$\Delta Y = 1000\left[1 + c + c^2 + c^3 + \cdots\right]$$
- This is an infinite **geometric progression** with common ratio $r = c = 2/3 < 1$.
- Sum of an infinite GP:
$$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}$$
- Applying it:
$$\Delta Y = 1000 \times \frac{1}{1 - \frac{2}{3}} = 1000 \times 3 = 3000 \text{ crore}$$
- **Both routes give the same multiplier** $\frac{1}{1-c}$:
  - the **equation route**: $Y = AD = \bar{A} + cY$;
  - the **GP route**: summing the spending rounds.

### 2.7 Crucial assumption: unemployed resources
- The multiplier works **only if the economy has unemployed resources** (idle labour and capacity), as in a recession.
- At **full employment** there is no spare output to absorb new demand.
  - Example: a new ₹1,000 crore highway is announced. Cement and steel firms have no extra output, so they supply the highway from existing production at **higher prices**.
  - Cement is diverted away from house construction, and house-construction workers become road workers.
  - The result is only **reallocation** between sectors. There is no net new demand, no multiplier effect, and only **inflation**.
- **Policy implication:** demand-boosting policy is useful in recessions, not at full employment.

### 2.8 Fiscal policy with proportional taxes
- Fiscal policy acts through two channels: government **spending** and **taxes/transfers**.
- Taxes are usually **proportional** rather than lump-sum: $TA = tY$.
- **Disposable income:**
$$Y_D = Y + \overline{TR} - tY$$
- **Consumption:**
$$C = \bar{C} + c\overline{TR} + c(1-t)Y$$
- **Aggregate demand:**
$$AD = \underbrace{\bar{C} + c\overline{TR} + \bar{I} + \bar{G}}_{\bar{A}} + c(1-t)Y$$
  - Note: this $\bar{A}$ **no longer contains** $-c\overline{TA}$, because taxes now enter through $t$.
- **Equilibrium:**
  1. Set $Y = \bar{A} + c(1-t)Y$.
  2. Rearrange to $Y\left[1 - c(1-t)\right] = \bar{A}$.
  3. Solve:
$$Y^{*} = \frac{1}{1-c(1-t)}\,\bar{A}, \qquad \Delta Y = \frac{1}{1-c(1-t)}\,\Delta\bar{A}$$
- **Multiplier with proportional taxes:**
$$\alpha_G = \frac{1}{1-c(1-t)}$$

### 2.9 In-class poll 1: which multiplier is bigger? (counted for attendance)
- Question: is $\frac{1}{1-c}$ larger or smaller than $\frac{1}{1-c(1-t)}$, with $t = 20\%$?
- **Answer: $\frac{1}{1-c}$ is bigger.**
- Reasoning:
  1. Since $t > 0$, we have $1 - t < 1$.
  2. So $c(1-t) < c$; multiplying $c$ by a fraction such as 0.9 or 0.8 always gives less than $c$.
  3. Therefore $1 - c(1-t) > 1 - c$, which makes the proportional-tax multiplier smaller.
- *Illustration (not computed in class):* with $c = 0.8$ and $t = 0.2$,
  - $c(1-t) = 0.64$, so $\alpha_G = \frac{1}{0.36} \approx 2.78$;
  - compare $\frac{1}{1-0.8} = 5$ without proportional taxes.

### 2.10 In-class poll 2: is a larger or smaller multiplier better?
**Student answers**
- Most said "larger": a small spending increase gives a big GDP and employment boost in a recession.
- One student said "depends": at full capacity, a large multiplier would trigger demand-pull inflation.

**Instructor's view: he generally prefers a smaller multiplier**
- The multiplier also works **in reverse**. Suppose investment falls because a firm relocates abroad, business uncertainty rises, or a project is judged unviable.
- Laid-off workers then cut consumption, which causes further layoffs, round after round.
- A smaller multiplier dampens these downturns, so the economy is more stable.

**Worked example (income ₹100, MPC $= 0.7$)**

| Scenario | Employed: consumption | Unemployed: consumption | Drop in demand when job lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| No taxes or transfers | $100 \times 0.7 = 70$ | $0$ | **70** |
| 30% income tax plus ₹30 unemployment benefit | Disposable income $= 100 - 30 = 70$; consumption $= 70 \times 0.7 = 49$ | Benefit $= 30$; consumption $= 30 \times 0.7 = 21$ | $49 - 21 =$ **28** |

- Without taxes and transfers, losing a job removes the full 70 of demand. This hits suppliers such as shirt-makers and restaurants, who also lose jobs.
- With the tax-and-transfer system, the second-round loss is only 28, so suppliers are hit far less.
- The ₹30 collected in good times is not wasted. It pays teachers and doctors, so demand is maintained either way.
- In effect, this is **macro insurance**: the government takes from you in good times and gives to you in bad times.

### 2.11 Automatic stabilizers
- **Definition:** income taxes and transfers (unemployment benefits, NREGA) that automatically dampen fluctuations in demand.
  - **Boom:** income rises, but disposable income rises less than proportionately because taxes take a share, so demand rises less.
  - **Bust:** disposable income falls less than proportionately because of lower taxes plus transfers, so demand falls less.
- **Why "automatic":** they avoid **political and intellectual delays**.
  - **Discretionary policy** is slow. Data must be collected, a project designed, parliamentary approval obtained, and regional disputes settled ("build the highway in my region").
  - **Stabilizers** need no case-by-case approval. People who lose jobs qualify for benefits or NREGA work automatically and keep spending.
- **Historical point:** after the Great Depression of the 1930s, the introduction of personal income tax and transfer systems made business cycles much less volatile. No comparable downturn has occurred since.

### 2.12 Equilibrium ≠ full employment; fiscal policy to reach $Y^*$
**Graph**
- Same axes as before ($Y$ horizontal, $AD$ vertical) with the $45^\circ$ line.
- A vertical line at $Y^*$ marks **potential (full-employment) output**: what the economy can produce using all labour and capital.
- The initial equilibrium is at $E_1$ with output $Y_1 < Y^*$.

**Key point (repeated by the instructor)**
> Equilibrium only means **whatever is produced is sold**. It does **not** mean **whatever can be produced is produced**.

- At $E_1$, output equals demand, but both are below the full-employment level.

**Policy**
1. The government raises $G$, for example by building a highway, and the AD line **shifts up**.
2. At the old $Y_1$, demand (up to the new AD line) now exceeds output (up to the $45^\circ$ line).
3. Inventories deplete, so firms expand production.
4. Newly paid workers spend, triggering further multiplier rounds.
5. The economy moves to a new, higher equilibrium. With enough stimulus it reaches $Y^*$.

## 3. Formula Summary

| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Consumption function | $C = \bar{C} + cY$ |
| Saving | $S = Y - C$ |
| Disposable income (lump-sum) | $Y_D = Y - \overline{TA} + \overline{TR}$ |
| Autonomous demand (lump-sum) | $\bar{A} = \bar{C} - c\overline{TA} + c\overline{TR} + \bar{I} + \bar{G}$ |
| Aggregate demand | $AD = \bar{A} + cY$ |
| Equilibrium condition | $Y = AD$ (equivalently $S = I$) |
| Equilibrium income | $Y^{*} = \frac{\bar{A}}{1-c}$ |
| Simple multiplier | $\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta \bar{A}} = \frac{1}{1-c}$ |
| Infinite GP sum | $S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}$, valid for $\lvert r\rvert < 1$ |
| Disposable income (proportional) | $Y_D = Y + \overline{TR} - tY$ |
| Autonomous demand (proportional) | $\bar{A} = \bar{C} + c\overline{TR} + \bar{I} + \bar{G}$ |
| AD (proportional) | $AD = \bar{A} + c(1-t)Y$ |
| Multiplier (proportional) | $\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta\bar{A}} = \frac{1}{1-c(1-t)} < \frac{1}{1-c}$ |

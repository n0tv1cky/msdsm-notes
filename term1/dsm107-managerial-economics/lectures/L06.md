# DSM-107 Managerial Economics — Session L06
## Constrained Choice: MRS, MRT, Equilibrium, and Income vs. Substitution Effects

---

## 1. Overview

This session completes the "work–leisure" constrained-choice model begun in the previous class. It starts by revisiting the feasible set / feasible frontier for Mr. Karim (consumption spending vs. hours of free time), clarifying that **opportunity cost is the slope of the feasible frontier**, and that this same slope — read the other way round (hours of work → income) — is the **Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT)**. The lecture then superimposes Karim's **indifference map** on his budget set and derives the optimum at the **tangency point** where **MRS = MRT**, giving the numerical solution of 17 hours free time / 7 hours work / €210 consumption. A point-by-point table (B, D, E, A) is used to give the *economic* (not merely geometric) logic: MRS is the price you are **willing** to pay for an extra hour of free time; MRT is the price you are **required** to pay. The second half analyses what happens when the wage (and hence the opportunity cost of free time) changes, and decomposes the total change into **income effect** (parallel shift of the budget line) and **substitution effect** (change in slope), using a new worked example of a student on a 70-day summer break. It closes with Keynes's failed 1930 prediction of a 15-hour working week as an application: the substitution effect dominating explains why we did not all take the gains from higher wages as leisure. Housekeeping on the upcoming quiz is given at the end.

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order of Teaching

### 2.1 Recap — Feasible set and feasible frontier (Karim)

- **Setup:** Karim allocates 24 hours between **free time (t)** and **hours of work**. Two goods he cares about: **consumption spending (C)** and **free time (t)**.
- **Feasible frontier equation:**

  **C = w × (24 − t)**

  where w = hourly wage (= €30 in the base example), t = hours of free time, (24 − t) = hours of work.
- **Graph description (main graph):**
  - x-axis: **hours of free time (t)**, 0 → 24
  - y-axis: **consumption spending, C (€)**
  - The **red line** is the **feasible frontier** (downward sloping, straight, slope = −30).
  - The shaded triangle under it is the **feasible set**.
  - A point **C** lying outside the triangle is **not affordable**.
- **Terminology equivalences (stated explicitly):**
  - Feasible frontier = **budget constraint** = budget line
  - Feasible set = **budget set**

### 2.2 Q&A clarification — Is opportunity cost always the slope?

- **Answer:** Yes, provided you define opportunity cost in terms of *one extra unit of the good on the x-axis*.
  - Opportunity cost of **1 extra hour of free time** = the **slope** of the frontier (here €30).
  - Opportunity cost of **1 extra € earned** = the **inverse of the slope**.
- This generalises to any frontier graph (e.g. the "grade vs. hours of study" example from the emailed question set): the opportunity cost is the slope of that boundary/frontier.
- **Important caveat:** the slope need **not** be constant — the frontier may be a **curve**, in which case opportunity cost varies along it.

### 2.3 The two mirror-image graphs

- **Income–hours-of-work line:**
  - x-axis: **hours of work per day**; y-axis: **income**
  - **Upward (positive) sloping**, slope = hourly wage = €30
- **Consumption–free-time line:**
  - x-axis transformed to **free time = 24 − hours of work**
  - **Downward (negative) sloping**, slope magnitude still €30
- **Key point:** the two graphs are **mirror images**; only the x-axis definition differs. **The slope magnitude (the wage) is identical in both.**
- Interpretation: "If free time increases by 1 hour, maximum possible consumption falls by €30." → slope = 30 = opportunity cost of free time.

### 2.4 Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT)

- The income/hours-of-work line is a **transformation curve** (a production/income function): it transforms hours available for work into income.
  - 1 hour of work → €30
  - 2 hours → €60
  - 4 hours → €120
- **MRT = slope of the transformation curve** = the amount of consumption gained per extra hour of work.
- **MRT = opportunity cost** — they are the *same magnitude/concept*, only the axis convention differs:
  - Define x-axis as **free time** → call the slope **opportunity cost**
  - Define x-axis as **hours of work** → call the slope **MRT**
- In this example **MRT = 30, constant** (straight line). It need not be constant in general.
- Constraint noted in the example: maximum hours of work = 16, so minimum free time = 8.

### 2.5 Decision-making under scarcity — the two trade-offs

| | Concept | Slope of | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade-off 1 | **MRT** (= opportunity cost) | **Feasible frontier / budget line** | The trade-off Karim is **constrained to make** (set by the market/employer) |
| Trade-off 2 | **MRS** (Marginal Rate of Substitution) | **Indifference curve** | The trade-off Karim is **willing to make** (his preferences) |

### 2.6 The optimum — juxtaposing indifference map and budget set

- **Graph:** same axes (x = hours of free time; y = consumption spending €). Four indifference curves **IC1, IC2, IC3, IC4** drawn, plus the red feasible frontier.
- Utility ranking: **IC4 > IC3 > IC2 > IC1**.
- Karim would *like* IC4, but **IC4 lies entirely outside the feasible set** → not feasible.
- **Objective:** reach the **highest indifference curve attainable given the feasible set**.
- **Iterative logic taught:**
  1. A rational person will be **on the frontier**, not inside it (interior points are suboptimal).
  2. Take two frontier points **A** and **B**, both on IC1.
  3. Any point **between A and B** on the frontier (e.g. **D**, on IC2) gives higher satisfaction.
  4. Any point lying **between the current indifference curve and the frontier** is both feasible and an improvement → keep moving.
  5. This converges to the **point of tangency, E**.
- **At E:** slope of indifference curve (**MRS**) = slope of feasible frontier (**MRT / opportunity cost**).
- Beyond E → infeasible. Inside/short of E → suboptimal (lower satisfaction).
- E is *not* "maximum possible consumption" but the **most satisfying feasible bundle**.
- **Numerical solution at E:** 17 hours free time → 7 hours of work → 7 × €30 = **€210 consumption**.
- This is a **constrained optimisation**: maximise satisfaction subject to the feasible set.

### 2.7 Worked table — the economic logic behind E

All four points lie on the **feasible frontier**, so **MRT = 30 at every point** (constant slope of a straight line). MRS varies (falls as you move right — the diminishing-MRS property from the previous session).

| Point | Free time (hrs) | Consumption (€) | MRT (required to pay) | MRS (willing to pay) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **B** | 9.5 | 435 | 30 | **223** | MRS > MRT → free time is "cheap" → **take more free time** → move right |
| **D** | 12 | — | 30 | **105** | MRS > MRT → still cheap → **move further right** |
| **E** | **17** | **210** | 30 | **30** | **MRS = MRT → EQUILIBRIUM** |
| **A** | 21.9 | — | 30 | (< 30) | MRS < MRT → free time too expensive → **cut back, move left to E** |

**The intuition (emphasised heavily):**
- **MRT = the price Karim is *required / constrained* to pay** for one extra hour of free time — set by the market/employer, not by him.
- **MRS = the price Karim is *willing* to pay** for one extra hour of free time (how much consumption he'd give up and stay equally satisfied).
- At B: willing to pay €223 but only required to pay €30 → free time is a **bargain** → buy more of it.
- At A: required to pay €30 but only willing to pay less than that → **too expensive** → buy less of it.
- At E: willingness to pay exactly equals the required price → no incentive to move → **equilibrium**.

### 2.8 Q&A — Is there a mathematical function behind indifference curves?

- Yes. With two goods X and Y, write **Z = f(X, Y)** where Z = satisfaction/utility.
- Hold **Z constant** and find all combinations of X and Y giving that constant Z → that set of points **is one indifference curve**.
- Varying Z traces out different curves: it is effectively a **3-dimensional surface** (1st and 2nd dimensions = the two goods; 3rd dimension = satisfaction), and indifference curves are its **contours** projected into 2-D.

### 2.9 Definition — constrained choice problem

> **A constrained choice problem is a problem in which a decision maker chooses the value of one or more variables to achieve some objective, subject to a constraint.**

- Here: chooses **free time** and **consumption of goods (in €)**; objective = **be on as high an indifference curve as possible** (maximise satisfaction); constraint = **budget set / feasible set**.
- Both goods are **scarce**: more of one good thing means less of the other (they have an opportunity cost) — this is what makes it an optimisation problem.

### 2.10 Extension to firms (forward reference)

- Firms face the same generic structure: they **maximise profit** subject to a constraint.
- Their constraint is the **demand curve**: people will not buy whatever quantity at whatever price the firm wishes.
- Tension: firms want **both high quantity sold and high price**; consumers buy more only when price is **low**.
- This will be covered in **Module / Chapter 7**. Point of the exercise: the same generic constrained-optimisation model applies to consumers *and* firms.

### 2.11 Change in the wage — living standards

- Over time, technical progress raises wages — but only with a **lag**, and only to the extent that workers had the **bargaining power** to force firms to share higher profits. Rise in GDP ≠ automatic rise in worker wages.
- **Living standards ≠ wages alone.** Living standards = the combination of **income AND free time** → measured by **which indifference curve you attain**. (Stylised fact recalled from the first session: on average we have *more* free time than 2–3 centuries ago, *and* higher income.)
- **Worked change: wage rises from €30 → €45 per hour.**
  - Equation: **C = w × (24 − t)**; only **w** changes.
  - **x-intercept does not change** (24 hours of free time still gives zero consumption).
  - **y-intercept rises** (24 × 45 instead of 24 × 30).
  - → the budget line **PIVOTS outwards around the x-intercept**; the feasible set expands.
  - New optimum: Karim moves from **E** to a new tangency point **F**, consuming **more free time AND more consumption**.
- **Important qualification:** this is only *one* possible outcome. Depending on preferences (shape of the indifference curves), a person might instead:
  - keep free time constant and take all the gain as extra consumption; or
  - keep consumption constant (€210 now needs only ~5 hours of work at €45/hr instead of 7) and take the gain as extra free time; or
  - any mix in between.
- **What IS certain for everyone:** the person ends up on a **higher indifference curve** → living standards rise.

### 2.12 Income effect and substitution effect

**Income effect**
- **Definition:** the effect that an increase in income has on an individual's demand for a good.
- Graphically: the budget constraint shifts **outwards in a parallel fashion** — **slope unchanged**, **relative prices unchanged**; combinations previously unaffordable become affordable.
- Sign: **positive or zero** for a **normal good** (you consume the same or more as income rises).
- **Inferior good:** a good whose consumption **falls** as income rises (e.g. a very low-quality food/bread consumed only because it is cheap). Noted as rare and **not a focus of this course**.
- For a wage rise, the income effect makes Karim want **more free time**.

**Substitution effect**
- **Definition:** when the **relative price** of a good changes (holding the income aspect aside), consumers shift consumption towards whatever has become relatively **cheaper**.
- A wage rise **raises the opportunity cost (price) of free time** → gives greater incentive to **work more** → tips the balance towards **consumption, away from free time**.
- Described informally as a "fear of missing out": every extra hour of leisure now costs more.
- Real-world illustration: as you are **promoted**, income rises *and* the opportunity cost of your free time rises (more responsibility) → you typically end up with **less** free time.

**Net result:** the two effects work in **opposite directions** on free time. Whichever **dominates** determines whether free time rises or falls on balance.

**Rule of thumb taught for reading graphs:**
- **Parallel shift (slope unchanged) → pure INCOME effect.**
- **Change in slope → SUBSTITUTION effect comes into play.**

### 2.13 Worked example 2 — The student on summer break

**Setup**
- 10-week / **70-day** summer break, split between working in a local shop and leisure with friends/family.
- Wage = **$90 per day**.
- Two goods: **free time (D, in days)** and **consumption ($)**.
- **Feasible frontier:** **C = 90 × (70 − D)**
  - x-intercept: **D = 70 days** (all leisure, zero consumption)
  - y-intercept: **70 × 90 = $6,300** (all work)
  - Note: same structure as Karim's equation; only the notation (t → D), the wage (hourly → daily), and the total time endowment (24 → 70) change.
- **Graph:** x-axis = days of free time (0–70); y-axis = consumption ($); red line = feasible frontier; blue curves = indifference curves.
- Initial optimum: bundle **A** (tangency with the highest attainable IC).

**Shock 1 — the "mysterious benefactor" / scam email: a gift of $1,000**
- Money arrives **independently of hours worked** → adds to consumption at every level of free time.
- New constraint: **C = 90 × (70 − D) + 1,000**
- Graphically: **parallel outward shift** (dashed line). **Slope (= daily wage, $90) unchanged; only the intercepts change.**
- → This is a **PURE INCOME EFFECT** (relative prices unchanged).
- **Two possible responses shown on two slides:**
  - **Left slide:** she increases **both** free time and consumption.
  - **Right slide:** she increases **only consumption**; free time unchanged.
  - (A third possibility discussed verbally: keep consumption constant and take all the gain as free time.)
  - Which happens depends **entirely on the shape of her indifference curves**.

**Digression — why do people's indifference curves differ in steepness?**
- Slope of IC = **MRS** = how much consumption a person will give up for one extra unit of free time.
- **Steeper IC** → willing to give up a lot of consumption for free time (values free time highly).
- **Flatter IC** → less willing to give up consumption (needs the money).
- Example: a student who must **pay rent** during the break is desperate for income → flatter IC → takes the $1,000 as consumption. A student **staying at home rent-free** doesn't need the income → steeper IC → converts the windfall into free time.
- Bottom line: **the indifference curve encodes tastes/preferences.**

**Shock 2 — the email was a hoax; but a new job pays $40 per day more**
- Now the **slope changes** (daily wage rises by $40/day), while the "gift" intercept addition disappears.
- The new frontier is steeper; the **x-intercept (70 days) is unchanged**; the **y-intercept rises**.
- She moves from **A** to **D**.
- **Result at D: LESS free time, MORE consumption** than at A. (Seemingly paradoxical.)
- **Class question — is she worse off at D because she has less free time?**
  - **No.** She has moved from **IC2 to IC4** → higher satisfaction → **higher living standards**.
  - She has **willingly chosen** to work more; the satisfaction gain comes through higher consumption.
  - General lesson: *people can reach higher living standards by working harder* (cf. wanting a promotion even though it means more work).
  - Again, this is preference-dependent: for some people D lies **north-east** of A (more of both); for others **north-west** (less free time, more consumption).

**Decomposition of A → D into the two effects (the key diagram)**
- Introduce an **imaginary intermediate point C**.
- **C lies on a dashed line that is PARALLEL to the original line through A**, and is tangent to an indifference curve.
- **A → C = INCOME EFFECT** (parallel shift, slope/relative prices unchanged → free time increases, moves **rightwards**).
- **C → D = SUBSTITUTION EFFECT** (the trade-off/slope has now changed to the new steeper line; free time has become expensive → cut back, move **leftwards**).
- **Total effect A → D = income effect + substitution effect.**
- In this diagram the **C→D leftward movement is larger than the A→C rightward movement**, so **the substitution effect dominates** and net free time **falls**.
- **Numbers given when a student asked how we "see" the dominance** (measured on the **x-axis intercepts / horizontal distances**, not diagonal distance):
  - A → C: free time rises from roughly **33 days to about 45** (rightward)
  - C → D: free time falls from about **45 back to 30** (leftward)
  - → net leftward movement of about **3 units** more → substitution effect > income effect.
- Note: in reality we only observe **A** and **D** (her choice in the old shop and in the new superstore); **C is a purely analytical construct** used to separate the two effects.

### 2.14 Application — Keynes's failed prediction

- **John Maynard Keynes, 1930 essay:** predicted that 100 years later (≈2030) people would work only **15 hours per week**, because **wages would rise eight-fold**. He even worried people would not find enough ways to fill their leisure.
- **Reality (~96 years later):** world average is still around **40 hours/week**; countries like **India** are around **50 hours/week**.
- **Explanation using the model:** an eight-fold rise in wages raises income (income effect → more leisure) **but it equally raises the opportunity cost/price of leisure** (substitution effect → less leisure). Leisure became **very expensive**, so people did not "buy" much more of it — they kept consuming more instead.
- Clarification: this does **not** mean we work *more* than 100 years ago — we do work less — but we have **not cut hours anything like as much as Keynes predicted**.

### 2.15 Q&A — Must the budget line always be tangential to an indifference curve?

- For the **bold (actual) budget lines**: yes, in the general case. We maximise utility subject to the constraint by finding the furthest-out indifference curve still touching the feasible set → that contact is a **tangency**.
- **Why only one point:** if the indifference curve **crossed** the frontier at more than one point, you would not be exploiting the feasible set fully — a **higher** indifference curve would still be attainable.
- **Exception:** if the indifference curve is itself a **straight line** that coincides with the budget line, the whole segment is optimal → **multiple optimal bundles**, not a unique one.

## 3. Formulas to Memorise

- **Karim:** `C = w × (24 − t)`, with w = €30 (then €45); t = hours of free time.
- **Student:** `C = 90 × (70 − D)`; with windfall: `C = 90 × (70 − D) + 1,000`; D = days of free time.
- **Opportunity cost of free time = |slope of feasible frontier| = wage (w).**
- **MRT = slope of the transformation (income/hours-of-work) curve = w.**
- **MRS = |slope of the indifference curve| = amount of consumption willingly sacrificed for one extra unit of free time.**
- **Optimum condition: MRS = MRT (= opportunity cost).** Geometrically: tangency of the highest attainable IC with the budget constraint.
- **Total effect = Income effect + Substitution effect.**
- Utility function form: `Z = f(X, Y)`; an indifference curve is the locus of (X, Y) with **Z held constant**.

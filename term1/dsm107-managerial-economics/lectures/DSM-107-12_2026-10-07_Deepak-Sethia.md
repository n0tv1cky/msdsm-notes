# DSM-107 Managerial Economics, L12: Measuring GDP (Inventories, Expenditure Components, Factor Cost vs Market Price, NDP, GNP, Savings–Investment Identity, Nominal vs Real GDP)

## 1. Overview

This session continues national income accounting. It finishes the wheat–bread example from the previous class, covering self-consumption, unsold inventory and perishable unsold output, and shows how losses keep the income and expenditure approaches equal. It then explains each expenditure component of $Y = C + I + G + X - M$:

- imputed rent in consumption
- planned vs unplanned inventories in investment
- cost-based valuation of government services
- why imports are subtracted, using iPhone and Tata Nano examples

It works through the three equivalent approaches to GDP (production/value-added, income, expenditure) with a wheat → atta → dough → pizza chain, and introduces GDP at factor cost vs market prices, including indirect taxes and subsidies. It then covers GDP vs NDP and why macroeconomics focuses on GDP, GDP vs GNP based on residency (remittances are not factor income), the savings–investment identity and India's twin deficits leading to the 1991 crisis, what is excluded from GDP, and finally nominal vs real GDP. Real GDP continues next session.

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap: GDP with self-consumption (wheat–bread example)

**Setup:**
- Total wheat production is 50 kg at ₹15/kg.
- 30 kg is sold to the baker.
- 20 kg is retained by the farmer for self-consumption.
- The baker converts 30 kg of wheat into 30 kg of bread, adding ₹5/kg of value. Bread sells at ₹20/kg.

**Production approach:**
- Wheat: $50 \times 15 = 750$
- Bread value added: $30 \times 5 = 150$
- GDP $= 750 + 150 = ₹900$

**Expenditure approach (final consumption only):**
- Farmer's self-consumed wheat: $20 \times 15 = 300$
- Bread sold to consumers: $30 \times 20 = 600$
- GDP $= 300 + 600 = ₹900$

Self-consumed output is counted in GDP.

### 2.2 Unsold inventory (non-perishable bread)

Suppose some bread (10 kg) is unsold at year-end. The ₹5 value addition per kg splits into two parts:
- ₹3 for converting wheat to bread (done this year)
- ₹2 for marketing and sales (not yet done; it happens next year)

Treatment:
- Unsold bread is valued as inventory at $15 + 3 = ₹18$/kg, not ₹20. The firm books inventory at ₹18 because the ₹2 activity has not happened yet.
- This year, farmers and conversion workers have been paid. Sales and marketing staff have not.
- **Rule:** activity done this year counts in this year's GDP. Wheat production and conversion to bread enter this year's GDP as inventory investment. The sales/marketing ₹2 counts in next year's GDP.
- Implied total (not stated explicitly in class): $300 + 20\times 20 + 10 \times 18 = 300 + 400 + 180 = ₹880$.

### 2.3 Perishable unsold output

Everything is the same as above, but the bread is perishable, so the 10 kg of unsold bread perishes.

**Expenditure side:**
- Wheat: $20 \times 15 = 300$
- Bread: $20 \times 20 = 400$
- GDP $= ₹700$. The 10 kg perished and was never consumed, so it is not counted.

**The puzzle:** Farmers and bakery workers were paid for producing that 10 kg, so the income side seems to show income without matching output.

**Resolution (student answer, confirmed by lecturer):** use double-entry logic. Per kg of perished bread:
- Farmer receives +15
- Bakery workers receive +3
- The baker (entrepreneur) earns a negative profit of −18

$$15 + 3 - 18 = 0$$

**Rule:** all incomes, positive or negative, count in GDP. Worthless or perished items do not appear on the expenditure side, and an equal loss to the business owner appears on the income side. Both approaches still match.

### 2.4 Uses of output and the three approaches

$$Y = C + I + G + X - M$$

Output must be consumed, invested, used by government, exported, or (subtracted) imported.

**Three approaches:**
1. **Expenditure approach:** sum of final demand.
2. **Income approach:** incomes received by factors (wages, profits, rent, interest).
3. **Production approach:** value of output minus value of inputs, summed across the economy (gross value added).

**Circular flow diagram (from last session):**
- Red lines show final demand ($C, I, G, X - M$).
- Blue lines show factor payments.
- Black lines show transfers.
- Total final demand (red) equals total factor payments (blue).

### 2.5 Consumption (C)

- Consumption can be classified as durable/non-durable or food/non-food.
- Building a house is investment. Living in a house is consumption.
- **Imputed rent:**
  - A tenant consumes housing services and pays rent, which counts in GDP.
  - An owner-occupier is treated as renting the house to themselves. The imputed rent is counted in GDP.
  - This is an exception for a service, analogous to counting farmers' self-consumed grain.
- **Tax analogy:** if you own a third house, tax authorities deem rental income on it even if it is not let out. Earlier the threshold was the second house; now two houses are exempt.

### 2.6 Investment (I)

There are two types of investment.

**1. Fixed investment:** factories, machines and similar assets.

**2. Inventories:**
- **Planned:** e.g., Maruti produces extra cars in April–July, knowing they won't sell then, to meet higher sales from the festive season (around Ganesh Chaturthi) onward.
- **Unplanned:** cars produced but not sold.
- Both count as investment, since accumulated stock is future consumption.

The same item can be consumption or investment depending on its use:
- A car bought for the household is consumption. The same car bought for a business is investment.
- A laptop used for a firm is investment. A laptop bought for home use is consumption.

### 2.7 Government spending (G)

- Government services are not sold at meaningful market prices. For example, college education may cost ₹100 or ₹5,000 a year, which cannot fund professors' salaries. A surgery might cost ₹1 lakh privately but ₹3,000–5,000 in a government hospital.
- **Rule:** value government services at their **cost of production**.
- Examples include education, healthcare, defence (soldiers) and police/law and order. Contrast this with private security guards, whose price is observed in the market.

### 2.8 Exports and imports: why $+X$ and $-M$

**iPhone example (imports):**
- Imported at ₹70,000. The seller's margin is ₹10,000, so it is sold at ₹80,000 for personal use.
- $C = 80{,}000$, but only ₹10,000 of output and income is generated in India (seller's wages and profits).
- The ₹70,000 goes to foreign workers and firms.
- To reconcile, $M = 70{,}000$:

$$Y = 80{,}000 - 70{,}000 = 10{,}000$$

- Domestic activity (sales, marketing, seller's margin) is counted in India. Foreign manufacturing is not.

**Tata Nano example (exports):**
- A car worth ₹1 lakh is produced in India and exported to South Africa.
- $C = I = G = 0$, yet domestic wages and profits of ₹1 lakh are generated.
- This shows up as $X = 1$ lakh.

**Logic:**
- When foreigners buy Indian goods, we earn income without domestic $C$, $I$ or $G$, so we add $X$.
- When we import, we spend through $C$, $I$ or $G$ without earning domestic income, so we subtract $M$.

### 2.9 Value-added chain: equivalence of the three approaches

| Stage | Sales value (₹) | Intermediate input (₹) | Value added (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer (wheat) | 23 | 0 | 23 |
| Atta (flour) mill | 53 | 23 | 30 |
| Dough maker | 110 | 53 | 57 |
| Pizza maker / pizzeria | 200 | 110 | 90 |
| **Total** | | | **200** |

- **Final product approach:** value of the pizza is 200.
- **Production (value-added) approach:**

$$\sum (\text{sales} - \text{intermediate inputs}) = 23 + 30 + 57 + 90 = 200$$

- **Income approach:** each stage's value added is distributed as wages and profits. Summing these also gives 200.

**Adding indirect tax:**
- With 5% GST, the consumer pays $200 \times 1.05 = 210$.
- Factor owners still receive only 200; the ₹10 goes to government.

$$\text{GDP at market prices} = \text{GDP at factor cost} + \text{indirect taxes} - \text{subsidies}$$

Summary of the three approaches:
- **Expenditure:** $C + I + G + NX$
- **Production:** $\sum$ GVA (output minus intermediate consumption) plus net production taxes
- **Income:** $\sum$ wages, profits, etc. plus net indirect taxes

All three give the same GDP.

### 2.10 Worked example: GDP at factor cost vs market prices (mobile phones)

**Data:**
- Imported inputs (parts and components): ₹6,000
- Salaries for assembly: ₹3,000
- Operating profit: ₹1,000
- Price at factor cost: $6{,}000 + 3{,}000 + 1{,}000 = ₹10{,}000$
- Indirect taxes/duty: ₹2,000. Subsidy (e.g., Make in India): ₹500.
- Market price: $10{,}000 + 2{,}000 - 500 = ₹11{,}500$

**GDP at factor cost (income earned in India):**

$$3{,}000 + 1{,}000 = 4{,}000$$

**Expenditure approach:**

$$Y = C - M = 11{,}500 - 6{,}000 = 5{,}500$$

**Income approach at market prices:**

$$4{,}000 + 2{,}000 - 500 = 5{,}500$$

Both approaches give ₹5,500. Indirect taxes are added and subsidies deducted.

### 2.11 Gross vs Net: NDP

$$\text{NDP} = Y - \text{Depreciation}$$

- Depreciation is technically called **consumption of fixed capital**. It covers physical wear and tear and **obsolescence**. For example, a 5-year-old laptop still works but has depreciated because it is obsolete.

$$\text{Net investment} = I - \text{Depreciation}$$

**Example:**
- Gross investment is ₹1,500 crore, of which ₹600 crore replaces depreciated capital.

$$\text{Net investment} = 1{,}500 - 600 = ₹900 \text{ crore}$$

**Why we focus on GDP, not NDP:**
- For **growth**, which concerns creating new capacity and supply, net additions to capacity (₹900 cr) matter.
- For **macroeconomics**, which concerns the short run, demand, and use of existing capacity, all ₹1,500 cr of output creates jobs this year, whether it replaces old capital or adds new capital.
- Employment depends on how much is produced this year, not where it is used.
- So GDP is the relevant measure for this course.

### 2.12 GDP vs GNP

- **GDP** counts output produced within the geographic boundary of a nation, i.e., the location of production.
- **GNP** is based on the **resident status of the owner of the factor of production**.

**Labour example:**
- The lecturer, an Indian resident, delivers a paid lecture at Harvard.
- It counts in US GDP, because the activity occurred in the US.
- It counts in India's GNP, because the lecturer is an Indian resident who files taxes in India.

**Capital example (Maruti Suzuki):**
- Cars are produced in India, so they count in India's GDP.
- The share of proceeds paid as dividends to Suzuki (Japan) counts in Japan's GNP.

**Residency, not citizenship, is the test:**
- NRIs (e.g., Indians working in the Middle East or US) are taxed abroad. Their income is part of that country's GDP and GNP.
- Employees sent abroad on an Indian payroll: it depends on where the income is taxed and residency criteria (e.g., more than 183 days).

**When the gap matters:**
- The GDP–GNP gap is small for India and the US. Few residents earn abroad relative to a population of about 140 crore, and Indian capital abroad is small.
- The gap is large for Ireland and Iceland. Many citizens work abroad, and many multinationals (e.g., Apple, Amazon) have headquarters there. Part of other countries' GDP becomes their GNP.

**Remittances:**
- Example: money sent home from the UAE.
- Remittances are **not factor income**. They are **current transfers**, like gifts.
- The income was produced and taxed in the UAE, so remittances are not part of India's GNP.

### 2.13 Savings–investment identity and twin deficits

Start with

$$Y = C + I + G + X - M$$

Let $T$ be tax revenue and $TR$ be government transfer payments. Subtract $T$ and add $TR$ on both sides:

$$\underbrace{Y - T + TR}_{\text{household disposable income}} = C + I + \underbrace{(G + TR - T)}_{\text{fiscal deficit}} + (X - M)$$

**Example:** income 100, tax 30, transfer 10 (e.g., PM-Kisan Samman Nidhi or Ladli Behna):

$$100 - 30 + 10 = 80$$

Moving $C$ to the left side gives household savings:

$$\underbrace{(Y - T + TR - C)}_{S_{\text{household}}} = I + \underbrace{(G + TR - T)}_{\text{fiscal deficit}} + \underbrace{(X - M)}_{\text{trade surplus}}$$

**Interpretation:** household savings, deposited in banks, fund:
- firms' investment
- the government's fiscal deficit
- the trade surplus (output not consumed is sold abroad)

**Implication:** if household savings and investment are unchanged and the fiscal deficit rises, the trade surplus must fall. In other words, the current account deficit rises.

**India, 1975 to the 1990s (chart):**
- Axes: years on the x-axis, % of GDP on the y-axis.
- Household savings were roughly stable (about +1.3 percentage points).
- Investment rose slightly (about +0.9 pp).
- The fiscal deficit rose sharply.
- The current account deficit rose consistently alongside it.

This is the **twin deficit** phenomenon. The 1991 crisis is usually attributed to the current account deficit, but the lecturer argues the fiscal deficit was the underlying cause.

### 2.14 What is NOT counted in GDP

**Financial transactions:** buying or selling shares, stocks and bonds creates no new output.

**Second-hand goods:**
- A resold car or an already-built house is not counted again; the car was counted in its year of production.
- Only the broker's or reseller's margin (commission) is counted in the current year, since it pays the reseller's workers.
- Original production wages are not counted again.

**Government transfer payments:**
- Excluded: pensions, unemployment compensation, cash schemes such as PM-Kisan Samman Nidhi and Ladli Behna.
- MGNREGA is not a good example of a pure transfer, because work is involved.

**Gifts:** e.g., wedding or birthday gifts are excluded, since nothing new is created.

**Edge cases:**
- **Matchmaking/brokerage is counted.** Bringing buyers and sellers together is a service; a wedding matchmaker's income counts.
- **Legal informal/underground activity is partly captured.** Samosa sellers and barbers are hard to survey for income, so their value added is estimated from household consumption surveys (e.g., household spending on haircuts) in national sample surveys.
- **Illegal transactions are not captured.**

### 2.15 Nominal vs Real GDP

GDP can rise through higher volume, higher prices, or both.

**Nominal GDP (GDP at current prices):** current-year quantities × current-year prices. For example:

$$\text{Nominal GDP}_{2026} = \sum Q_{2026} \times P_{2026}$$

**Real GDP (GDP at constant prices):** quantities valued at base-year prices. For example, with 2015 as the base year:

$$\text{Real GDP}_{2026} = \sum Q_{2026} \times P_{2015}$$

**Example:**
- Income rose from ₹50,000 (2015) to ₹1.5 lakh, a nominal tripling.
- Prices also rose about 50–70%, so actual purchasing power rose only about 1.5–2 times.
- To compare 2026 and 2015, value both years' output at 2015 prices.

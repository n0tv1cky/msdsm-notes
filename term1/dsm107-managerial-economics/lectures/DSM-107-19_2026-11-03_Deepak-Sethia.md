# DSM-107 Managerial Economics, Session 19 (03 Nov 2026, Deepak Sethia)

**Topic:** Open-Economy Macro (Fixed vs. Flexible Exchange Rates, Interest Parity, Impossible Trinity, Balance of Payments), then an Introduction to Aggregate Supply and Unemployment

---

## 1. Overview

The session finishes the open-economy macroeconomics module and starts aggregate supply.

**Open-economy part.**
- It recaps how the trade balance reflects the gap between national saving and investment in a small open economy.
- It works through monetary and fiscal expansion under **fixed** and **flexible** exchange rates with free capital mobility. The key result: under a fixed rate, monetary policy is ineffective and fiscal policy is "accommodated" by the central bank. Under a flexible rate, monetary policy works through currency depreciation and higher net exports.
- It explains why interest rates differ across countries:
  - **expected depreciation**, which is driven by **inflation differentials**, with a numerical India–US example;
  - **risk premia**.
- It introduces the **impossible trinity**, with the euro zone, Hong Kong and China as examples.
- It compares fixed and flexible exchange regimes.
- It walks through India's balance of payments.

**Aggregate supply part.**
- It contrasts the Keynesian (horizontal) short-run aggregate supply curve with the vertical long-run curve at potential output.
- It explains why macro and micro supply elasticities are "reversed".
- It defines the unemployment rate and three types of unemployment (frictional, structural, cyclical).
- It ends with the realistic "flat, then upward-sloping, then near-vertical" AS curve.
- This sets up next session on the short-run inflation–unemployment trade-off.

## 2. Topics Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap: Trade balance as the saving–investment gap (small open economy)

**Core identity.** In an open economy, net exports equal the gap between national saving and investment:

```math
NX = S - I
```

**Small open economy.**
- The domestic interest rate is pinned to the **world (foreign) interest rate**, $r = r^\ast $.
- Investment depends on the interest rate.

**Graph described.**
- Vertical axis: interest rate $r$. Horizontal axis: quantity of saving or investment.
- The investment demand curve $I(r)$ slopes **downward**.
- There is a horizontal line at the world interest rate $r^\ast $.
- The initial equilibrium is at point $E$, where saving equals investment.

**Case A: Fiscal deficit rises** (government spending goes up or taxes fall).
1. Government saving falls, so national saving $S$ falls and the saving curve shifts.
2. This puts **upward pressure on the domestic interest rate**.
3. Higher returns attract **capital inflows**, which increases the supply of foreign exchange.
4. The exchange rate **appreciates**. Example: from Rs 50/USD toward a lower number.
5. At $r^\ast $, investment exceeds saving ($I > S$), so there is a \ast \ast trade deficit\ast \ast  ($NX < 0$).
6. The extra imports are financed by the capital inflow.

**Case B: Fiscal discipline improves** (spending is cut or tax revenue rises).
1. National saving rises, which puts **downward pressure on interest rates**.
2. Domestic investors prefer to invest abroad. They convert rupees to dollars through the RBI or the forex market, so demand for dollars rises.
3. There is a **capital outflow**, and the rupee **depreciates**. Example: from 50 to 60 Rs/USD.
4. Saving exceeds investment ($S > I$), so there is a **trade surplus** ($NX > 0$).
5. The country is effectively lending abroad.

### 2.2 Policy under a FIXED exchange rate with free capital mobility

Note the **inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates (yields)**:
- If the central bank buys bonds, bond prices rise and yields fall.
- If it sells bonds, bond prices fall and yields rise.

**(a) Monetary tightening under a fixed rate (briefly mentioned).**
1. The RBI sells government bonds to drain liquidity, so bond prices fall and interest rates rise.
2. Capital flows in, so supply of dollars rises.
3. To hold the peg, the RBI must buy dollars and issue rupees.
4. The money supply expands again, and the tightening is reversed.

**(b) Monetary expansion under a fixed rate** (for example, to fight a recession).
1. The RBI buys government bonds and gives currency to the public, so the money supply rises.
2. RBI demand absorbs part of the existing bond supply, so bond prices rise and the interest rate falls.
3. Mobile capital seeks higher returns abroad. Investors convert rupees to dollars, creating a **capital outflow**.
4. There is pressure for the rupee to depreciate (50 toward 60).
5. Because the RBI has promised to hold the rate at 50, it **sells dollars and takes back rupees**.
6. The money supply contracts back. The RBI created rupees by buying bonds, then reabsorbed the same rupees by selling dollars.

**Result: monetary policy is ineffective.** You cannot keep an interest rate different from the rest of the world under a fixed rate with free capital flows, because **arbitrage** would follow:
- If the domestic rate is lower, money flows out.
- If the domestic rate is higher, money flows in.

**(c) Fiscal expansion under a fixed rate.**
1. The government ("Delhi") spends more and must borrow. The new borrower in the market pushes **interest rates up**.
2. Capital flows in and the supply of dollars rises.
3. There is pressure for the rupee to appreciate (50 toward 40).
4. The RBI ("Mumbai") buys the extra dollars at the fixed rate of 50 and pays rupees, which **injects liquidity**.

**Result:** fiscal policy is **accommodated by the central bank**. The monetary expansion happens automatically, so fiscal policy is effective under a fixed rate.

### 2.3 Policy under a FLEXIBLE exchange rate

**Monetary expansion under a flexible rate.**
1. The RBI buys bonds, so bond prices rise and the interest rate falls.
2. Capital flows out, and the rupee **depreciates** (50 to 60). This time the **RBI does not intervene**.
3. Capital outflows tend to pull the domestic interest rate back toward the global rate. So the attempt to lower interest rates may not fully succeed.
4. However, depreciation makes exports cheaper, so **exports rise** and $NX$ rises.
5. Aggregate demand rises through its $NX$ component:

```math
AD = C + I + G + NX
```

**Result:** monetary policy is **effective**. It raises output by raising net exports.

**Graph: Keynesian cross, open economy.**
- Horizontal axis: output $Y$. Vertical axis: aggregate demand or expenditure. There is a 45° line.
- Autonomous demand $\bar{A}$ now **includes the net export component**.
- After the interest rate falls from $i_1$ to $i_2$ (with $i_2 < i_1$), the currency depreciates and $NX$ rises.
- The AD line **shifts upward**, and the equilibrium moves from $E_1$ to $E_2$ at a higher output.
- Exports depend on the exchange rate, global income and other factors.

### 2.4 Remedies for recession (answer to a student question)

A recession means **unused capacity, i.e., deficient aggregate demand**. The fix is to raise one of $C$, $I$, $G$ or $NX$:
1. Cut interest rates so that investment rises.
2. Let the currency depreciate so that exports rise and idle factories produce for export.
3. Fiscal expansion, i.e., higher $G$.
4. Government measures that build investor or consumer confidence, so that $I$ or $C$ recovers.

Increasing the money supply is **not the only** solution. The solution always lies in aggregate demand.

**Capital mobility in practice.**
- India still has some restrictions on capital mobility.
- Capital is highly mobile in the EU, Canada, Japan, the US, Hong Kong and Singapore.

### 2.5 Why interest rates differ across countries

**Observed gaps:**

| | India 10-yr bond yield | US 10-yr bond yield | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Now | ≈ 7% | ≈ 4.8–4.9% (call it 5%) | ≈ 2 percentage points |
| Earlier | ≈ 7–7.5% | ≈ 3–3.5% | ≈ 4 percentage points |

- **Under a fixed exchange rate**, interest rates should converge because of arbitrage. Example: if India pays 10% and the US pays 5% at a fixed rate, everyone invests in India.
- **Under flexible rates**, differentials can persist.

#### Worked example: Uncovered interest parity

**Setup.**
- 2025 exchange rate: $E = 50$ Rs/USD.
- The rupee is expected to depreciate 10% next year, so $E^e = 55$.
- US interest rate: $i^\ast  = 2\text{%}$.
- Question: what Indian T-bill rate makes an investor **indifferent** between the two countries?

**Steps.**
1. Invest USD 1 in US bonds. Next year you have $1 \times 1.02 = 1.02$ USD.
2. In rupees at next year's expected rate, that is $1.02 \times 55 = 56.1$ rupees.
3. The equivalent rupee investment today is Rs 50 (= USD 1). So Rs 50 must grow to Rs 56.1:

```math
i = \frac{(1+i^*)\,E^e}{E} - 1 = \frac{1.02 \times 55}{50} - 1 = \frac{56.1}{50} - 1 = 0.122 = 12.2\%
```
4. Check: $56.1 / 55 = 1.02$ USD, the same as investing in the US. So 12.2% in India is equivalent to 2% in the US.

**General formula (exact):**

```math
1 + i = (1 + i^*)\,\frac{E^e}{E}
```

**Approximation:**

```math
i \approx i^* + \frac{E^e - E}{E}
```
In the example, $2\text{%} + 10\text{%} \approx 12\text{%}$.

**Key takeaway (lecturer repeated this):** the interest rate differential between two countries exists **because the market expects the higher-rate country's currency to depreciate**.
- Example: India at 7% and the US at 5% implies an expected rupee depreciation of about 2% per year.

#### Why is depreciation expected? Inflation differentials

**Real exchange rate** (recalled from the previous session):

```math
\varepsilon = E \cdot \frac{P_F}{P_D}
```
Here $E$ is Rs per USD, $P_F$ is the foreign price level and $P_D$ is the domestic price level.

**The causal chain:**
1. If domestic inflation is higher, $P_D$ rises faster than $P_F$.
2. For the real exchange rate to stay roughly constant, $E$ must rise, i.e., the rupee depreciates.
3. So investors expect depreciation.
4. So they demand a higher domestic interest rate:

```math
i = i^* + \text{expected depreciation}
```

**Implications.**
- The right question is not "why is my currency depreciating?" but "**why is domestic inflation high?**" The problem to address is the inflation differential.
- In the last decade the India–US inflation gap has narrowed (US about 3–3.5%, India about 4.5%, a gap of roughly 1.5 points).
- That is why the interest gap fell from about 4% to about 2%. It does not mean India should raise rates simply because the gap narrowed.

#### Second reason: Risk premium

- **Analogy:** lend to 100 people. In one group 1–2 default; in another 6–7 default. You charge the riskier group a higher rate.
- Countries with higher **default risk, political instability, an uncertain business environment, or tax uncertainty** pay a **risk premium**. Examples given: Pakistan, Nigeria.
- Example of tax uncertainty: foreigners earn in India and then the government suddenly raises taxes.
- **Graph:** the investment demand curve faces an interest rate equal to the global rate plus expected depreciation. With higher country risk, the relevant rate is **higher** by the risk premium.
- **Lesson:** political stability and tax and policy certainty are crucial for a conducive business environment and for exchange-rate stability.

### 2.6 The Impossible Trinity (Trilemma)

**The three goals.** A country **cannot simultaneously** have all three of:
1. free capital mobility,
2. a fixed exchange rate,
3. independent monetary policy (setting its own interest rate).

**It must choose two and sacrifice one:**

| Choice | Sacrificed | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Independent monetary policy + free capital flows | Fixed exchange rate (so the rate floats) | US, Euro area as a whole (EU) |
| Fixed exchange rate + free capital flows | Independent monetary policy (domestic rate follows the anchor) | Hong Kong (pegged to USD, follows US rates) |
| Fixed exchange rate + independent monetary policy | Free capital flows (capital controls: restrict inflows when rates are high, block capital flight) | Capital-control regimes |

**China** now allows some currency movement, but it is a **managed float**, not a free float.

### 2.7 The Euro as a fixed-exchange-rate case

**How it works.**
- France, Germany, Italy and others gave up the franc, mark and lira. Their mutual exchange rate is fixed one-to-one through a common currency.
- So there must be **one common interest rate**, set by the **European Central Bank**.

**The problem.**
- If Italy or France faces a recession, ideally it would cut rates. That would lead to capital outflow, depreciation and higher exports. But it no longer has that authority.

**Why devaluation helps.**
1. To regain demand you need lower prices, which requires lower costs, which means lower wages.
2. But **nominal wages are sticky downward**: people resist nominal pay cuts.
3. Devaluation keeps nominal wages fixed but lowers them in dollar terms. Output becomes cheaper abroad and exports rise.
4. Eurozone members have given up this tool individually.

**Why did they accept it?**
- History of **competitive devaluations** and trade wars (and world wars) among European countries.
- Integration was meant to reduce conflict and improve capital mobility.

**Competitive devaluation ("exporting unemployment")**
- I devalue to sell abroad, which shifts my unemployment onto others. They retaliate.
- This creates a need for international coordination.

### 2.8 Fixed vs. flexible exchange rates: Which is better?

**Fixed rate.**
- Advantages:
  - Certainty.
  - No currency conversion. Example: travel or trade from Germany to France.
  - No exchange-rate risk on lending. A French lender to a German is repaid in the same euros.
  - Contrast: a US lender to an Indian faces risk about the rate when rupees are redeemed two years later.
- Requirement: **monetary discipline**. Do not print excess money or create domestic inflation. With higher inflation, you must eventually devalue.

**Flexible rate.**
- Allows independent monetary policy and adjustment through the exchange rate.

**Bottom line (lecturer: "let me be very clear"):**
- In the long run, exchange-rate movements are rooted in **inflation differentials**.
- Countries that want stable exchange rates must maintain **inflation discipline**.
- In the short run, capital flows also move the rate.

### 2.10 Aggregate Supply, Unemployment and Inflation (new part)

**Motivation.**
- Why do economies deviate from a smooth growth path (recessions and booms)?
- What can policy do about **unemployment (berozgari)** and **inflation (mehngai)**, the two perpetual electoral issues?
- Preview: there is a **trade-off between inflation and unemployment in the short run, but not in the long run**. This is next session's topic.

#### Keynesian (short-run) AS curve: horizontal

**Graph.** Price level $P$ on the vertical axis, output $Y$ on the horizontal axis. The curve is a horizontal line, i.e., perfectly elastic.

**Logic.**
- With unemployment, **wages are sticky** in the short run.
- Idle workers are willing to work at the prevailing wage, though they will not accept wage cuts.
- So firms can hire more workers without raising costs and expand output **at the prevailing prices**, provided there is demand.

#### Long-run AS curve: vertical at potential output

- In the long run, output depends on **capital stock, technology and labour supply**, not on prices or demand.
- **Potential output $Y^\ast $** is the output when existing capacity is used at **full employment**.
- Graph: a vertical line at $Y^\ast $ on the $(Y, P)$ axes. The price level has little effect on long-run output.
- **Flagged ("we need to understand this particular part very clearly"):**
  - In the long run, output depends solely on productive capacity.
  - In the short run, output depends on demand, i.e., how much of existing capacity is used.
- The LRAS is **not fixed forever**. It **shifts right** over time with new factories, a trained labour force and technological progress. That is a long-run phenomenon, not something monetary policy delivers.

#### Macro vs. micro supply elasticity: the "reversal"

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| **Micro** | Inelastic (some factors are fixed) | Elastic (all factors are variable) |
| **Macro** | Horizontal/elastic (sticky wages, idle capacity) | Vertical/inelastic (total resources are fixed) |

**Why the reversal?**
- **Micro** is about **relative** demand. If demand for one product rises (pulses, coarse cereals, milk) while others stay constant, that producer can draw labour and capital **from other sectors**. It is a substitution of output across sectors.
- **Macro** is about demand for **all** products rising together (hotels, aviation, construction, housing). Every firm competes for the same fixed total labour and capital and pushes up wages. Total output cannot rise beyond capacity.

#### Different market speeds

- Example: the RBI cuts interest rates.
  - **Asset and bond markets** react immediately.
  - The **goods market** reacts slowly: construction takes time, so cement demand materialises with a lag.
  - The **labour market** is slowest: firms do not hire immediately and wages do not adjust immediately.
- This mismatch in flexibility across markets underlies short-run versus long-run behaviour.

#### Unemployment rate (definition)

```math
\text{Labour force } (L) = \text{Employed } (N) + \text{Unemployed } (U)
```

```math
u = \frac{U}{L} = \frac{U}{N + U}
```

**Zero unemployment is neither feasible nor desirable.** "Full employment" at potential output does **not** mean every worker has a job.

#### Three types of unemployment

1. **Frictional.** The time taken to match workers with jobs.
   - Examples: firms close and open as tastes shift (samosas over french fries), retirements, new entrants searching for a suitable job rather than taking the first offer.
   - Frictional unemployment is **higher where unemployment benefits are generous**, as in European countries, because people search longer.
2. **Structural.** A skill or sector mismatch.
   - Examples: the shift to shared mobility moves workers from car manufacturing to driving; the shift to millets or coarse cereals moves farmers between crops.
3. **Cyclical.** Occurs when output is **below potential output** (a recession) and all sectors suffer.
   - **This is the type macro policy should address.**

"Full employment" = only frictional plus structural unemployment remain.

#### Realistic (medium-run) AS curve

**Graph.** Price level $P$ on the vertical axis, output $Y$ on the horizontal axis. The curve has three segments:
1. **Far below potential output:** **flat/horizontal**. Rising AD raises output without raising prices.
2. **Approaching full employment:** **upward sloping**. Further AD increases bring some output gain and rising prices.
3. **At or near full employment:** **nearly vertical**. Rising AD mostly raises **prices**, with very little extra output.

In the short run the economy can even produce slightly beyond full-employment output for a short duration, but at the cost of large price increases. This is to be explored next session.

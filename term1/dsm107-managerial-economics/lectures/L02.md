# DSM-107 Managerial Economics — Session L02 Study Notes

## 1. Overview

This session completes the discussion of **economic rent** (begun in L01), extends it to **innovation rent**, and shows how **relative prices** and **economic incentives/disincentives** shape firms' technology choices. It then turns to **specialization**, explaining why modern economies produce almost nothing they consume individually, and the three mechanisms (learning by doing, differences in ability, economies of scale) through which specialization creates prosperity. The bulk of the class is a fully worked two-person, two-good example (Greta and Carlos; apples and wheat) that distinguishes **absolute advantage** from **comparative advantage**, computes opportunity costs in common units, and proves the **gains from trade** — showing total output and each individual's consumption both rise under specialization plus trade, and deriving the **range of feasible trading prices**. The running motivating question throughout is *why the Industrial Revolution began in Britain*, answered (partially) via the low price of coal relative to the high price of labour. The session ends by introducing the concept of **technology as a set of input combinations**, with a five-technology table (workers vs. coal) that will be analysed next session (isocost/isoquant-type analysis to follow on Wednesday).

## 2. Topics and Concepts, in Order Taught

### A. Recap: Opportunity Cost, Economic Cost, Economic Rent

- **Economic cost = direct (out-of-pocket) cost + opportunity cost**
- **Opportunity cost** = net benefit of the *next best* forgone option.
- **Economic rent — three equivalent definitions** (all given as equivalent in class):
  1. `Economic rent = Net benefit from option taken − Net benefit from next best option`
  2. `Economic rent = Net benefit from option taken − Opportunity cost`
  3. `Economic rent = Benefit − Economic cost`
- **Decision rule:** If action A yields a **positive economic rent → do it** (or keep doing it). If rent = 0, you are indifferent (same net benefit as the next best option), so continuing is acceptable. Negative rent → don't.

### B. Innovation Rent

- **Definition:** `Innovation rent = Profits from using the new technology − Profits from using the existing (old) technology`
- A special case of economic rent, arising when a firm adopts a new technology whose net benefit exceeds that of the incumbent technology.
- **Mechanism / example described:** One firm in an industry innovates and **patents** the innovation, so rivals cannot legally copy. That firm earns higher profits vis-à-vis all firms still using the old technology. This extra profit *is* the innovation rent.
- **Why it matters:** The prospect of innovation rents is the "carrot" that drives firms to invest in **R&D**. Economic growth is largely attributed to technological progress/innovation.
- **Important qualifier raised in class:** Inventing is not enough. Adoption requires the new technology to actually be *cheaper* given the firm's cost structure — this is where **relative prices** enter.
- **Q&A clarification (asked by a student):** Does a firm that restructures and returns to profit earn *innovation rent*?
  - Yes, **if** the restructuring counts as **managerial/organizational innovation** — e.g., reassigning tasks so workers are more productive, introducing specialization, streamlining the assembly line. Innovation need not be scientific/technological; it can be managerial or entrepreneurial.
  - **No**, if the "restructuring" is simply selling off a loss-making unit — that is an accounting matter (shedding dead weight), not innovation.

### C. Relative Prices, Economic Incentives and Disincentives

- **Economic incentive** = a reward for undertaking a particular action. **Economic disincentive** = the converse, a penalty.
- Incentives/disincentives affect the choices people and firms make; **relative prices are a key determinant of economic incentives**.
- **Relative price definition:** the price of one thing relative to the price of another; expressed as a **ratio of two prices**, e.g. `P_machinery / P_labour`.
- **Examples given:**
  - Price of machinery vs. price of labour → drives automation. Rising labour costs / labour shortages in developed countries push firms toward industrial robots and AI.
  - Price of renewable energy vs. conventional energy → sourcing choice for firms.
  - Cost of an internal-combustion-engine (ICE) vehicle vs. an electric vehicle (EV) → consumer choice.
  - Hire one more person vs. adopt automation.
- **Q&A clarification (asked by a student):** Can a government manipulate relative prices/incentives to force particular choices (e.g., EV adoption)? Yes, but economics cannot predict outcomes with certainty — models generate *likely* predictions; unanticipated factors not in the model can change the dynamic entirely. The model need not be 100% correct.

### D. Application: Relative Prices and the Industrial Revolution

- **What the Industrial Revolution was (in this framing):** innovation in **machinery that saved on human labour**, letting tasks be done with less human input. Many early innovations originated in **cloth / textiles / garment-making**.
- **Why deliberate (not serendipitous) innovation:** **Labour was costly in Britain** — wages were rising — so firms deliberately sought labour-saving machines.
- **The energy link:** Machines need an energy source if humans don't supply it. **Coal** is that source. Britain was **endowed with abundant coal**, but *endowment alone is not enough* — the **price** of coal had to be sufficiently low **relative to the price of human labour (the wage)**.
- **Core mechanism to remember:**
  > The price of one energy resource (**coal**) fell *relative to* the price of another energy resource (**human labour**), and this **relative price influenced the choice of technology**.
- The lecturer explicitly notes this is **one explanation among many** — others include the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, political/cultural factors, the "Protestant ethic", and **colonialism** (captive markets, forced de-industrialization of colonies, resource extraction). The course focuses on the labour-vs-energy relative price channel because it teaches the economics.

### E. Specialization and Long-Run Living Standards

- **Key claim:** *What a society specializes in has centuries-long consequences for living standards.*
  - Specialize in sectors with **constant/rapid technological innovation** (manufacturing, high-end IT services) → living standards rise year after year.
  - Specialize in sectors with **low scope for technological progress** (e.g., agriculture) → society falls behind, relatively.
- **Historical illustration:**
  - Around **1600**, South Asia/India had many manufacturing hubs (primitive, labour-intensive cloth-making) and was known worldwide for producing non-food goods more efficiently than the rest of the world.
  - Over the next ~400 years, **Western Europe and North America accelerated**; **India/South Asia fell behind**. Reason: differing specializations. Europe and its offshoots specialized in **manufacturing** → rapid technological progress → higher income per person / output per worker.
  - **Colonialism** contributed: Europeans forced colonies away from manufacturing.
  - Consequence: South Asia remained in the **Malthusian trap** — output per capita did not rise. From roughly **1000 AD to 1700 AD**, per-capita income growth in India was very small relative to European nations.

### F. Specialization in Everyday Life

- Thought exercise: look at your desk — how much of it did *you* make? The snack maybe cooked by you, but you didn't grow the food; you certainly didn't manufacture the laptop.
- ~300 years ago (around **1700**), most things people consumed were made by themselves or by people in close proximity (their village).
- Today: laptop made in China; design in one place, R&D in another, parts from multiple locations — a **global value chain**.
- **Distinctive feature of the modern economy:** we make almost none of what we consume; a very small fraction is our own input. This is possible because each of us **specializes in very small tasks** and then **exchanges/trades**.
- **Adam Smith's idea:** we get better at producing things if we specialize, and this increases our capacity to produce.

### G. Three Reasons Specialization Creates Prosperity

1. **Learning by doing** — the more you repeatedly do something, the better you get at it (e.g., the more you code the better you code; the more AI prompts you write, the better your prompting).
2. **Differences in innate ability** — innate skills differ (analytical vs. writing vs. design). Also **environmental/natural differences**: some soils suit food crops, others suit cash crops. So the "ability" can belong to the natural environment, not just the person.
3. **Economies of scale** — larger scale lowers unit cost. A factory producing **only one smartphone model** has lower cost per smartphone than the same floor producing smartphones + tablets + laptops together. **Overheads, design cost and R&D cost get spread over more units.** Firms can even specialize in small *components* of a product.

### H. The Counter-intuitive Result to Be Proved

- Even if one person is an **all-rounder** — excellent at cooking, coding, sport, presentations — it is still **better for total prosperity if that person specializes in one task** and leaves the rest to others.
- The open question: **which** task should the all-rounder specialize in? Answer comes from **comparative advantage** / lowest opportunity cost.

## 3. Worked Example — Greta and Carlos, Apples and Wheat

### Setup
- A world of **two individuals**: **Greta** and **Carlos**.
- **Two goods**: **apples** and **wheat (tons)**. Both must consume **both** goods to survive.
- They own **different land with different fertility**: Greta's land is **more fertile**; Carlos's is **less fertile**.
- **Simplifying assumption (deliberate):** there are **no out-of-pocket costs at all** — land is their own, labour is their own, assume organic farming (no fertilizer) and seeds carried over from the previous year's crop. This is assumed **precisely to drive home that opportunity cost still exists and still matters**.

### Productivity Table (output if 100% of time spent on one good, per year)

| | Apples (if 100% of time) | Wheat, tons (if 100% of time) |
|---|---|---|
| **Greta** | 1,250 | 50 |
| **Carlos** | 1,000 | 20 |

### Absolute Advantage
- **Definition:** A person or country has an **absolute advantage** in producing a good if, **for a given available set of inputs, they can produce more of it** than the other person/country.
- Here **Greta has absolute advantage in BOTH apples and wheat** (1,250 > 1,000 and 50 > 20).
- Equivalently, **Carlos has an absolute disadvantage in both crops**.
- Note: land can be used for only one crop; you cannot do both with 100% of time.

### Comparative Advantage via Productivity Ratios (unit-free)
- Apples: `1,250 / 1,000 = 1.25` → Greta is **1.25×** as productive as Carlos in apples.
- Wheat: `50 / 20 = 2.5` → Greta is **2.5×** as productive as Carlos in wheat.
- Since **1.25 < 2.5**, Carlos's **disadvantage is smaller in apples** than in wheat.
- **Greta's relative productivity is highest in wheat → Greta has comparative advantage in WHEAT.**
- **Carlos has comparative advantage in APPLES** (comparative advantage is complementary — if one has it in one good, the other automatically has it in the other).
- **Why ratios?** Ratios of like-with-like (apples÷apples, wheat÷wheat) are **unit-free**, so they are comparable across goods. "Literally an apples-to-apples comparison."

### Comparative Advantage — Formal Definition
> A person or country has a **comparative advantage** in the production of a particular good if **the cost to them of producing that good, relative to the cost of another good, is lower** than it is for another person/country.
> Here "cost" means **economic cost** = actual cost + opportunity cost; in this example, since actual cost = 0, it is purely **opportunity cost**.

### Opportunity Cost Calculations

**Greta:** producing 1,250 apples means giving up 50 tons of wheat.
- Opportunity cost of **1 ton of wheat** = `1250 / 50` = **25 apples**
- Opportunity cost of **1 apple** = `50 / 1250` = **0.04 tons of wheat**

**Carlos:** producing 1,000 apples means giving up 20 tons of wheat.
- Opportunity cost of **1 ton of wheat** = `1000 / 20` = **50 apples**
- Opportunity cost of **1 apple** = `20 / 1000` = **0.02 tons of wheat**

### Opportunity Cost Summary Table

| | Opportunity cost of 1 ton of wheat (in apples) | Opportunity cost of 1 apple (in tons of wheat) |
|---|---|---|
| **Greta** | **25 apples** | **0.04 tons** |
| **Carlos** | **50 apples** | **0.02 tons** |

- **Wheat:** Greta's cost (25) < Carlos's cost (50) → **Greta specializes in wheat.**
- **Apples:** Carlos's cost (0.02) < Greta's cost (0.04) → **Carlos specializes in apples.**
- **Decision rule:** `Profit = output − cost`; the person with the **lower (opportunity) cost** should undertake that production.
- **Units caution (raised in class):** When comparing opportunity cost of wheat, both figures are measured **in apples**; when comparing opportunity cost of apples, both are **in tons of wheat**. Only like units are compared.
- **Intuition:** "Even if you are good at both, you don't have time for both. The question to ask is: *what is the best use of my time?* Answer: **whatever has the lower opportunity cost.**" Greta **loses less** by producing wheat than by producing apples.

### Gains From Trade — Step 1: The Self-Sufficiency ("Lockdown") Benchmark

No trade; each person produces and consumes exactly what they make.

**Greta's time allocation:** 40% apples, 60% wheat
- Apples: `40% × 1,250 = 500 apples`
- Wheat: `60% × 50 = 30 tons`

**Carlos's time allocation:** 30% apples, 70% wheat
- Apples: `30% × 1,000 = 300 apples`
- Wheat: `70% × 20 = 14 tons`
- (*Rationale given:* everyone needs some minimum wheat to survive; Carlos's land is less fertile, so he must devote 10 percentage points more time to wheat than Greta.)

**Total economy output (no trade): 800 apples and 44 tons of wheat.**

### Gains From Trade — Step 2: Full Specialization

- Greta specializes in **wheat → 50 tons**, 0 apples.
- Carlos specializes in **apples → 1,000 apples**, 0 wheat.
- **Total economy output: 1,000 apples and 50 tons of wheat.**

### Gains From Trade — Step 3: Trade at a Given Price

**Assumed trading price: 1 ton of wheat = 40 apples.**

Carlos wants to consume **15 tons of wheat** (chosen because he could produce 14 tons himself, so 15 is an improvement).
- Apples Carlos must pay: `15 × 40 = 600 apples`
- **Carlos ends with:** `1,000 − 600 = 400 apples` and **15 tons of wheat**
- **Greta ends with:** `50 − 15 = 35 tons of wheat` and **600 apples**

### Comparison Table (the "key table" the lecturer says will be provided)

| | **Col 1: Produce both, no trade** | **Col 2: Specialize (production)** | **Col 3: Consumption after trade** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Greta — apples** | 500 | 0 | **600** |
| **Greta — wheat (tons)** | 30 | 50 | **35** |
| **Carlos — apples** | 300 | 1,000 | **400** |
| **Carlos — wheat (tons)** | 14 | 0 | **15** |
| **Total apples** | 800 | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| **Total wheat (tons)** | 44 | 50 | 50 |

**Conclusion:** Comparing Column 1 with Column 3 — **both Greta and Carlos have more apples AND more wheat**. Total GDP (the "pie") is larger, so each gets a larger share of a larger pie. **Specializing and trading beats trying to do everything.**

### The Range of Feasible Prices (bargaining range)

Check the price `1 ton of wheat = 40 apples` against each party's opportunity cost:

- **Greta (the wheat seller):** her cost of producing 1 ton of wheat is **25 apples**. She receives 40 apples → she gains **15 apples** per ton (lecturer said "14 more apples" — the correct figure is 40 − 25 = 15). **She profits.** She will never accept **below 25 apples**.
- **Carlos (the wheat buyer):** producing 1 ton of wheat himself would cost him **50 apples**. Buying it at 40 apples is cheaper → **he profits.** He will never pay **50 or more** (at exactly 50 he is indifferent between making and buying; above 50 he makes it himself).

> **Feasible price range: 25 apples < price of 1 ton of wheat < 50 apples.**
> Lower bound = Greta's opportunity cost (25). Upper bound = Carlos's opportunity cost (50). 40 apples is just one of many prices at which trade is mutually beneficial.

### Broader Moral Drawn in Class
- Even if you have **no out-of-pocket expense, you always have an opportunity cost.**
- Even if someone is **less productive than you in everything** (e.g., due to lack of access to education), that does **not** mean they should not be employed or assigned tasks. It is better for everybody if each specializes where their **relative gap** is most favourable.
- A student asked whether the productivity ratios (1.25 vs 2.5) alone are enough to identify who does what. **Answer:** the ratios are enough to *identify* comparative advantage, but you still need the **opportunity cost calculation** to *demonstrate to the all-rounder (Greta)* that doing both is worse for her — i.e., to prove that the world (and she) is better off with specialization and trade.

## 5. Additional Q&A (Malthusian Trap — carried over from L01)

- **Graph discussed:** the historical chart of **wages** and **population** over time (pre- and post-Industrial Revolution). Before the Industrial Revolution: the **Malthusian trap** — population growth eroded any wage gains, so output per capita didn't rise. After the Industrial Revolution: a **spike in both wages and population**, and the trap was broken.
- **⚠ Graph-reading warning (explicitly flagged):** The two curves are plotted on **two different y-axes** — one read on the **left axis**, the other on the **right axis**. Therefore **even where the curves cross, you cannot conclude one variable exceeds the other**; the scales differ. Do not infer a return to the Malthusian trap from the curves converging.
- **Will we fall back into the Malthusian trap?** Unlikely, because **continuous technological progress** keeps raising the productivity of inputs — in the same 24 hours we produce far more than before, keeping us well above subsistence. For a return to the trap you would need not just growth to *stop* but growth to become **negative**.
- Other existential risks (global warming, climate change) exist but are a different mechanism; green technology may mitigate them.
- **Hunger today** is mainly a **distributional** problem, not a production problem — the world can produce enough food for everyone.
- **Wealth distribution:** important for how humans live, but a **second-order** question. The **first-order** question — *can we even produce enough?* — has been solved by technology; **fair distribution has not been solved.**

## 1. Overview

The session introduces macroeconomics and how it differs from microeconomics. Macro studies **aggregates**, meaning the whole economy, where all markets tend to move together. It focuses on **short-run fluctuations around a long-run trend**, which are demand-driven, rather than long-run growth, which is supply-driven. The Great Depression motivates the idea of an **output gap**: the productive capacity exists, but it goes unused because firms fear they cannot sell their output.

The lecture then outlines the course and begins **national income accounting**:
- the definition of GDP;
- the identity *output = income = expenditure*;
- a detailed **circular flow diagram** with households, firms, government, financial markets and the rest of the world;
- a simple apple-economy example.

It closes with a farmer–baker example worked in three variants. These teach the **expenditure (final output) approach** and the **value-added (production) approach**, how to avoid double counting, the formula $\text{GVA} = \text{GVO} - \text{IC}$, imputing self-consumption, the exclusion of household services, and inventory valuation. A cliff-hanger question about perishable unsold bread is left for the next session.

## 2. Topics Covered (in order taught)

### 2.1 Motivation: The Great Depression and the Output Gap

- **The images:** The US in the 1920s (the "Roaring Twenties") looked happy. The economy was booming and the job market was good. After the **1929 crash**, the US in the 1930s was in very bad shape.
- **Question: what changed during the Great Depression?** Two scenarios were offered:
  1. Factories and farms that existed in the 1920s **disappeared** in the 1930s, so workers had nowhere to work.
  2. Factories, farms and workers **all still existed**, but firms **would not hire** because they feared they could not sell the output.
- **Answer: scenario 2.** Productive capacity was intact; the problem was **expected lack of demand**.
- **Contrast with the Indian context:** A typical development problem is the first kind. Workers exist but factories and capacity do not, so output cannot be produced.
- **Definitions:**
  - **Potential output:** the output that is feasible given existing capacity.
  - **Actual output:** what is actually produced, which is lower in a depression.
  - **Output gap:** the difference between potential and actual output.

### 2.2 Core Questions of Macroeconomics

- Why do we have **expansions**, when job markets are good and firms hire?
- Why do we have **recessions**, when job markets are bad and firms don't hire?
- What causes **inflation**, defined as a *general* rise in prices?
- **Should the government intervene?** If so, how and when? Sometimes *not* intervening is the better choice.

### 2.3 Micro vs. Macro

- **Micro** studies individuals, firms and particular goods. An example question is how food demand responds to a change in the price of clothing.
- **Macro** studies **aggregates**, the entire economy taken together.
  - In a recession, **all markets face a demand constraint together**. Auto, aviation and hotel workers are all being fired at the same time.
  - In good times, all goods enjoy decent demand.
- **Contrast with sectoral shifts.** These are *not* macro slowdowns:
  - Auto sales fall because people prefer shared mobility (Ola, Uber, Rapido).
  - Demand for landlines falls while demand for mobiles rises.
  - People switch diets, so producers of the old ingredients lose their market.
  - In these cases one good's demand falls while another's rises, and new jobs are created elsewhere.
- **Should the government bail out the declining sector?** This depends on political economy, but **probably not**. Consumers' preferences are shifting, so producers need to change what they produce.

### 2.6 National Income Accounting: Agenda

1. Circular flow of income and output.
2. Approaches to measuring GDP.
3. The saving–investment identity.
4. Nominal vs. real GDP, and issues with price indices.
5. Limitations of GDP as a measure.

**Definition of GDP (highlighted terms):** GDP is the **market value** of all **final goods and services** produced by **factors of production** **within a nation's borders** in a given period.

**Fundamental identity:** One person's output is another's income, which is someone's expenditure.
- When you buy a shirt or phone, it is your **expenditure**.
- It is also the seller's **income**.
- It corresponds to someone's **output**.

$$\text{Output} \equiv \text{Income} \equiv \text{Expenditure}$$

### 2.7 The Circular Flow Diagram

**Notation used in the diagram:**

| Line style / colour | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dashed** line | Flow of *real* resources (goods, services, factors of production) |
| **Solid** line | *Monetary* flow (payments) |
| **Blue** | Factor flows (factors of production and factor payments) |
| **Red** | Final demand (final goods and services) |
| **Black** | **Transfer payments**: money moves with **no production** of goods or services and no one-to-one return |

**Step-by-step build-up of the diagram:**

1. **Households → Firms (blue).** Households **own all factors of production** and supply them to firms. Firms pay factor incomes back.
   - Example: Mukesh Ambani "belongs to" the Antilia household and works at Reliance.

   | Factor | Payment |
   |---|---|
   | Land | Rent |
   | Labour | Wages |
   | Capital | Interest |
   | Entrepreneurship | Profit |

2. **Firms → Households (red).** Firms sell goods and services for consumption (dashed line). Households pay **consumption expenditure** (solid line).
3. **Saving (black).** Households don't spend all their income. Savings go into banks, bonds and equity, which together form the **asset/financial market**.
4. **Investment.** Firms borrow from the asset market and buy **investment goods** such as cement and steel to build factories (red).
5. **Government.**
   - It collects **taxes** from households and from firms. These are black lines (transfers), because there is no one-to-one return at that moment.
   - If taxes are insufficient to fund spending, it **borrows from the asset market**. This shortfall is the **fiscal deficit**.
   - It **hires factors** such as teachers, doctors and soldiers (blue), paying wages.
   - It **produces final services** such as health, education and governance for households (red).
     - The *payment line back* to government is drawn **thin**.
     - Fees such as a government university charging Rs 1,000/year or a school charging Rs 500–1,000/year are **economically insignificant prices**.
     - These services are funded by **taxes**. Whether to produce an extra unit is **not decided by the price charged**, unlike a private firm that compares cost with consumers' willingness to pay.
   - It **buys goods from firms**, such as blackboards and medicines (red), and pays for them.
   - It **provides law & order, governance and infrastructure to firms**, and may charge for them.
   - It makes **transfers to households** with nothing taken back in that transaction, such as Kisan Samman Nidhi and the Ladli Behna scheme (black).
6. **Rest of the world.** **Exports** of goods and services go out (red dashed line), with payment received. **Imports** are the reverse flow.

**Student Q&A: are exports different from selling to households?**
- From the **firm's perspective** it makes no difference whether it sells to you or to an American. It gets paid either way.
- That is why **all red flows are added together**.
- The instructor said it does make a difference elsewhere, to be discussed later.

**Key takeaway: two equivalent ways to measure GDP.**
- **Add up all red flows**, the final uses:
$$\text{GDP} = C + I + G + NX$$
  where $C$ = consumption, $I$ = investment, $G$ = government purchases, and $NX$ = net exports $= X - M$.
- **Add up all blue payments**, the factor incomes paid by firms and government (wages, rent, interest, profit).
- Both give the **same GDP**. Output, when sold, becomes income for those who produced it.

### 2.8 Worked Example 1: The Apple Economy

**Production and income:**
- Output: **100 apples** at **Rs 1 each**, so output $= \text{Rs } 100$.
- Firms pay **wages = Rs 70** and **profit = Rs 30**. Both go to households, since firm owners are also households.
- Total household income $= 70 + 30 = \text{Rs } 100$.

**Uses of the 100 apples:**
- **Consumption:** households spend Rs 60 and eat 60 apples, leaving 40.
- **Government:** it takes **10** as taxes. Collecting 10 apples directly, or collecting Rs 10 and buying 10 apples, is the same thing. It distributes them, for example through Ladli Behna or a food subsidy programme.
- **Saving and investment:** the remaining **30** is saved, with Rs 30 put in banks. Banks lend it to the apple farm, which uses it to finance the 30 apples, e.g., replanting. This is **investment**.

$$C + G + I = 60 + 10 + 30 = 100 = \text{Output} = \text{Income}$$

- **Lesson:** goods and money are counterparts. Goods flow one way and payment flows the other, for both final goods and factors.
- *Small inconsistency in the lecture:* while walking through the diagram, the instructor said households "buy back 70 apples and save 30," omitting the government's 10. The fully specified version is the 60 + 10 + 30 split above.

### 2.9 Worked Example 2: Farmer and Baker (Value Added and Avoiding Double Counting)

**Setup:** The economy has only two producers.
- **Farmer:** produces **50 kg wheat** at **Rs 15/kg** and sells all of it to the baker. Value $= 50 \times 15 = \text{Rs } 750$.
- **Baker:** converts it into **50 units of bread** sold at **Rs 20** each. Value $= 50 \times 20 = \text{Rs } 1{,}000$.

**Common mistake:** adding $750 + 1{,}000 = 1{,}750$. This is **double counting**.
- Once bread is produced, **no wheat is left** in the economy. The wheat is embedded in the bread.
- **GDP = Rs 1,000.**

**Value-added (production) approach:**

| Producer | Gross value of output | Intermediate consumption | Value added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farmer (agriculture) | 750 | 0 | **750** |
| Baker (manufacturing) | 1,000 | 750 | **250** |
| **Total** | | | **1,000** |

- **Formula:**
$$\text{GVA} = \text{GVO} - \text{IC}$$
  where GVA = gross value added, GVO = gross value of output, and IC = intermediate consumption.
- **Sector shares:** agriculture contributes $750/1000 = 75\%$ of GDP and manufacturing $250/1000 = 25\%$.

**Value added per unit, the "price" of the process:**
- On the slide the bakery's "price" is **Rs 5**. This is the price of the *conversion process*, **not** of the bread itself:
$$20 - 15 = 5 \text{ per unit}, \qquad 50 \times 5 = 250$$
- **Refinery analogy:** a refinery buys crude oil at \$100/barrel and sells refined product at \$105/barrel. Its value added, or refining margin, is \$5 per barrel.

**GVA is not profit.**
- A student suggested that the 250 is profit. The instructor corrected this.
- The 250 is shared among **all factors** in bakery production: wages to bakery workers, rent, interest (e.g., on loans for ovens and utensils) and profit to owners.

**Two approaches named:**
- **Expenditure (final output) approach:** count only **final goods**.
- **Value-added (production) approach:** sum the value added at each stage of production.
- **Intermediate goods** are goods that become **embedded in the final good**, like wheat in bread. Don't count both.

### 2.10 Worked Example 3: Self-Consumption (Imputation)

**Change to the setup:** The farmer produces 50 kg wheat but **keeps 20 kg for self-consumption** and sells **30 kg** to the baker. The baker makes 30 units of bread at Rs 20.

- **A student's wrong answer:** Rs 600, on the reasoning that retained output isn't counted.

**Correct answer: Rs 900, by the expenditure approach:**
- Bread: $30 \times 20 = 600$.
- Self-consumed wheat, imputed at the market price: $20 \times 15 = 300$. A relevant market price exists because the farmer *could* have sold it at Rs 15/kg.
$$\text{GDP} = 600 + 300 = 900$$

**The same answer by the production approach:**
- Wheat: $50 \times 15 = 750$, whether sold or self-consumed, since the market price applies to both.
- Bakery value added: $30 \times 5 = 150$.
$$\text{GDP} = 750 + 150 = 900$$

**GDP ignores distribution.**
- 100 people eating 10 kg each gives the same GDP as one person eating 1,000 kg while 99 go hungry.
- GDP measures the value of output, not how it is distributed.
- However, self-produced output *is* counted. For example, a house built and used for one's own consumption still counts.

**Exception: household services are not counted.**
- **Paying a maid** for cooking, cleaning or childcare **is counted**. It is a factor payment with a market price.
- **Doing it yourself at home is not counted.**
- **Why the difference?** Self-produced **commodities** (wheat, home-grown vegetables) have a relevant **market price** and are imputed. Self-produced **services** (cooking, childcare) are non-marketed, and there is no fair price to apply.
- **Mukesh Ambani example:** if he spends 1–2 hours cooking or minding his grandkids, which wage should be applied?
  - The maid's wage? That is not fair.
  - His own earnings rate? That is not fair either.
  - Perhaps he simply enjoys the activity.
- **Rule:**
  - Self-produced **goods** are counted at market price.
  - Household **services** bought **from the market** are counted.
  - Household **services** produced and consumed **at home** are **not counted**.

### 2.11 Worked Example 4: Unsold Output (Inventory Valuation)

**Setup:** Same as Example 3, but now:
- The baker produces **30 units of bread** and **sells only 20** at Rs 20.
- **10 units remain unsold.** The bread is **non-perishable**, so it can be sold next year.
- The bakery's conversion cost, *excluding* selling costs, is **Rs 3 per unit**.

**Answer: GDP = Rs 880, by the expenditure approach:**

| Final product | Calculation | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Self-consumed wheat | $20 \times 15$ | 300 |
| Bread sold to consumers | $20 \times 20$ | 400 |
| Unsold bread → **inventory** | $10 \times (15 + 3) = 10 \times 18$ | 180 |
| **GDP** | | **880** |

**Why value inventory at Rs 18 and not Rs 20?**
- The unsold bread has gone through wheat production (Rs 15) and conversion (Rs 3), but **selling has not been done**.
- It is economically **not the same good** as bread delivered to consumers.
- **Apple analogy:** apples sell at Rs 20/kg in Himachal and at Rs 80/kg in Indore. Moving the product to consumers is itself an economic value addition.

**Cross-check by the production approach** (this check is not in the lecture, but it is consistent with it):
$$\underbrace{750}_{\text{wheat}} + \underbrace{20 \times 5}_{\text{sold bread VA}} + \underbrace{10 \times 3}_{\text{unsold bread VA}} = 750 + 100 + 30 = 880$$

- **The puzzle:** the final good is not counted, yet the farmer was paid for the wheat and the baker paid salaries. Those *incomes* seem to be part of GDP.

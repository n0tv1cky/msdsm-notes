# DSM‑107 Managerial Economics — Session L01 (Microeconomics)
### Introductory Session: Why Do Economies Grow? Opportunity Cost, Economic Cost & Economic Rent

---

## 1. Overview

This first session sets up the microeconomics half of the course (sessions 1–10; sessions 11–20 cover macroeconomics with another instructor) and explains course logistics, the open-source CORE Econ textbook, and the evaluation scheme. It then introduces the *way economists think*: economists use **models** — deliberate simplifications of a hugely complex reality (the "Google Maps" analogy) — to answer big questions. The motivating "big picture question" for the session is: **why did sustained economic growth begin only ~250 years ago?** Using long-run data on GDP per capita (1000+ years, five countries) and on real wages/population in Britain (1260–2008), the lecture shows the "hockey-stick" pattern of growth, explains the **Malthusian trap** (income gains eaten up by population growth), and contrasts **Malthus (1798)** with **Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776)**, whose prediction of sustained per-person income growth turned out right because of continuous **technological progress** (the Industrial Revolution, starting in Great Britain). The second half builds the first analytical toolkit for studying *how people decide whether to take an action*: **opportunity cost, economic cost, and economic rent** (plus incentives), developed through a fully worked concert-vs-babysitting numerical example, and a real-world steel-firm/power-contract illustration of why economic cost ≠ accounting cost. A second toolkit — **specialization, division of labour, comparative advantage** — is previewed for the next session.

### 2.3 Economic models
- Economy = a **complex system**, "like a weather system": millions of interactions between people, firms, countries.
- **Economic model** = a deliberate simplification; a basic way of understanding a phenomenon using simple principles; captures a *fraction* of complexity, not the whole world.
- **Google Maps analogy**: a map distils complex terrain into one simple line from origin to destination; it ignores everything off the route. A model likewise distils complexity into a straightforward analytical device showing **how the variables of interest are related**.

### 2.4 The "big picture question": why does the economy grow?
- Framing: **economics is fundamentally about growth and distribution** — "what makes the pie grow, and who gets what share of the pie."
- **Sustained economic growth is a recent phenomenon — roughly 250 years old**, versus millions of years of human existence.

#### Graph 1 — GDP per capita over 1000+ years
- **Definitions given**: **GDP** = gross domestic product = value of all things produced within a country in a year. **Per capita** = total output ÷ population = output (income) per person. Used as a **proxy for how prosperous/poor a country is**.
- **Axes**: x-axis = time (over a thousand years, up to the present); y-axis = GDP per capita.
- **Series**: five countries — **Britain, Japan, Italy, China, India**.
- **Shape**: a **"hockey stick"** — flat for hundreds of years, then a sharp upward kink. The **blue line (Great Britain) ticks up first, in the late 18th century**; other countries follow later (a student noted the visible surge "around 1900" for all five).
- **Key reading of the graph**: before ~1750, growth was **not** absent, but came as **discrete, sudden jumps** after a new discovery (e.g. discovering that **potato** could be used for consumption → sudden jump in food availability → new plateau until the next discovery, maybe 100 years later). What is distinctive about the last 250 years is **year-on-year incremental growth (1%, 2%, some positive %)**.
- **Data sources** (relevant for a data-science programme): **Our World in Data (OWID)**; CORE Econ publishes the underlying data file/source for every graph — you can download the primary data.

#### Graph 2 — Real wages and population in Britain, 1260–2008
- **x-axis**: time, **1260 AD to 2008 AD** (~800 years).
- **Left y-axis**: **real wage index** — data on **skilled artisans/craftsmen in London** (from an economic historian's work).
- **Right y-axis**: **population in millions** (Britain).
- **"Real"** = **adjusted for inflation**; expressed in terms of something real (a bag of rice, food, gold) so it doesn't erode in value like nominal cash.
- **Shape**: the **wage line (orange) is flat for even longer** than GDP per capita, then **spikes up around ~1900**; population also rises sharply. Mirrors the hockey-stick pattern.

#### The Malthusian trap
- **Mechanism**: a discovery (e.g. potato) raises food/energy availability → **population rises** → per-person food availability returns to where it started.
- "Family income increases, but family size also increases → **per-person resources are unchanged**."
- **Malthus** wrote his **essay in 1798** describing this; he was pessimistic about humanity's prospects for raising per-person resources. This is why economics got the label **"the dismal science."**
- **Escaping the Malthusian trap** = income per person rises **faster** than population → everyone becomes richer per person. This is the phenomenon to be explained.

#### Adam Smith
- **1776** (≈250 years ago): **Adam Smith** (a Scot) wrote ***The Wealth of Nations*** — *before* Malthus's 1798 essay.
- He observed the *incipient* signs of the new phenomenon in Britain and predicted **per-person income can rise, with population growth not keeping up**, so everyone becomes richer per person.
- His question — reflected in the title — was: **what made Britain grow like this, and what can others do?**

#### The answer economists give
- **The recent rapid, sustained rise in income and living standards is due to TECHNOLOGICAL PROGRESS.**
- Progression: discrete early events (taming fire, discovery of cultivation, specific crops like potato) → **continuous year-on-year inventions/discoveries** (up to artificial intelligence today).
- ~200–250 years ago, major changes happened suddenly and put economies on a **continuous path** of technological discovery — hence **"revolution"**: the **Industrial Revolution**, starting in **Great Britain**; Western Europe prospered soon after; other regions took much longer.

## 3. Core Definitions and Formulas

> Write these exactly.

- **Net benefit**
  `Net benefit = Benefit − Cost`
  (both benefit and cost are **subjective** and differ across individuals)

- **Opportunity cost**
  **The opportunity cost of an action is the net benefit of the next-best alternative action.**
  `Opportunity cost of A = Net benefit of the next-best alternative B = (Benefit of B − Cost of B)`
  - Not "all other things you could do" — the **single highest-net-payoff alternative**.
  - Also called the **reservation option**: the chosen action's net benefit must be **at least** as large as this for the action to be worth taking.

- **Decision rule (version 1)**
  `Undertake action A if: Net benefit of A > Opportunity cost of A`

- **Economic cost**
  `Economic cost of an action = Direct (accounting) cost of the action + Opportunity cost of the action`

- **Decision rule (version 2 — equivalent)**
  `Undertake action A if: Benefit of A > Economic cost of A`
  - The two rules are **algebraically identical** (the direct cost sits on the left in version 1 and on the right in version 2). **They can never disagree.**

- **Economic rent**
  `Economic rent = Net benefit of the action − Opportunity cost`
  `= (Benefit − Direct cost) − Opportunity cost`
  `= Benefit − Economic cost`
  - Interpretation: the **excess** benefit you get from the chosen action **over and above** your next-best alternative.
  - **Why "rent"?** Economists define rent as **excess income** — excess relative to the next-best alternative. Comes from the classical view of a landlord's income from property as income received without doing anything further.

## 4. Worked Example — Concert vs Babysitting

**Setup:** You are deciding whether to attend a concert.

**Step 0 (first pass):**
- Ticket price (direct cost) = **$25**
- Subjective benefit of the concert = **$50**
- Net benefit = 50 − 25 = **$25**

**Step 1 — how to measure a subjective *benefit*:** ask yourself *"what is the maximum price I would pay for this ticket?"* Keep raising the price ($25, $30, $35, $40 …); the point at which you say "no, I will not pay that much" reveals your maximum valuation.

**Step 2 — the numbers used for the full example:**
- Benefit from concert (this person's maximum willingness to pay) = **$55**
- Ticket cost = **$25**
- **Net benefit of concert = 55 − 25 = $30**

**Step 3 — the next-best alternative: babysitting for a neighbour**
- Neighbour offers to pay = **benefit = $40**
- **How to measure a subjective *cost*:** ask *"what is the minimum amount my neighbour would have to pay me for me to accept the offer?"* (If the offer doesn't even cover your cost, you refuse.) Here that minimum = **$18** = cost (effort of getting the child to sleep).
- **Net benefit of babysitting = 40 − 18 = $22**

**Step 4 — opportunity cost**
- **Opportunity cost of attending the concert = net benefit of babysitting = $22** (babysitting is the next-best action).

**Step 5 — decision, method 1 (compare net benefits)**
- Net benefit of concert **$30** > Opportunity cost **$22** → **Go to the concert.**

**Step 6 — decision, method 2 (economic cost)**
- **Economic cost of concert = direct cost $25 + opportunity cost $22 = $47**
- Benefit of concert **$55** > Economic cost **$47** → **Go to the concert.**
- Note: the "gap" is **$8** under both methods (55 − 47 = 8; 30 − 22 = 8) → the methods are identical.

**Step 7 — economic rent**
- **Economic rent = 30 − 22 = $8** (equivalently 55 − 47 = $8).
- **Lesson to take away:** the accountant says your gain from the concert is **$30**. The economist says your true gain is **$8**, because you gave up a $22 net benefit from babysitting.

## 5. Economic Cost vs Accounting Cost (and the steel-firm case)

- An **accountant** would say the cost of the concert is **$25** — that is what is **objectively verifiable** (you can produce the ticket as proof).
- **Economic cost includes opportunity cost, which is based on subjective valuations** ($18 to babysit for me, maybe $10 or $20 for someone else) and is therefore **not objectively verifiable**.
- Purposes differ:
  - **Accounting cost**: an objective, verifiable cost picture for **shareholders, governance agencies, company executives**.
  - **Economic cost**: explains **why an action was chosen** — how a manager actually weighs alternatives. Managers routinely decide to buy/sell based on opportunity cost, but that calculation can't be shown on the books.
- *Student Q: if the two rules always agree, why define economic cost at all?* **Answer:** it is a re-arrangement/second vocabulary for the same phenomenon; but without it you cannot see the **difference between the accountant's and the economist's notion of cost**.

**Real-world case — US steel maker and the California power crisis (~2000):**
- Steel making requires a lot of power. A steel firm signed a **long-term power purchase agreement** at (illustrative figure) **$3 per unit**, which was the market standard at the time.
- During the **California power crisis around 2000**, the **spot price of power rose to about $30 per unit (≈10×)**.
- Now: **accounting cost of power = $3**, but **opportunity cost of using that power = $30** (the firm could sell the contracted power directly in the market instead of turning it into steel).
- Because the benefit from manufacturing steel was **less than the opportunity cost** of the power, the firm **shut its steel plant and became a power-trading firm**.
- Accounting cost alone would never reveal this.

## 6. Key Clarifications from Q&A (likely exam traps)

- When you **quote an opportunity cost**, you quote the **net benefit of the next-best alternative** — not the cost of the option you are considering. Both options are **netted** (benefit − cost); you then compare the two net benefits.
- **Net-benefit comparison and economic-cost comparison can never give opposite answers.** They are defined so as to align; the "gap" is the same number (here, $8).
- Both benefit **and** cost are **subjective** and vary by person; hence economic cost/rent are person-specific.
- **Reservation option** = the opportunity cost, i.e. the minimum net benefit the chosen action must deliver.

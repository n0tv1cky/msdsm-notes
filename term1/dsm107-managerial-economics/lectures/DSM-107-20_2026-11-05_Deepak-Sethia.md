# DSM-107 Managerial Economics, Session 20 (2026-11-05, Deepak Sethia)
## Study Notes: Sticky Wages, the Phillips Curve, Inflation Expectations, Credibility, and Stabilisation Policy

---

## 1. Overview

This session continues the macroeconomics discussion of unemployment and connects it to inflation and stabilisation policy. It starts by recapping voluntary and involuntary unemployment in the labour market. It then explains why nominal wages are "sticky" (slow to fall): labour contracts, information problems, menu costs, coordination failures, the insider/outsider problem, hiring and firing costs, and efficiency wages. This stickiness explains the shape of the short-run aggregate supply curve: flat below full employment and steep near or above it.

The lecture then introduces the **Phillips curve** (A.W. Phillips, UK data 1861–1913) and its short-run trade-off between unemployment and inflation. It extends this to the **expectations-augmented Phillips curve**, in which inflation can persist even at full employment because wage demands follow expected inflation. This leads to the ideas of **NAIRU**, **inflation inertia**, and the need to "engineer a recession" to lower expectations (the Paul Volcker episode). Three central-bank **credibility** scenarios follow, then the costs of anticipated vs. unanticipated inflation, why **deflation** is worse than inflation, and inflation targeting (RBI: $`4\% \pm 2\%`$).

The session closes with **counter-cyclical vs. pro-cyclical fiscal policy**, the three ways to finance a fiscal deficit, crowding out, and the effect of government borrowing on future generations.

## 2. Topics and Concepts (in order taught)

### 2.1 Recap: types of unemployment and "full employment"
- The previous session covered **structural, frictional and cyclical** unemployment.
- **Even at the full-employment level, not everyone is employed.** Frictional and structural unemployment still exist.

### 2.2 Labour market: voluntary vs. involuntary unemployment

**Graph described:**
- Axes: wage rate on the vertical axis; quantity of labour (workers hired) on the horizontal axis.
- **Labour supply curve:** flat at first, then becomes **steeper**. The steep part arises because, once more workers are needed, firms must compete for the same workers by paying higher wages. The lecturer said "cheaper"; this is an ASR error for "steeper".
- **Labour demand curve:** downward sloping. The initial equilibrium is at wage $`W^*`$.

**Voluntary unemployment**
- These are people who would join the workforce only at a wage *higher* than the prevailing $`W^*`$.
- Examples: someone who left a job because it was too far away; a fresh graduate still searching.

**Recession case**
- A demand slowdown shifts labour demand **leftward**, so firms want fewer workers.
- **If workers accepted wage cuts,** the wage would fall and the labour market would clear at a lower wage. Output would still be below potential.
- **If the wage stays at $`W^*`$** because workers resist nominal wage cuts:
  - Firms hire only the smaller quantity read off the new demand curve at $`W^*`$.
  - The gap between the number willing to work at $`W^*`$ and the number hired is **involuntary unemployment**. These workers (shown in red on the slide) are willing to work at the prevailing wage but cannot find jobs.
- Firms *would* hire more if wages fell, because lower costs would let them sell more output. Workers, however, resist the cuts.
- Workers resist cuts to **nominal** wages for morale and motivation reasons. They may also suspect the employer is "bluffing" to raise profits.
- The typical sequence:
  1. Workers refuse the cut and are laid off or quit.
  2. They search for jobs and discover a widespread recession.
  3. Only after a period of unemployment do they accept lower wages.
  4. Meanwhile there is large-scale unemployment.

### 2.3 Why wages (and prices) are sticky

1. **Labour contracts.**
   - Wages are fixed in advance, typically for 1, 3 or 5 years.
   - If inflation rises mid-contract, firms receive higher prices while paying fixed wages, so they are willing to hire more.
   - In a recession, firms cannot cut wages, so they **cut employment instead**.

2. **Other fixed nominal contracts.**
   - Examples: housing loans (e.g., 10-year), deposits, and long-dated government bonds (30 or 50 years) with fixed interest rates.
   - Higher inflation erodes the real value of the fixed nominal returns, but the terms were set in advance.

3. **Information problems (misperception).**
   - Example: a textile producer sees textile prices rising and believes demand for *their own* product has gone up.
     - A worker who earlier would not work for ₹10,000/month is now offered ₹10,000–₹11,000.
     - Alternatively, existing workers are paid extra for overtime.
     - Marginal cost rises, but this is viable because the price has risen on *all* units.
     - Wages on the existing production line are fixed in advance, so the **profit margin initially rises**.
   - Later, both the firm and the workers realise that *all* prices have risen. It is general inflation, not a demand shift specific to textiles.
     - Workers find that ₹10,000 buys less than expected.
     - At the next negotiation they demand ₹11,000 (about 10% more) on *average*, not just at the margin.
   - The price signal is clear for one's own product but noisy for the whole economy. Firms can be "fooled" into **over-expansion**.
   - Similarly, workers may confuse a nominal wage rise (₹10,000 to ₹11,000) with a real one, until they see that the goods they buy are also about 10% dearer.

4. **Menu costs.**
   - Example: a restaurant facing seasonal price spikes in onions and tomatoes does not reprint its menu immediately.
   - It revises prices only if the higher costs persist.
   - Reasons: printing is a cost, and price changes signal something to consumers who remember the old prices.

5. **Coordination failure.**
   - If one firm raises prices and others do not, it loses customers and market share. So firms hesitate to move first.

6. **Insider/outsider problem.**
   - Unions (insiders) and employers negotiate wages.
   - The unemployed (outsiders) would work for less but are **not at the bargaining table**.
   - Insiders reject wage cuts, so cutting wages is difficult.

7. **Hiring and firing (adjustment) costs.**
   - Firing is costly, and rehiring and training new workers when demand recovers is also costly.
   - Firms may therefore tolerate **unutilised capacity** rather than fire workers or cut wages.

8. **Efficiency wages / motivation (principal–agent problem).**
   - Firms may pay *above* market-clearing wages to motivate workers.
   - The resulting pool of unemployed people disciplines current workers: if fired for shirking, they would take longer to find a new job.
   - The employer must both pay salaries **and monitor** effort (e.g., through HR teams). An unemployed pool raises the cost of being caught cheating, which reduces monitoring needs.

### 2.4 Shape of the short-run aggregate supply (AS) curve

**Graph described:**
- Axes: output (real GDP) on the horizontal axis; price level on the vertical axis.
- The AS curve is **flat below full employment** and **steep near or above full employment** (potential output).

**Reasoning:**
- Below full employment, labour is unutilised. Firms can expand output without much increase in prices.
- At or beyond full employment, all workers are already hired. More output needs overtime or bidding workers away from other firms, which raises wages and costs.
- In this region, an increase in AD produces **little extra output and large price increases**.
- "Short run" here means far from potential; the curve gets steeper as the economy approaches potential output.

### 2.5 The Phillips curve

**Origin**
- A.W. Phillips plotted UK data for **1861–1913**.
- Horizontal axis: **unemployment rate**.
- Vertical axis: **rate of change of nominal wages** (annual wage growth); later versions use the inflation rate.

**Shape**
- Downward sloping and convex.
  - **Steep** at low unemployment: when labour markets are tight, workers demand and get big raises.
  - **Flat** at high unemployment: workers do not ask for raises because jobs are hard to find.

**Interpretation: a short-run trade-off**
- **Low unemployment with high inflation**, or
- **High unemployment with low inflation.** When there is widespread joblessness, inflation is not a problem, except when caused by supply shocks (to be covered later).

**Policy relevance**
- Politicians may treat the curve as a menu of policy choices.

### 2.6 Expectations-based inflation at full employment

**Worked logic (4% example)**
1. The economy is at full employment.
2. At the annual negotiation the worker argues:

```math
\text{Wage hike} = \text{productivity growth} + \text{expected inflation}\ (=4\%)
```
3. The employer sees that its own sales prices rose about 4% last year, so it grants the 4% inflation adjustment.
4. Next year, costs rise by 4% and firms raise prices by 4%. Inflation turns out to be 4%.
5. Next negotiation: the worker asks for 4% again, citing last year. The employer agrees, since workers' incomes also rose 4% and they can buy the output.
6. The cycle repeats.

**Conclusion**
- **Inflation can persist even at full employment** because it is **expectation-driven**.
- This is a **self-fulfilling prophecy**: "expectations translate into reality." If people expect 4%, inflation will be 4%.
- To reduce inflation, **expectations (thinking) must change**. Two ways:
  - **Recession:** workers remember 4% inflation, but with firms firing and the labour market weak, they accept smaller raises. Cost growth slows, price growth slows, and **inflationary expectations are crushed**.
  - **Boom (the reverse):** with strong labour demand, workers reject 4% and demand 6%. If the central bank accommodates (e.g., loose monetary policy), inflation rises to match.

### 2.7 Dynamic AD–AS with expected inflation (3% example)

**Graph**
- Axes: output on the horizontal axis; price level on the vertical axis.

**Steady state**
- Workers ask for a **3%** raise based on recent experience, so costs rise 3% and **AS shifts up by 3%**.
- Higher incomes plus monetary accommodation (money supply expanded about 3%) shift **AD up/right by 3%**.
- The price level rises 3%.
- After year 1 the price level is $`1.03\,P_0`$. After year 2 it is:

```math
P_2 = (1.03)^2 P_0
```
- **Output stays at potential.** AD shifts in step with AS.

**Without monetary accommodation**
- If money supply is not expanded, the cost increase is not matched by money "chasing goods".
- Demand falls short, and prices come under downward pressure (along with output and jobs).

**If AD rises faster than expected** (very loose monetary policy or fiscal expansion)
- Workers got 3% and costs rose 3%.
- Firms facing higher demand raise prices by **more than 3%** ("more money chasing the same goods").
- Next year's inflation exceeds 3%. This is an inflationary outbreak.

**Key point**
- You can create demand by "printing notes," but you cannot create supply.

### 2.8 Expectations-augmented Phillips curve, NAIRU, inflation inertia

**Inflation vs. price level**
- Inflation is the *rate of change* of the price level.
- If prices stay where they are, inflation is $`0`$. Prices do not have to fall for inflation to be zero.

**Steady state**
- With 3% every year (wage hike, then costs, then prices), the economy stays at **potential output / full employment** on a Phillips curve whose height reflects 3% expected inflation.

**Demand surprise**
- Higher-than-expected demand moves the economy **along** the curve (e.g., to point B):
  - Unemployment falls below the natural rate.
  - Firms hire more and pay slightly more.
  - Inflation accelerates from about 3% to about 4–5%.
- Next year the **Phillips curve shifts upward**, because expectations are now about 5%.

**Key definitions**
- **The height of the Phillips curve equals expected inflation.**
- **NAIRU (Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment):** the unemployment rate at which inflation neither accelerates nor decelerates. The economy is at full employment with steady inflation (3%, 3%, 3%, ...). The transcript's "non-accelerating rate of inflation / Nairu" refers to this.
- **Inflation inertia:** we expect inflation simply because we have had it. The lecturer attributed this point to **Robert Solow**.

### 2.9 Bringing inflation down: engineering a recession (disinflation)

**Class Q&A: how do you get back down from point C?**
- Students suggested reducing demand. "Importing" was rejected.
- The correct answer: **raise interest rates**.
  1. Higher loan rates mean people stop buying cars and houses.
  2. Jobs are lost in autos, construction, cement and steel.
  3. At the next negotiation, those still employed **don't ask for raises** despite past inflation.

**Path on the graph**
- The economy moves down along the curve from B/C to a point like D (higher unemployment, lower output).
- Next year the Phillips curve **shifts down** to the new, lower expectations.
- Demand is kept low and unemployment high, so expectations step down repeatedly.

**Numerical illustration**
- Expectations start at 5%. Unemployment is created, so workers accept a 4% raise; costs and prices grow 4%.
- The Phillips curve shifts down even though inflation is still positive.
- Next year workers accept 3%, and so on.

**Paul Volcker (US Federal Reserve)**
- Raised US short-term interest rates to around **14–15%**.
- At mortgage rates of 16–17%, nobody buys houses. Housing workers lose jobs, wage growth collapses, cost growth slows, and inflation falls.

**The cost**
- Disinflation requires **sacrificing output and jobs**: a "cruel" trade-off and a lot of waste.
- When the Fed says "the labour market is tight," the signal is that rates will rise. In effect it will create unemployment, though it will not say so.
- Once workers accept roughly 2–3% raises (starting from about 7–8%), the economy is stable and demand is allowed to recover.

### 2.10 Does it always work? The role of central bank credibility

This was a student question. The answer: it depends partly on **credibility**.

**Illustration**
- The RBI Governor says inflation has been about 10% but the target is now **2%**, and he commits not to over-expand money supply.
- The worker asks for 10% (past trend). The employer points to the governor's announcement, and they agree on 2%.
- Costs and prices rise 2%. The governor delivered only 2% money growth, so it works and everyone "thanks" each other.

**Three scenarios**

| # | Central bank | Public | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Credible** (promises 2% and delivers) | **Believes** | Wage hikes about 2%, inflation about 2%, **no unemployment cost**. The **most desirable** case. |
| 2 | **Bluffs** (promises 2% but prints more) | **Believes** | Demand rises faster than promised and prices rise more than 2%. Credibility is lost, so people won't believe next time. |
| 3 | **Credible** (delivers 2%) | **Doesn't believe** | Workers still demand 10%, so **AS shifts up about 10%** while **AD shifts only about 2%**. Goods go unsold and unemployment results. Only then do people accept the governor was right. |

**Why might a central bank bluff? Seigniorage.**
- A ₹2,000 note costs about ₹5–10 to print.
- Profit to the RBI ≈ ₹2,000 − ₹10 = **₹1,990** per note.

**Analogy: municipal traffic rules**
- If a city announces "drive on this side only," and people believe and comply, it becomes the norm.
- If the commissioner or police drive on the wrong side, or announced fines are never levied, credibility collapses.
- **Lesson:** policy should be credible. Deliver what you announce, and losses will be much lower.

### 2.11 Anticipated vs. unanticipated inflation

**Anticipated inflation**
- Expected 3% and actual 3%, so the 3% wage hike keeps **real wages constant**.
- There are still some costs, such as revising contracts and prices every year. These are the "true" costs even of anticipated inflation.

**Unanticipated inflation (wages)**
- Expected 3% but actual 10%. The real wage change is approximately:

```math
\%\Delta W_{real} \approx \%\Delta W_{nominal} - \pi = 3\% - 10\% = -7\%
```
- Workers on fixed contracts **lose**. Firms selling at 10% higher prices **gain**.
- So unanticipated inflation redistributes *away from* those receiving fixed nominal payments.

**Unanticipated inflation (loans)**
- The nominal rate is set to give the desired real rate:

```math
i = r + \pi^e = 3\% + 3\% = 6\%
```
- If actual inflation is 10%, the realised real rate is:

```math
r_{actual} \approx i - \pi = 6\% - 10\% = -4\%
```
- The **borrower gains** by repaying in "cheaper rupees." The **lender loses** purchasing power.
- This is a **redistribution from lenders to borrowers**.

**High inflation brings uncertainty**
- At 25% inflation, an error of $`\pm 5`$ points (20% or 30%) is very costly. At 3%, an error of 2–4% matters little.
- Higher inflation means greater **volatility and uncertainty**, which distorts markets.

**Inflation targeting**
- RBI's framework targets **$`4\% \pm 2\%`$**, i.e., a tolerance band of 2% to 6%. Exact precision is impossible, but the band is a commitment.
- A band like $`15\% \pm 5\%`$ would make life "interesting" (i.e., bad).

### 2.12 Why deflation is more dangerous than inflation

**Problems with falling prices**
1. **Costs must fall,** which means cutting nominal wages. This is hard because wages are sticky.
2. **Postponed spending:** consumers delay purchases, and firms delay building factories ("build it tomorrow"), and tomorrow may never come.
3. **Zero lower bound:** in deflation, interest rates are already near zero and cannot go meaningfully negative. Monetary policy cannot stimulate investment further.

**Example: a ₹100 loan at 0% interest with $`\pi = -2\%`$**
- ₹100 is repaid, but it buys more than before. The real rate is:

```math
r \approx i - \pi = 0 - (-2\%) = +2\%
```
- The borrower's selling prices are falling too, so repayment is harder.

**Policy conclusions**
- **The best policy is to stay away from deflation.** About 2–3% is a healthy inflation rate.
- The US and Europe target **2%**; India targets about **4%**. The choice depends on country context.
- Indian context: annual increases in **Minimum Support Prices** (MSP) for farmers feed into food inflation. Moderate inflation is part of a "social bargain."

### 2.13 Counter-cyclical vs. pro-cyclical fiscal policy

**Graph described**
- Axes: **time** on the horizontal axis; **output gap** on the vertical axis.
- An output gap of $`0`$ means the economy is at full employment.
- The blue "business as usual" line oscillates around zero (booms above, recessions below).

**Pro-cyclical policy (wrong)**
- In a boom, the government collects more tax and **spends more**, which amplifies the boom.
- In a recession, it earns less and **spends less**, which further destroys aggregate demand and deepens the recession.
- This **amplifies** the swings.

**Counter-cyclical policy (correct)**
- In a recession, **spend more** despite lower revenue to lift demand toward full employment.
- In a boom, **raise taxes / cut spending**, because the private sector is already spending.
- This **smooths** the cycle.

**General rule for demand shortfalls**
- The government should spend, or the monetary authority should cut interest rates.

### 2.14 Fiscal deficits and how they are financed

**Definition**

```math
\text{Fiscal deficit} = \text{Government spending} - \text{Government revenue}
```
- The **primary deficit** in particular adds to demand each year.

**Rule of thumb**
- In normal times, stay roughly balanced. In recessions, run larger deficits.

**Three financing options**

1. **Print money**
   - More money chases the same goods.
   - With unemployment present, output can rise without much inflation.
   - At full employment, it just raises prices.

2. **Borrow domestically: crowding out**
   - **Graph:** quantity of savings/investment on the horizontal axis; interest rate on the vertical axis.
   - Government borrowing reduces the savings available to private investors. Supply of loanable funds shifts left.
   - The interest rate rises, and private investment (and future output) falls.

3. **Borrow from abroad**
   - The loans must be repaid in future.
   - A past **current account deficit** was financed by a **capital account surplus** (borrowing).
   - Future repayment requires a **current account surplus**: export more, then repay.

### 2.15 Who bears the burden of government borrowing?

**Repayment**
- Future taxes must be raised to pay bondholders.
- **Domestic bondholders:** an internal redistribution.
- **Foreign bondholders:** the country must export to repay.

**Effect on future generations**
- Domestic borrowing at **full employment** diverts resources from private investment (cement, steel, factories) to government uses.
- **Future generations inherit a smaller capital stock** (fewer factories).

**When deficits are fine**
- Deficits are acceptable to the extent they do not reduce overall investment.
- **Below full employment:** firms are not investing anyway. Deficit spending does not crowd out and may **crowd in** investment by raising demand and profitability.
- **At full employment:** deficits should be lower.

### 2.16 Concluding message
- **Macroeconomic policy is about stabilising output at potential output.**
- Use macro tools actively when the economy deviates from potential **on either side**.
- When the economy is at full employment, maintain the status quo rather than intervene.

## 3. Key Formulas Summary

- Wage-demand rule:

```math
\text{Wage hike} = \text{productivity growth} + \pi^e
```
- Price level with steady 3% inflation:

```math
P_t = P_0 (1.03)^t
```
- Real wage change:

```math
\%\Delta W_{real} \approx \%\Delta W_{nom} - \pi
```
- Fisher relation:

```math
i = r + \pi^e \quad (\text{example: } 3\% + 3\% = 6\%)
```
- Realised real interest rate:

```math
r \approx i - \pi \quad (\text{examples: } 6\% - 10\% = -4\%;\ \ 0 - (-2\%) = +2\%)
```
- Fiscal deficit:

```math
FD = G - T
```
- Seigniorage per ₹2,000 note: about $`2000 - 10 = 1990`$ rupees.
- RBI target: $`\pi^* = 4\% \pm 2\%`$.

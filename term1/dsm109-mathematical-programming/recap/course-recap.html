Why OR, and why not brute force

Enumerating every option stops working long before problems get realistic, so we need systematic methods that also guarantee they found the best answer.

WHAT OR IS

OR / management science
Applying advanced analytical methods to make better decisions. OR is the more mathematical side, MS the applied side. They are essentially one field.
why "operations"
From WWII military operations, not operations management (a student's guess, corrected). Teams of mathematicians, logicians, statisticians and code-breakers.
the pattern
Max/min an objective s.t. constraints. Military: min travel time under limited ammunition and manpower. Business: max profit or min cost under limited hours, budget and resources.
Dantzig → simplex
Worked for the US Air Force and invented the simplex method for LP. It is exponential in the worst case on paper but very fast in practice.

CONVEX VS NON-CONVEX

single airline price
Demand $D(p) = a - bp$, so revenue is $R(p) = ap - bp^2$ on $0 \le p \le a/b$.
$R' = a - 2bp = 0 \Rightarrow p^* = \frac{a}{2b}$, and $R'' = -2b < 0$ confirms a max.
$Q^* = a/2$, $R^* = a^2/(4b)$.
three routes to p*
Calculus (closed form), plotting (the parabola peaks midway between its roots 0 and a/b), or step search. A price ±1–2% off p* barely changes revenue, because p* is a stationary point.
convex
One peak (maximising a concave function). Search can't get trapped: every local optimum is global.
non-convex
Many peaks. Search can stop at a wrong local max: a local optimum may not be global.
course scope
LPs generally have no closed form, so calculus isn't the tool here.

n! — assignment, TSP

ASSIGNMENT LOOKS HARDER, IS EASIER (S2)

assignment as an LP
$\min \sum_i\sum_j d_{ij}x_{ij}$ s.t. row sums = 1 (each dozer to one site), column sums = 1 (each site gets one dozer), $x_{ij} \ge 0$. Solve with Simplex LP. An arbitrary 1A 2B 3C 4D gives 255; Solver finds 150.
integrality property
The constraint structure makes the LP optimum 0/1 without declaring binary. Simplex never enumerates n! options, yet still guarantees optimality. Polynomial, so 1,000×1,000 or even 10,000×10,000 instances solve in milliseconds.
subset-sum as a binary IP
$\sum_i w_i y_i = 40$, $y_i \in \{0,1\}$, with the objective left empty.
If you forget binary, you get ≈5.7 copies of one item (5.71×7 ≈ 40).
With binary: 7+10+3+14+6 = 40 (5 items). Minimising the item count gives 14+17+9 = 40 (3 items).
The binary requirement is what makes it NP-hard.
branch and bound
Solve the LP relaxation first (that is where the 5.7 came from), track upper and lower bounds, then branch on a fractional variable (0.4 → "= 0" | "= 1").
Solver returns one solution and is deterministic. Gurobi and CPLEX may randomise which variable they branch on.

WHY LP IS SO POPULAR (S2)

1 · applicable
Even nonlinear problems can be approximated linearly, e.g. with tangents.
2 · efficient
Karmarkar (1984) gave a polynomial-time interior-point method, and LPs with millions of variables solve fast. Khachiyan's ellipsoid method (1979) was the first polynomial algorithm; Karmarkar's was the first practical one.
3 · insights
Shadow price (what to pay for an overtime hour), reduced cost (which product mixes are viable) and allowable increase/decrease (does a price swing change the mix?). "The optimal solution is not the most interesting part; the insights are."

Where OR comes from (S1):

  • Enigma: simplify the password to 10 letters and there are $26^{10} \approx 1.41\times10^{14}$ settings, reset every midnight. Turing's early computer could not brute-force that.
  • What cracked it was structure: the 6 a.m. weather report always contained known words ("weather", "Heil Hitler"), which cut the search space drastically. Recommended film: The Imitation Game.
  • OR scientists also went in with the troops at Normandy.

"Don't get absorbed in the math and miss the bigger picture" (S1, repeated S2):

  • Single price: revenue is the largest rectangle p × Q under the demand line, and nothing beats p* with one price. But the whole area under the curve is money on the table.
  • Price discrimination: airlines add priority boarding and snacks, which cost little but are valued. HUL sells Surf Excel variants (hand wash, top-loader, front-loader); the front-loader one costs ≈₹10–20/kg more for marginal differences. Hotels do the same.
  • JIT vs EOQ: US firms computed EOQ ($Q^* = \sqrt{2DS/H}$, not derived) to balance holding cost against ordering cost. Toyota asked "why not order one unit?" and that question led to lean / just-in-time.

Why you need a guarantee of optimality (S1):

  • Flight puzzle: trial and error stalls at ₹34,000 with no sign that ₹30,000 exists. That is embarrassing if a boss or client finds it in 2 minutes. "A guarantee of optimality is priceless." (Worked below.)
  • Dozers: picking the closest free dozer site by site gives 5+45+55+85 = 190 km. A student's reassignment, 45+35+55+15 = 150 km, saves (190−150)/190 ≈ 21%: "about 20%, a huge number". Greedy can be badly off.

Growth stories (S1, S2):

  • 50! on an exaflop machine (S1): 3.04×10⁶⁴ ÷ 10¹⁸ per second ≈ 3.04×10⁴⁶ s ≈ 9.6×10³⁸ years. The universe is ≈1.4×10¹⁰ years old, so a machine running since the Big Bang would have checked a vanishing fraction (~10⁻²⁹) of the options.
  • Chessboard and wheat (S2): Sissa asked for 1 grain on square 1, then 2, 4, 8, … doubling. Square k holds $2^{k-1}$ grains, for a total of $2^{64}-1 \approx 1.8\times10^{19}$. The king underestimated compounding: "think about the last square."
  • Doubling hardware (S2): four years of progress buys an exponential algorithm exactly one more city.

WORKED EXAMPLES

Five weeks in Delhi: fly Indore→Delhi Monday and Delhi→Indore Wednesday each week. One-way ₹5,000; same-week round trip ₹8,000; round trip spanning a weekend ₹6,000. What is the cheapest plan, and how do you know?S1 22 Oct
  1. There are 10 legs: outbound $O_1,\dots,O_5$ and return $R_1,\dots,R_5$.
  2. (a) Five same-week round trips $(O_k, R_k)$: $5 \times 8{,}000 = ₹40{,}000$.
  3. (b) Pair $(R_k, O_{k+1})$ for k = 1..4 as Delhi→Indore→Delhi weekend round trips, plus one-ways $O_1$ and $R_5$: $4\times 6{,}000 + 2\times 5{,}000 = ₹34{,}000$.
  4. (c) Club $O_1$ and $R_5$ into an Indore→Delhi→Indore round trip, which also spans weekends: $5 \times 6{,}000 = ₹30{,}000$.
  5. Lower bound: a ticket covers at most 2 legs, and the cheapest way to cover 2 legs is ₹6,000 (vs ₹8,000 or 2×₹5,000). So $\text{Cost} \ge \frac{10}{2}\times 6{,}000 = ₹30{,}000$. Plan (c) meets the bound, so it is optimal.
  6. Alternate optimum: pair $(O_k, R_{k+1})$ for k = 1..4 and club $R_1$ with $O_5$. This also costs ₹30,000, so the optimum is not unique.

₹30,000, proved optimal by the lower bound (multiple optimal plans exist)

Truck loading with n = 10 items: to answer "no subset sums to exactly 40 t" by enumeration, how many subsets must you check in the worst case?S2 23 Oct
  1. Count by size: singles $\binom{10}{1} = 10$, pairs $\binom{10}{2} = 45$, triples 120, then 210, 252, 210, 120, 45, 10, 1.
  2. Sum: $10+45+120+210+252+210+120+45+10+1 = 1023$.
  3. Shortcut: $\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k} = 2^n$; drop the empty set to get $2^{10}-1 = 1023$.
  4. A "yes" can stop early (e.g. 17+14+9 = 40). Only a "no" forces the full count. At n = 100 the count is $2^{100}-1$.

Formulating correctly

Variables, objective and constraints, each with units. Then the Merton Truck lesson: a wrong profit expression gives a confidently wrong optimum.

ANATOMY OF A FORMULATION

decision variable
What management decides, or what Solver should find. The objective and every constraint must be writable in them. E.g. $x_1$ = tables per week (units).
parameter
Known data: coefficients 4, 3, 2, 1, 7, 5 and capacities 240, 100.
three parts, with units
variables (units/week) · objective (profit/week) · constraints (hours used ≤ hours available)
linear
Every variable appears to power 1 in the objective and the constraints, so use Simplex LP.

SIGN RESTRICTIONS: DECIDE FROM THE USE CASE

  • $x_1 = -10$ would mean buying tables from the market.
  • Resources are hours. Dismantling a bought table gives no hours back, so $x_1, x_2 \ge 0$.
  • If the resources were materials (240 kg iron, 100 kg aluminium), dismantling would return material, so negatives might make sense.
  • Portfolio: +10 lakh = invest, −10 lakh = short. Negatives allowed.

EXCEL SOLVER RECIPE (S2)

  • Coefficient rows (objective, each resource) plus a RHS column.
  • Yellow decision cells. Trial values are only for testing formulas: (1, 2) → profit 17, woodwork 10.
  • Objective: =SUMPRODUCT(coeffs, decision cells)
  • LHS: =SUMPRODUCT(row, $B$3:$C$3). The $ refs let you copy it down.
  • Max · By Changing = yellow cells · LHS ≤ RHS (all rows at once) · tick Make Unconstrained Variables Non-Negative · Simplex LP

Profit A vs Profit B: the conflict at Merton (S3):

  • The sales manager said "we are losing 1,205 dollars on each Model 101 we sell", so drop it. The controller said make more Model 101s so the fixed cost spreads thinner. Both argued from apportioned costs.
  • Profit A (apportioned) $= -1205x_1 + 2070x_2$. Profit B (contribution) $= 3000x_1 + 5000x_2 - 8{,}600{,}000$.
  • Test any profit expression at zero output: the fixed cost doesn't vanish. A gives 0 and B gives −8.6M, so B is correct.
  • A and B agree only at the apportioning point (1000, 1500), where both equal 1.9M. The whole analysis is about changing $x$, so A is useless.
  • Delhi–Indore flight: 100 seats, Rs 4,00,000 fixed cost, Rs 200 per passenger. Apportioned: $4{,}200x$. Correct: $200x + 4{,}00{,}000$. They agree only at $x = 100$ (Rs 4,20,000).
  • Apportioning is "made-up logic". EA's 1.7M was split 25% / 75% by hours, giving 425 and 850 per truck. Fixed cost should not be apportioned at all.
  • "Garbage in, garbage out": fix the expression before you optimise. "Once you resolve the conflict, about 80–90% of the job is done; the rest is analysis." This error is common in industry.

Method 1, the swap argument, and why it isn't enough (S3):

  • EA is full, so to make more Model 101s you must free EA hours. One swap = −1 Model 102, +2 Model 101. EA net 0, MS +2 h, A101 +4 h, A102 −3 h.
  • Each swap earns $2(3000) - 5000 = +1000$. MS has 1,000 spare hours, so 500 swaps are possible. That adds +0.5M and moves the plan to (2000, 1000).
  • Three drawbacks: tedious (start afresh whenever the data change), impractical for large problems (exponentially many trade-offs), and it can't be automated or easily taught. That is why we use an LP plus a solver.
  • Don't add capacity before solving optimally. The apparent bottlenecks (EA, A102) are not the binding ones at the optimum (EA, MS).

Graphical method and why corners

Draw the region, list its corners and slide the iso-profit line, then learn the three theorems that explain why the best plan always sits at a corner and why convex problems are easy.

GRAPHICAL RECIPE (2 VARIABLES ONLY)

  • Each $\le$ constraint = a line (the equality) plus one feasible side (half-plane).
  • Intercepts: set the other variable to 0, then join the two points. Engine assembly x₁ + 2x₂ = 4000 → (4000, 0), (0, 2000).
  • Test the origin: $0 + 0 \le 4000$ is true → the origin's side is feasible. Shade what satisfies every constraint, including $x_1, x_2 \ge 0$.
  • Corners: solve pairs of lines and keep only the points that satisfy all the other constraints.
  • Evaluate Z at each corner (Method 1), or slide the iso-line (Method 2).
  • 3 variables needs a 3-D plot; 4 or more can't be drawn. The method is for intuition.

THREE METHODS + ALTERNATE OPTIMA

Method 1 · corner enumeration
List all corners, evaluate Z, pick the best. Needs a bounded region (the "qualification").
Method 2 · iso-objective line
Every point on it has the same Z ("iso" = same). Slide it parallel in the improving direction until it is about to leave the region. A line leaving a polygon always exits at a corner.
alternate optima
Objective parallel to an edge → the line exits through two corners at once → the whole edge is optimal. Solver reports only one corner.
Method 3 · local search
Stop at a corner that beats all its adjacent corners. Merton: D (11M) beats C (10.5M) and E (10M), so D is optimal without looking at A, B or F.

DEFINITIONS (S4)

solution
any point in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane, feasible or not
feasible solution
satisfies all constraints, including non-negativity. Example: (1000, 1000). There are infinitely many.
optimal solution
a feasible solution with the best Z
convex set
for any $P, Q \in S$, the whole segment $PQ$ lies in $S$. A notch or dent breaks this.
convex combination
$\lambda x_P + (1-\lambda)x_Q$ with $0 \le \lambda \le 1$ (a weighted average)
extreme (corner) point
Cannot be written as a weighted average of two other distinct points of the region. An edge midpoint = 0.5·one end + 0.5·the other, so it is not a corner.

THE THREE THEOREMS (S4)

Theorem 1 · the LP region is convex
Each linear constraint keeps one side of a fully extended line. A dent would need both sides of some line to be feasible. Proof: $Ax_P \le b$ and $Ax_Q \le b$; multiply by $\lambda$ and $1-\lambda$ (both $\ge 0$, so the direction is kept) and add: $$A\big(\lambda x_P + (1-\lambda)x_Q\big) \le \lambda b + (1-\lambda)b = b$$
Theorem 2 · if an optimum exists, a corner is optimal
Z is linear: $$Z(\lambda x_B + (1-\lambda)x_C) = \lambda Z(x_B) + (1-\lambda)Z(x_C)$$ Every point is a convex combination of corners, so its Z is a weighted average of corner Z's and can't beat the best corner. Midpoint of BC: 9M = average of 7.5M and 10.5M.
Theorem 3 · convex problem: local = global
Any local optimum is the global optimum. This gives Method 3, which is the simplex idea.

CONVEX OPTIMISATION (S5)

convex function
$f(\lambda x_a + (1-\lambda)x_b) \le \lambda f(x_a) + (1-\lambda)f(x_b)$. Every chord lies on or above the curve (a bowl). Concave = reversed ($-f$ convex). Linear is both.
convex problem
$\min f(x)$ s.t. $g_i(x) \le 0$, with $f$ and all $g_i$ convex. "Convex ≤ constant" gives a convex region, e.g. the disc $x^2 + y^2 \le 5$ (RHS reconstructed). Max of a concave $f$ = min of $-f$, so it is convex too.
every LP is convex
whether you max or min
local vs global
local: at least as good as every feasible point in a small ball · global: at least as good as every feasible point
infeasible · unbounded
no feasible point · no finite optimum. Otherwise at least one corner is optimal.
the takeaway
Non-convexity, not non-linearity, makes problems hard.

Why the pictures matter (S4):

  • "The optimal solution is not the most interesting part; the insights are": bottlenecks, shadow prices and allowable ranges, which can be used to negotiate. Vatsa said you need the graphical method to read Solver's Sensitivity Report properly.
  • A student asked why only corners need checking. The answer is Method 2 (the line always leaves at a corner) and Theorem 2.
  • Generic polygon a, b, c, d…: he said "improvement", not max/min. Change the improving direction and d, a or c becomes optimal. Push past the last corner and nothing on the line is feasible.
  • Solver does not brute-force the corners. It walks between adjacent corners and stops at a local (= global) optimum: the Simplex LP engine. The link to simplex is inferred from the Solver setting.

Easy vs hard, drawn in S5:

  • Dented polygon A–E: corner C beats D and everything in a small ball around it, so C is a local optimum. E is strictly better. A local search stopping at C is wrong, and this can't happen in a convex problem.
  • $e^{-x}$ is convex but has no minimiser: it keeps decreasing, and its "minimum" is only approached as $x \to \infty$. A student proposed "$f(a) \le f(a^-)$ and $f(a) \le f(a^+)$" as a definition; that defines a local min at a point, not convexity.
  • Convex NLP (curved convex region, linear objective): the tangency point is both local and global, and convex NLPs are today about as easy as LPs. Dent the region and a tangency point can be only local.

Why is maximising a bowl-shaped f on $[x_L, x_U]$ hard when minimising it is easy?S5 6 Nov
  1. Minimise: from any start, move downhill until you reach the bottom. It is both the local and the global min.
  2. Maximise: start left of the bottom and you climb to $x_L$; start right and you reach $x_U$. Each endpoint beats everything in a small ball around it, so both are local maxima.
  3. A local search stops at whichever endpoint it reaches. To be sure, you must compare every such candidate, and in large problems there can be very many.
  4. Maximising a convex $f$ is not of the form "min convex s.t. convex ≤ 0". Flip it to a concave hill (picture 3) and the problem is easy again.

Where "reduced cost" comes from (S7):

  • George Dantzig, working for the US Air Force (Pentagon), had to feed soldiers at minimum cost. This is the diet problem: $\min \sum_j c_j x_j$ s.t. $L_i \le \sum_j a_{ij}x_j \le U_i$, $x \ge 0$, where $x_j$ = servings of food j.
  • If milk is 0 in the optimal diet, other foods supply its nutrients more cheaply. Its reduced cost is how much milk's price per serving must be reduced before it enters the diet. Hence the name.

Shadow-price signs, duality, and an introduction to IP

This module covers why each shadow price has the sign it does, how every LP carries a dual whose variables are those shadow prices, and what changes when the answer must be a whole number.

REGION → SIGN (S09)

the principle
Region expands → the optimum can't worsen, because the old corner is still available. Region shrinks → the optimum can't improve, because you may lose the old corner.
RHS +1 (that constraint alone)
≤ expands: $3x_1+2x_2\le 8 \to \le 9$; also $-3x_1-8x_2\le -16 \to \le -15$, so the coefficient signs don't matter.
≥ shrinks: $3x_1+5x_2\ge 25 \to \ge 26$.
= shifts: $x_2=4 \to x_2=5$, a different line.
better / worse
max: worse = lower profit · min: worse = higher cost
standard constraints
$\le$ for max (profit is limited by resources) and $\ge$ for min → shadow price $\ge 0$. Non-standard → $\le 0$. Equality → unrestricted.

PRIMAL ↔ DUAL (S09)

dual variables
= the primal's shadow prices. Each primal constraint gives one dual variable; each primal variable gives one dual constraint.
conversion
max ↔ min · primal RHS → dual objective · primal objective → dual RHS · transpose: primal column $j$ → dual row $j$. The dual of the dual is the primal.
sign matching
standard variable ($\ge 0$) ↔ standard constraint · non-standard ↔ non-standard · unrestricted variable ↔ equality. Shortcut: read the dual-variable signs off the shadow-price table.
which to solve
Graphical: the 2-variable one; read the other off it. Simplex: the one with fewer constraints. A primal with 2 vars and 30 constraints → 32 vars / 30 equations. Its dual → 32 vars / 2 equations.
optimality test
At a point where both primal and dual are feasible, the solution is optimal. Transportation and network algorithms stop on this test.

INTEGER PROGRAMMING (S09)

ILP / MILP
The objective and constraints are still linear, but all variables (pure ILP) or some (MILP) must be integers. In Solver this is an extra int or bin constraint.
counts
3.5 cars or 4.5 trucks makes no sense. Kg of milk, curd or yogurt can be fractional.
binary 0–1
knapsack / truck loading · capital budgeting · facility location (you can't put 0.35 of a warehouse here and 0.65 there)
logic via binaries

The smelly resource: can more of a resource hurt profit? (S08)

  • Problem: $\max 20x_1 + x_2$ with $x_1+x_2\le 10$ and $x_2\le 4$. Here $x_1$ is very profitable and $x_2$ is barely profitable. The optimum is $(10,0)$, $Z=200$, with $u_1=20$ and $u_2=0$.
  • Now make it $x_2 = 4$. Picture the resource as obnoxious, smelly or chemically reactive: you are forced to use all of it, and only $x_2$ can consume it. The region collapses to the segment from (0,4) to (6,4). The optimum moves to (6,4), $Z=124$.
  • One more unit of it forces more of the weak $x_2$. Through $x_1+x_2\le 10$, that crowds out the strong $x_1$. $Z$ falls to 105, so $u_2=-19$.
  • The rule: a $\le$ constraint in a max problem is never negative, because extra resource can always be left unused. An $=$ constraint can go either way. A $\ge$ constraint can be negative, like the quality constraints in Red Brand Canners.

"Buy my resources": where the dual comes from (S09):

  • You offer to take over my 18 h of resource 1 and 11 h of resource 2, at prices $u_1, u_2$ per hour. You pay $18u_1+11u_2$, and naturally you want to minimise that.
  • The offer must make producing pointless for me. Product 1 earns 8 and uses 3 h + 2 h, so I give it up only if $3u_1+2u_2\ge 8$. The same test applies to products 2 and 3.
  • That is the dual: "two sides of the same coin". Solving an LP gives the plan and the worth of every unit of each resource.
  • In Excel's Sensitivity Report, the primal shows $u_1,u_2$ under Constraints. In the dual's report the roles swap: $x_1,x_2,x_3$ become the shadow prices.

Merton's dual, solved in Solver (S10):

  • Dual: $\min 4000u_1+6000u_2+5000u_3+4500u_4$ s.t. $u_1+2u_2+2u_3\ge 3000$, $2u_1+2u_2+3u_4\ge 5000$, $u\ge 0$. Build it in Excel with Copy → Paste Special → Transpose.
  • Optimum $u=(2000,500,0,0)$ and $W=11$M, equal to the primal's $Z$. The dual's variables are the primal's shadow prices; the dual's shadow prices are the primal's plan (2000, 1000). What sits under Constraints in one report sits under Variables in the other.
  • Which to solve: the one with fewer constraints, since each needs a slack variable. 2 variables and 100 constraints means 102 variables in 100 equations.
  • For huge models, Gurobi and CPLEX race primal simplex, dual simplex and the interior-point method (Karmarkar, 1984) on different cores and stop when the first one finishes.

Why logic needs a trick (S10):

      
    
        
  • Solver treats every constraint as AND. The feasible region is the overlap of all of them, so "either RM2a or RM2b" can't be typed in directly. The binary $y$ and a big $M$ make one of the two constraints harmless.
    • 
        
  • With $y=1$, RM2a reads $3(40)-1000=-880\le 80$: "redundant by a big margin". That is big-M doing its job.
    • 
        
  • An IF or MAX formula makes the model non-linear, and Simplex LP refuses it ("linearity conditions are not satisfied"). Split it into linear pieces instead.
    • 
        
  • The truth table for "5 if 1 or 3": when neither 1 nor 3 is chosen, 5 is free. So "$x_1+x_3\ge 1$ and $x_5\ge 1$" is wrong: it forces project 5 every time.

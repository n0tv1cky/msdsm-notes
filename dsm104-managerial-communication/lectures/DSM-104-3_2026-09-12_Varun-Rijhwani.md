## 1. Overview

This session opens the **Managerial Writing** module, following the earlier modules on verbal and non-verbal communication. It begins with a class discussion on *why writing still needs to be studied in the age of generative AI* (review/judgement, data-leakage and confidentiality risks, AI hallucination, machine-heuristic bias, loss of critical thinking, "AI slop" sameness in emails). The instructor's core claim is that **writing reveals leadership** — a grammatically perfect email can still make a manager sound defensive, vague or evasive, because writing signals judgement, emotional maturity, accountability and credibility. The class then diagnoses a badly written managerial email, learns the **Seven C's of workplace communication** as a drafting checklist, tests whether an email can satisfy all seven C's and still fail (yes — tone, word choice, emotional appeal and above all *ownership*), and works through a comparative exercise on two real CEO layoff emails plus Tim Cook's Apple 50th-anniversary email. These lead to **Level 5 Leadership**: *look in the mirror when things go wrong; look out of the window when things go right.* The session closes with the release of the **individual elevator-pitch video assignment (40% weightage, due 3 October 2026)**.

### 2.2 Why study writing when AI exists? (opening discussion)
Student points, endorsed by the instructor:
- **Someone must review AI output** — a human judgement call is always needed; knowing how to write sharpens the *rubric in your head* for reviewing.
- **Confidentiality / data leakage:** pasting company material into AI tools feeds it into model training. Sensitive documents must be written yourself. AI is safer for low-risk items (e.g. grammar check on a leave email).
- **Technical/domain accuracy (SAP client example):** A student described a client-facing SAP implementation where the client repeatedly quoted ChatGPT-suggested "solutions" that were technically impossible. This had to be escalated to seniors because it was derailing the implementation. Lesson: AI scrapes whatever is on the web; if the web input is wrong, the output is wrong, and **AI has no mind of its own to verify it**. AI is fine for a plain letter to a friend; it fails where nuance and technical expertise are required.
- **Emotion / context:** AI misses the emotional register and the context you alone hold (e.g., writing to a friend).
- **Outsourcing what makes us human:** the instructor's push-back — for trivial tasks you can outsource, but do it often and you outsource *thinking* itself.

**Instructor's rules for mindful AI use:**
1. **Never outsource the entire task.** Don't say "write an email for me." Instead **draft it yourself, or feed it your own points**, and let AI *polish*. You know the context better than the AI does.
2. **AI hallucination:** AI fabricates information that doesn't exist → destroys your credibility. Giving it your own points reduces fabrication.
3. **Watermarks:** tools (e.g. Claude was cited) now watermark output; detectable in published/publishable material. Being caught using AI extensively without acknowledgement signals you didn't even proofread → credibility damage.
4. **Critical thinking erosion.**
5. **"AI slop":** every email now looks the same — same structure, same sentences, same composition. Emails don't land, audiences aren't convinced, backlash follows. **Distinctive writing is how you differentiate yourself.**
6. **Always proofread and review** — judgement is the human differentiator and it decides whether the email lands.

### 2.4 Exercise 1 — Diagnose the bad email (do NOT rewrite)
A short 3-line managerial email containing phrases such as: *"several teams did not escalate concerns in time… some requirements were not met… please review what went wrong in your respective areas and ensure it does not happen again."*

**Class diagnosis:**
- Assertive/aggressive; puts the burden of finding the fault on the reader.
- **No responsibility taken by the sender**; no credibility.
- **Blame and escalation** language directed at a team.
- Could have been constructive ("this happened, here's how we fix it") but is purely accusatory.
- **Vague:** "several teams," "some requirements," "please review" — nothing specific.
- No statement of *what actually went wrong*; addressed to everyone rather than the responsible team.
- **No call to action, no owner, no deadline.** A reader cannot act on it.

**Takeaway:** the email fails on concreteness and accountability → segue into the Seven C's.

### 2.5 The Seven C's of Workplace Communication
Presented as a **drafting checklist**, *not* a full definition of good managerial writing.

| C | Meaning as taught |
|---|---|
| **Clear** | Understandable on the **first reading**. Attention spans are dropping; no one will re-read to decode you. |
| **Concrete** | Specifics: named actions, named owners, actual numbers, timelines. The bad email had none. |
| **Coherent** | Logical flow and structure: **context → what happened → call to action**. (The bad email was coherent but not concrete.) |
| **Courteous** | Professional, formal, respectful and **humane** — treat the recipient as a human being, not a "tag of employees who will do the work." Practise this **even when communicating downwards**. |
| **Concise** | No unnecessary detail; **short sentences**. Long sentences lose the reader by the end → costs clarity. |
| **Correct** | Grammatically and factually accurate and **appropriate to the recipient**. |
| **Complete** | The reader must have **enough information to act**; incomplete info causes costly back-and-forth. |

**Related concept — Machine Heuristic Bias:** the tendency to assume that whatever a machine/AI produced must be correct. AI-generated emails do have very few grammatical errors, but **accuracy and appropriateness are still your responsibility.** Be aware of this bias.

**Student question — "Should I write 'Hope you're doing well'?"**
- It belongs to **Courtesy**. Use it for **clients, external stakeholders, or people you haven't interacted with for a while**. For **routine internal communication it can be dropped.** (Fuller email structure comes in the next session.)

**Student point:** semantics can still be affected → hence the emphasis on **appropriateness** to the audience.

### 2.6 Worked before/after examples (vague → concrete)
| Vague original | Revised (concrete) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Some issues were noticed in the report." | **"I noticed three missing figures in the report."** | Named agent (*I*), exact count, specific defect. |
| "Please send it soon." | Replace with an actual date/time. | **"Soon"** is relative — 2 days to you, today to someone else. Most overused, least specific word. |
| "There was confusion around ownership." | Name who owns what. | Process language hides the human. |

**Pattern to notice:** the shift is from **vague process language → specific human agency + action + time**. This is what removes back-and-forth and misunderstanding.

### 2.7 Can an email satisfy all Seven C's and still fail?
**Yes.** Class-generated reasons:
- Clarity/concision/coherence only **preserve meaning** — **word choice still sets the tone** of the whole conversation (semantics).
- **Over-concision** can fail a client who needs the cause of a failure explained from the basics; big words instead of fundamentals leave them lost.
- The **emotional connect** may not come through.
- Instructor's answer: what is typically missing is **accountability / ownership**.

**Real-world illustration mentioned:** public CEO layoff memos (e.g. Microsoft's). They explain the conditions that led to mass layoffs in detail, but **nobody takes the blame** → an example of ineffective leadership on the page. Also flagged: when things go well, do you **"blow your own trumpet"?**

Forward reference: **persuasion — ethos, logos (and pathos)** and the judgement of how much emotion vs. logic to put into an email, to be covered later.

### 2.8 Exercise 2 — Two CEO layoff emails (the main case study)
**Set-up given by the instructor:**
- Two CEOs announcing a **major workforce reduction**, at roughly the **same time**, for the **same stated reason (AI)**.
- Company **not** presented as being in immediate financial trouble.
- Both announce the decision **directly to employees**.
- **Focus of analysis:** *not* whether layoffs are right or wrong, *not* which CEO is a better leader — but **how responsibility is reflected in the writing**.

**Email 1 — formal/corporate (read first):**
- Bulleted, formal, well-structured, systematic: what → why → how employees will be supported.
- Warm, "you are part of us" framing; extended support, help finding new jobs.
- Detailed on accelerating growth, operations, improvements.
- Criticisms raised in class: reads as **AI-drafted/polished and impersonal**; staggered notification (India affected today, US affected, others "will get an email eventually") creates **prolonged anxiety** — people keep checking their inbox wondering if they're next; the closing meeting section shows little genuine connection.

**Email 2 — Jack Dorsey, released Feb 2026 (informal):**
- Direct, straight to the point; addresses colleagues personally.
- **Informal mechanics** — ellipses ("…") instead of full stops, loose punctuation. Deliberately *not* polished by AI.
- **Heavy use of first-person pronouns: "I am responsible," "I chose the latter."** He states he had two options (let people go one-by-one vs. all at once) and explains why he chose as he did.
- Key line quoted in class: *"I know doing this might feel awkward — I'd rather feel awkward and human than efficient and cold."*
- Hosts a single session so people can say goodbye; he makes himself **accessible** rather than delegating to individual emails.
- Criticism raised: tone slightly inconsistent (grateful / sorry / and then future AI strategy) — an awkward transition.

**Instructor's verdict:** Both emails are "correct at their own levels"; Email 1 has genuine merits. **The differentiator is ownership** — Dorsey's email takes complete responsibility and is more human, *because the situation demanded it*.

**Lesson drawn:**
> When things go badly in the workplace — and they will — **look in the mirror**. Take ownership. **Do not hide behind company policy, macro conditions, or "AI made us do it."** Those reasons may be true, but don't make them the *primary* reason: people see through it and read it as an absence of remorse and empathy.

**Sub-discussions raised during this exercise:**
- **Empathy vs. ownership — must you choose?** No. *"You can have the best of both worlds"* — add a line demonstrating empathy and still take Dorsey-style responsibility. Good managerial writing on sensitive issues does **both**.
- **Audience & culture:** the two CEOs were writing to different cultural audiences, which changed the style. This introduces **high-context vs. low-context cultures** — India is a **high-context** culture where things are not communicated directly; leaders switch between high- and low-context registers. To be covered in detail later, and revisited when negative messages are taught.
- Referenced back to the **context ("C") element of the model taught in Session 1** (ASR garbled — "BOP/ABC model… the C part is context"): audience and context must always be factored in.

### 2.9 Exercise 3 — Tim Cook, Apple 50th anniversary email
Short, celebratory email (Cook stepped down as CEO on 1 September, per the lecture).

**Class observations:**
- **Credits everyone's contribution** — from Steve Jobs to every employee — never makes it about himself.
- Clear subject line; warm, positive tone from the opening.
- Specific and genuinely appreciative rather than generic.
- Narrates the **timeline**: where we were → where we are → where we want to go, and says the future is only possible **with this team**.

**Lesson drawn:**
> When things go well — an anniversary, a milestone, a success — **look out of the window**: give credit to the people, teams and contributors who made it possible.

### 2.10 Level 5 Leadership (the framework to memorise)
| Situation | Direction of gaze | What your writing must do |
|---|---|---|
| **Things go badly / go wrong** | **Look in the MIRROR** | Take accountability and ownership; use first-person responsibility; don't blame the team, the market, policy or AI. |
| **Things go well / success, milestone** | **Look OUT OF THE WINDOW** | Give credit to people, teams, contributors; don't blow your own trumpet. |

Together with the **Seven C's**, this is the frame to carry into all subsequent writing sessions.

### 3.2 Assignment 1 — Elevator Pitch Video (INDIVIDUAL)
**Link to class content:** In Session 1 the class watched a clip from ***Band Baaja Baaraat*** as an example of an **elevator pitch** — in roughly one minute the protagonists convinced a couple that they were worth investing in. This assignment replicates that challenge.

**Objective:** Record a compelling elevator pitch introducing yourself, answering *"Tell me something about yourself."*

**Scenario (the twist):** You are at an **airport** and unexpectedly cross paths with a **C-suite executive of your dream company** (e.g. Sundar Pichai of Google). You have **1 minute** to introduce yourself and leave a lasting impression in this rare, high-stakes encounter.

**Format requirements:**
- **Maximum 1 minute**, tolerance **± 5 seconds**. Beyond that it is no longer an elevator pitch.
- **Single uninterrupted take — no edits, no cuts.**
- Prepare a **script**; know the flow, but don't sound memorised — it must come across as **natural**.

**Required content (structure of the pitch):**
1. **Personal introduction** — who you are, what you do professionally, your background, key achievements.
2. **Name the C-suite executive** you are pitching to, **and their company** (e.g. Sundar Pichai, Google).
3. **State the specific position / area of interest** you are targeting (e.g. marketing at Google).
4. **Connection to the company** — why you are passionate about this company and how your current skills and experience align with its mission.
5. **Value proposition** — the unique skills and qualities you bring to the table.
6. **Call to action** — how they should follow up / how you'll follow up (email, LinkedIn connection, etc.), appropriate to a C-suite recipient.

**Technical requirements:**
- **Sound:** clear audio, free of background noise.
- **Environment:** quiet place; **simple, non-distracting background** — ideally a plain/white wall.
- **Lighting:** proper lighting on the face; clearly visible, **no shadows or glare**.
- **Camera position:** **medium / waist shot** — upper body above the waist visible, so that **body language, facial expressions and gestures** can be assessed.
- **Dress code (asked in class):** because the setting is an airport, **not fully formal** — **business casual / "keep it professional."**

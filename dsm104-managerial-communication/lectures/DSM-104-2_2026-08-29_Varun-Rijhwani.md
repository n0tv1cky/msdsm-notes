## 1. Overview

This session opens with a student-led recap of Session 1 (audience analysis and the PAIBOC model, illustrated through two film clips) and then moves into the core topic: **non-verbal communication**. The instructor establishes that communication begins *before* you speak — through dress, punctuality, posture and expression — and introduces the **7–38–55 rule** to show how little of oral communication is carried by words alone. The bulk of the class is built around a repeated, layered analysis of a boardroom scene from the film *Jab We Met* (Shahid Kapoor's character addressing a hostile board after returning to the company): first watched **on mute** (decode behaviours, not conclusions), then **with audio**, then a third time for fine detail. From this clip the instructor derives the three dimensions of non-verbal communication — **kinesics** (posture, gestures, eye contact, facial expressions), **proxemics** (use of space/zones), and **paralanguage** (pace, pitch, pause, volume, stress) — along with specific tools: the **zone of influence**, the **leveler** and **placator** gestures, the **3-second periodic and distributive eye contact rule**, and a word-stress exercise on the sentence *"I didn't say he stole my money."* The session closes with the **congruence principle** (when words and body conflict, audiences believe the body) and practical Q&A on speaking from a podium and on delivering non-verbal cues over online/Zoom sessions.

## 2. Topics & Concepts, in Order Taught

### A. Recap of Session 1 — Audience Analysis

- **Clip 1 (recap):** A village panchayat scene about an open-defecation/sanitation drive. The speaker did **not** account for the villagers' existing knowledge or mindset; the villagers were indifferent. → Audience analysis was absent, so the communication failed.
- **Clip 2 (recap):** A wedding-planning scene (Anushka Sharma & Ranbir/Ranveer's characters) pitching to a client family. The pair had **done their homework** on the client before the meeting — e.g. they knew the (bride)groom's father had himself been backed with money to start a factory at a very young age despite being a novice, and they used that story to argue the case for trusting a newcomer.
  - **Lesson:** The second was far more **empathetic and effective** because the speakers researched the audience and framed the pitch in the audience's own frame of reference.

- **The PAIBOC model** (transcript renders it "PABOK/paybok" — as dictated in class):
  - **P – Purpose**
  - **A – Audience**
  - **I – Information**
  - **B – Business/Benefits**
  - **O – Objections** *(explicitly corrected in class: it is **objections**, NOT "objectives" — because "objective" would duplicate **Purpose**)*
  - **C – Context**
  - The instructor stressed this is a **thematic model taught in Session 1 deliberately** — it will be applied in **every** subsequent session and in both **oral and written** communication (including writing emails).

- **Why do audience analysis? (Why no one-size-fits-all message?)**
  - To make the communication **about the listener, not the speaker** — i.e. audience-centric language.
  - **Demographic analysis:** age, class, gender.
  - **Psychographic analysis:** values, beliefs, fears — these vary sharply across audience types.
  - Also assess: **knowledge level, interests, expectations, language preferences.**
  - "Stepping into the shoes of the audience" is the core skill.

### B. Why Non-Verbal Communication Matters

- **Live demonstration:** the instructor greeted the class twice — once in a flat monotone with gaze directed away (at the walls), once with energy, a smile and direct eye contact into the camera.
  - Student observations: difference in **tone**, **energy level**, **facial expression**, **gaze/eye contact**. The first felt "forced" and disinterested; the second felt "welcoming."
  - **Takeaway:** Powerful words fail if body language, tone and energy do not support them.

- **"You start communicating before you even open your mouth."** Cues that speak first:
  - **Facial expression / smile** → makes the audience receptive; a grave posture creates distance. Key word: **approachable** — the audience should feel the speaker is "one among us," not an alien.
  - **Dressing for the occasion** — not fancy dressing, **appropriate** dressing. Example given: arriving casually dressed to a professional business meeting causes the audience's mind to "block" before you even begin.
  - **Punctuality/timing is non-verbal communication.** Arriving 5 minutes late, especially as a *pattern*, signals: disinterest, "this meeting isn't a priority," lack of discipline. (Genuine, uncontrollable reasons excepted.)
  - Posture, hand movements, general conduct.

### C. The 7–38–55 Rule (for **oral** communication)

> **Communication impact = 7% words + 38% how you say it + 55% body language**

| Share | Channel | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| **7%** | **Words** — the verbal content | What you actually say |
| **38%** | **Vocal / paralanguage** — *how* you say it | Pitch, volume, pace, pause, stress/emphasis |
| **55%** | **Body language / kinesics** | Posture (how you sit/stand), gestures (natural hand movement), facial expressions, eye contact |

- Rule of application: **body language, tone and pace must be in sync with the words.** They must not work against you. No matter how strong the content, poor delivery means the message does not land.

### D. Case Analysis — *Jab We Met* Boardroom Scene (main in-class exercise)

**Context:** Aditya (Shahid Kapoor) returns to his company after running away due to personal problems, and addresses a **hostile boardroom of stakeholders** to whom he is answerable. Share prices are at an all-time low and rumours about him are circulating.

**Exercise design (worth noting as a method):**
1. **Watch 1 — on mute.** Questions posed on screen: *What is happening in the room? Who has the power, and how do you know? What is he feeling?*
2. **Critical instruction: Name the BEHAVIOUR, not the CONCLUSION.**
   - ✗ Conclusion: "He was confident."
   - ✓ Behaviour: "He maintained eye contact with the audience," "he walked upright past the audience," "he used hand gestures."
   - Students who hadn't seen the film were said to be at an *advantage* — fresh, unbiased decoding.
3. **Watch 2 — with audio.** Check whether words align with the decoded body language.
4. **Watch 3 — micro-detail** (gestures, pauses).

**Behaviours students identified:**
- Walked in with a **smile** and relaxed attitude; confident **walk** past the audience.
- Made **eye contact with the room** *before* stepping up, then smiled, then began.
- Used **hand gestures** continuously; hands rose when pressing an important idea, and pitch rose with them.
- **Facial expressions shifted** as the content shifted (fierce/determined about the company → soft, sad, neutral when speaking about his late father).
- Used **pauses**, including a long pause after a shocking statement.
- The room was initially **puzzled/confused**, looking at each other; the audience "warmed up to him" only later; ended with **applause**.

**The key behaviour the instructor was hunting for:**
- **He declined the offer to speak from the podium/mic and took centre stage instead.**
  - Why it matters (student answers accepted): enables **two-way connection**; makes him "among them" rather than above them; a podium **hides most of the body**, blocking the 55% channel; a podium **symbolises hierarchy**; hiding behind it after having abandoned the company would have signalled **guilt**; centre stage increases audience attention span.
- **He unbuttoned his blazer while walking up** (it was buttoned when he took the stage) — a signal of openness/approachability.

**The rhetorical opening — link back to PAIBOC "O = Objections":**
- He **acknowledged his mistake** up front and **confirmed the rumours were true**, using a **light/humorous, non-serious tone** at the start.
- **Lesson:** Anticipate the audience's objections and questions and **build them into your own communication**. Because the rumours were already in the market, addressing them head-on at the very start (a) built **trust and credibility** — honesty, "leading from the front" — and (b) **got them out of the way** so the room could focus on the task at hand.
- Had he opened seriously and arrogantly with company figures, he would have appeared arrogant and self-serving.

### E. The Three Dimensions of Non-Verbal Communication

#### 1. KINESICS — body movement

**(a) Posture & physical presence**
- Shahid's posture: **upright and straight** → denotes confidence.
- Posture also includes **where you stand or sit** (e.g., podium vs centre stage).
- **Physical appearance** goes hand-in-hand with posture.

**(b) Gestures**
- Gestures = natural movement of the hands. **Do not restrict them**; do not clasp hands behind your back and speak — the words then carry only ~7% and lose their intended effect.
- **Zone of Influence:** the area **between your belly button and your chin** (the embedded video widened this to "belly button to eyes"). *All hand movement should occur inside this zone.* Imagine an invisible **box** in front of you — gestures stay inside the box; beyond it they look awkward.
- **Two things to control in gestures:** (i) that they stay in the **zone of influence**, (ii) that they stay within a reasonable **range/amplitude**.
- **Two named gestures (both demonstrated from the clip):**
  - **The Leveler** — palms facing **down**, hands crossing and opening **downward/outward**. Used to **decline**, say no, or **make a firm point**. (Shahid used this to decline the podium.)
  - **The Placator** — palms **open and upward**, arms open, hands in the air. Used to **welcome/greet** an audience ("Good morning, everyone").

**(c) Supporting video clip — the "influence zone" demo**
- Content: A trainer puts two people side by side. One stands with hands limply by his side ("Hi, my name is…" — doesn't know what to do with his hands); the other has his hands in the influence zone. The trainer asks the room, *"Who's in charge?"* — nobody can tell when both have dead hands; the instant one is moved into the influence zone, everyone identifies him as the one in charge. Switching the hands switches the perceived authority.
- **Lessons:** Hands in the influence zone signal **who is in charge / who knows the content / who is prepared and confident.** Hands are used "like PowerPoint" — you draw pictures and express with them. **Without gestures, audience comprehension drops significantly.**

**(d) Eye contact**
- Must be **periodic** and **distributive**.
- Benefits: projects power, builds **trust**, keeps the audience **engaged**. Without it (staring at blank spaces, at your notes/chit, at the floor), **engagement drops significantly** and you lose the audience.
- **The 3-Second Rule — worked procedure:**
  1. Mentally divide the room into **left, centre, right**.
  2. Start on the **left**: hold eye contact with one person for **3 seconds**; then another person on the left.
  3. **Gradually shift to the centre**: one person for 3 seconds, then another.
  4. **Gradually shift to the right**: repeat the same.
- **Purpose:** giving **each section of the audience equal importance**; not restricting yourself to one zone. This must be a **conscious, effortful** act.
- **Upper limit:** **do not exceed 3 seconds** on one person — longer reads as **staring** and **intimidates** the audience.

**(e) Facial expressions & congruence**
- **Congruence** = facial expressions in sync with the content being spoken.
- Worked example from the clip: transitioning from the **company's vision** (fierce, determined expression, higher pitch) to his **late father** (soft, sad, neutral expression; a **drop in pitch, volume and overall vocal variety**). Expressions and voice moved together with the meaning — that is congruence.

#### 2. PROXEMICS — the study of space between speaker and audience

| Zone | Distance | Who / when |
|---|---|---|
| **Intimate zone** | **0 – 1.5 ft** | Highly personal — close friends and immediate family only |
| **Personal zone** | (≈1.5 – 4 ft) | Acquaintances, extended family |
| **Social zone** | **4 ft – 12 ft** | Workplace setting, colleagues, others placed in this category |
| **Public zone** | (beyond 12 ft) | Public speaking / addressing an audience, as in the film clip |

- Zones **vary with the situation** and must be chosen against the **objective of the communication**.
- **Applied to the clip:** the setting was inherently a **public zone**, but Shahid's character deliberately **minimised** it by refusing the podium and moving to centre stage — because the information required him to appear **credible, approachable, vulnerable and not hiding anything.** How he used the available space *is* proxemics.

#### 3. PARALANGUAGE — vocal variety
> Definition: **everything about the voice except the words themselves.**

- **Pace (speed):** If you rush, the audience cannot absorb the message.
  - *Clip example:* He spoke **fast** while recapping the company's history/decline (already known to the audience — just a quick glimpse), then **slowed down, stopped and paused** for each new point so it landed properly. This was **deliberate**.
- **Pitch — variation vs. monotone:**
  - *Clip example:* **Playful/humorous tone** at the opening (to diffuse the situation and address rumours) → **serious tone** for the company's condition → **high, sustained pitch** for the future/challenge ("we're no less, we'll do it") → **lowered pitch** for his father, signalling seriousness and grief.
- **Pause & silence:**
  - *Clip example 1:* After "all the rumours are true" — a pause that left the audience confused and waiting for what came next.
  - *Clip example 2:* A **long pause / silence** around the mention of his late father, and a pause before framing the challenge ("we can run the company without him").
  - **Rule:** Use pauses after a **strong, impactful or emotional point** so the audience can **absorb** it. Practically: when making an important point, **slow the pace deliberately, make the point, then pause.**
- **Volume:** must match the **size of the room** you are occupying.
- **Word stress & emphasis:** see the exercise below.

### F. In-Class Exercise — Word Stress

**Sentence:** *"I didn't say he stole my money."* Stress one word at a time; the meaning changes completely.

| Stressed word | Resulting meaning |
|---|---|
| **I** didn't say he stole my money | Someone else said it — not me |
| I **didn't** say he stole my money | I never uttered those words at all (denial) |
| I didn't **say** he stole my money | I may have **hinted/implied** it, but I didn't say it explicitly |
| I didn't say **he** stole my money | Someone else stole it — not him |
| I didn't say he **stole** my money | He may have been given it or borrowed it — but he didn't steal it |
| I didn't say he stole **my** money | He stole somebody **else's** money, not mine |
| I didn't say he stole my **money** | He stole **something else**, not the money |

- **Lesson:** This is the **power of word stress** — a single sentence yields seven different meanings purely through vocal emphasis. Proof that the 38% vocal channel carries real semantic weight.

### G. The Congruence Principle (summary framework)

- **Body language (55%)** = posture + gestures + eye contact + facial expressions
- **Tone & pace (38%)** = pitch + pace + volume + stress
- **Words (7%)**
- **When words, voice and body are congruent**, the message lands. When they **conflict** (you say one thing, your body says the opposite):
  - **Receivers look for non-verbal cues to interpret the speaker's true attitude or emotion.**
  - **Your words will not be trusted — your body language will be.**
  - "The body **leaks** the message" even when you're trying to say nothing; it reveals true intentions.
- Most people are **unaware** of what their body is doing, which is how others "see through" them. **The goal of the course: convert subconscious body language into a conscious, practised effort.** ("It's a communication *skills* course — a skill requires practice.")

### H. Q&A — Practical Applications

**Q1: Many corporate and academic forums force you to use a podium, which hides your body. Doesn't that kill the 55%?**
- Sometimes taking centre stage isn't possible. Compensate:
  1. Use **vocal variety** fully.
  2. Your **upper body is still visible** — use **hand gestures** to show you have stakes in the presentation.
  3. Use **eye contact**.
- **"Hiding behind the podium is not always a bad thing — it depends on the situation."**
- **Key principle:** *There is no single fixed meaning for any one body-language act. It all depends on the context.*

**Q2: If words and body language give different messages, which do we trust?**
- **Body language** — because the body leaks the message and audiences instinctively trust it over words. When they conflict, "the body has betrayed you."

**Q3: All of this was about in-person communication. What about online/Zoom?**
- All non-verbal elements still apply; you must **go an extra mile**:
  - **Keep the camera ON** throughout.
  - **Look directly into the camera** (that is your eye contact).
  - **Camera position/framing** must let the audience see you clearly.
  - Let your **hands move naturally** within frame; use gestures.
  - **Upright posture** — do not slouch; "imagine I sit like this and deliver the lecture — what impression does that give?"
  - Maintain a **listening posture** (hands in a set position) and **nod** while others speak — subtle cues that signal interest and engagement.
  - Use **facial expressions** and **vocal variety** deliberately.
- The instructor pointed out he was consciously modelling all of this during the session itself.
